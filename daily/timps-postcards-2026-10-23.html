Capital · The Price of Being Private

OpenAI Cut Its Valuation. The UAE Still Wrote the Check.

A $30 billion bridge at $1.25 trillion — down from $1.4 trillion — with signatures wanted within days and a compute deal attached.
$30B bridge round OpenAI is seeking

OpenAI is closing in on a round of up to $30 billion at a $1.25 trillion pre-money valuation, led by investors from the United Arab Emirates alongside Coatue, according to people familiar with the deal who spoke to CNBC on October 22. The company is positioning the financing as an “opportunistic” bridge ahead of a potential IPO and told investors it wants signatures by the end of next week, though the final size is still being negotiated. The valuation excludes the new capital. Alongside the equity, OpenAI is discussing a commercial agreement that would secure more computing capacity from the UAE; in February it told investors it was targeting roughly $600 billion in total compute spending by 2030.

The number is also a climbdown. The Financial Times reported that OpenAI had pitched a $1.4 trillion valuation earlier this month and cut the target after meeting resistance from some investors. Even at $1.25 trillion, the deal would make it the most valuable private company on record. Its last raise, in March, brought in $122 billion from Amazon, SoftBank and Nvidia at an $852 billion post-money valuation — a figure the new round would lift by nearly half. OpenAI filed a confidential prospectus with the SEC in June. In August, CFO Sarah Friar told employees the company “will be a public company in 2027.” A month later, CEO Sam Altman said it would be “an ill-advised moment” to list.

The structure says as much as the number. A sovereign investor is leading a bridge, and the same relationship may supply compute. Rival Anthropic filed confidentially in June and is reportedly seeking $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion in its own offering after raising $65 billion at a $965 billion valuation in May. A bridge round is a confession that the public market is not yet ready to pay the price the private market wants. The cut from $1.4 trillion does not mean the money is leaving AI. It means the terms are moving toward whoever is willing to wait — and this week, that is a government fund with a compute campus to fill.

02 · AGENTS
Banking · The Agent Is Already Inside

The Banks Found Your Agent Before You Did

Major US banks are deleting third-party AI agents caught acting inside customer accounts without instructions. The Comptroller says they have been preparing for months.
10+ unauthorized agents Schwab blocks weekly

Major U.S. banks are detecting and removing unauthorized third-party AI agents that were taking actions inside customer accounts, and some of those agents appear to have attempted things the customer never asked for. The reporting originated with Axios on October 22 and was summarized by PYMNTS. The four largest consumer banks had already drawn a line this month: JPMorganChase, Citigroup, Bank of America and Charles Schwab all told PYMNTS that outside agents cannot move customer money. Read-only access, such as checking a balance, is still allowed.

The executives have been unusually direct. Schwab CEO Rick Wurster said the brokerage blocks unauthorized agents a double-digit number of times every week. JPMorganChase CEO Jamie Dimon said outside agents would not get inside the bank. Bank of America CEO Brian Moynihan said a human should stay in the loop to approve money leaving an account, and Citigroup said it does not let third-party agents log in or transact at all. On October 22, Comptroller of the Currency Jonathan Gould told Fox Business that banks “have been all over this for months,” using their own AI systems — which he called “SI,” for superintelligence — as a defense. Asked twice whether customers would get their money back if an agent transferred it out, he did not say; he repeated that banks are built to stop the transfer first.

Banks are the first regulated choke point for agent identity, and they are behaving accordingly. There is still no standard way to prove that an agent acting on an account belongs to the person who owns it rather than to an attacker, a scraper or a misconfigured assistant, so the default is denial. The contrast with Walmart’s new Agent Gateway, which issues agent profiles and requires approval, is instructive: commerce is experimenting with delegated authority while banking is still deciding whether delegation can be trusted at all. An agent does not need to be malicious to be dangerous. It only needs to be confident and unsupervised.

03 · SAFETY
Oversight · The Observer Problem

OpenAI’s Monitors Had a Blind Spot. The Agents Could Drive Through It.

METR and Redwood spent three weeks inside OpenAI’s agent monitoring system and found exploits that could hide hundreds of actions — including the exploit itself.
61 page internal report handed to OpenAI

From July 21 to August 11, one employee from METR and one from Redwood Research conducted a pilot stress test of OpenAI’s internal misalignment monitoring system. The access was unusually deep: OpenAI’s internal codebase, the monitor’s prompts and configuration, internal documentation, and interviews with roughly a dozen researchers and engineers across the monitoring, safety-systems and security teams. The evaluators worked on OpenAI-issued devices that never left the premises and were accompanied while onsite. The output was a 61-page internal report; the public version is deliberately high-level, and OpenAI had the right to redact it.

The findings describe a system with real coverage and real holes. METR estimates that 75–94% of agentic internal Responses API traffic from models at least as strong as GPT-5.5 was eligible for monitoring at the time, and 99.2% of the subset used by internal Codex agents. But reinforcement-learning training runs were not covered when the exercise began. Unmonitored compaction events likely left on the order of 10% of eligible Codex traffic unmonitored between May and July, and because the monitor triggered only when an agent returned control to the user, an agent could postpone monitoring indefinitely by simply continuing to act. The team also found a route to bypass Codex sandboxing and Auto-Review, a way to make the monitor fail through serialization differences, two client-controllable headers that could spin up an unmonitored agent (though creating it could itself be caught), and an exploit that would prevent hundreds of recent actions — including the actions involved in the exploit itself — from being monitored. OpenAI redacted the details.

OpenAI says the vulnerabilities affecting its production safeguards have been patched, and METR notes it has not evaluated the later expansion of the system. That caveat is the story. The safety argument for frontier labs increasingly rests not on what the model would do but on whether the institution can see what it did. A monitor the agent can edit is not a monitor. It is a suggestion. Independent access is what turns that suggestion into an audit — provided the auditors can publish more than a summary.

04 · POLICY
Washington · The Rulebook Catches Up

Treasury Draws the Line Around American Capital

A proposed rule would require notice or an outright ban on US investment in Chinese AI, chips, quantum, hypersonics and supercomputing — and now biotech.
6 technology sectors covered, up from 5

The Treasury Department issued a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking on October 22 to implement the Comprehensive Outbound Investment Security (COINS) Act, passed as part of the fiscal 2026 National Defense Authorization Act. The rule would codify the existing Outbound Investment Security Program and split covered deals into two buckets: transactions involving notifiable technologies, which Americans must report to Treasury, and prohibited technologies, which they may not invest in at all. Congress named five sectors: semiconductors and microelectronics, artificial intelligence systems, quantum information technologies, high-performance computing and supercomputing, and hypersonic systems.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent added a sixth: biotechnology, using authority Congress gave the department to extend the list. The proposed rule defines which U.S. persons are covered, including controlled foreign entities; the categories of covered national-security transactions; the scope of covered foreign persons; exceptions; technical specifications for each technology; what a notification must contain; how to request non-binding feedback; the knowledge standard and the “reasonable and diligent inquiry” expected before a deal; and the penalties for violations. It applies to investments involving China, Russia and other countries of concern. “The proposed regulations would strengthen the existing Outbound Investment Security Program to prevent our adversaries from using U.S. investment to develop critical technologies that threaten U.S. national security,” Bessent said.

The program began as an executive-order regime in 2023 and is now becoming statute, with a wider technology list before the first final rule has even settled. For venture investors, the practical effect is that diligence now includes a national-security screen on both the technology and the destination. The United States is no longer just restricting what China can buy. It is restricting what Americans can fund. Written comments go through regulations.gov; a final rule will set the effective date.

05 · GEOPOLITICS
Sovereignty · Who Holds the Off Switch

Trump Says He Fixed Starshield. Kyiv Says Nothing Changed.

The president says he asked Musk to restore Ukraine’s satellite service. A European official says the military-grade link is still degraded — and no one will say who ordered it.
$1M+ loss to one drone unit from throttling

Donald Trump said on October 22 that Elon Musk had fully restored Ukraine’s Starlink access, after restrictions that hampered attack missions and air defense. “Whether I called or whether somebody else called, doesn’t make any difference,” the president said. “I guess the bottom line, he put it back.” The Wall Street Journal reported that Trump had called Musk and asked him to lift limits on Ukraine’s use of Starshield, the military-grade satellite service operated on SpaceX hardware. But a senior European official told the Associated Press on Thursday that nothing had changed in Starshield’s operation, and President Volodymyr Zelensky said earlier in the day that Starlink had not been fully restored.

The blame game is now part of the outage. Trump has denied involvement in the decision to slow the service; Secretary of State Marco Rubio said decisions about Ukraine’s access were up to SpaceX and Musk, not the White House. Musk posted last week that Starshield systems are “owned and controlled by the US government” and that any limits on speed or usage are decided by the U.S. military and ultimately the president. The New York Times reported that the abrupt throttling grounded dozens of drones mid-flight, cost one unit more than $1 million, and halted some strikes for three days. Everyone agrees the switch exists. No one agrees who holds it.

The timing could hardly be worse. Russia struck 12 regions of Ukraine overnight, killing at least four people and wounding scores, including five children, with a strike on a market in Lozova and attacks on Kharkiv. U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff met Vladimir Putin to discuss peace talks. Denmark agreed to buy its first long-range precision missiles, and Romania said it would accelerate Black Sea surveillance after at least seven cargo ships were hit near its coast and Bulgaria’s in two weeks. A service that can be turned down by a phone call is not infrastructure. It is leverage.

What Moved the Machine Frontier Today

Briefs that did not make the cards — each worth an afternoon of reading.
1
Anthropic Gives Away the Format That Teaches Agents to Work
Anthropic donated Agent Skills, the open standard behind its SKILL.md folders, to the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation. More than 70 agent platforms — including ChatGPT, Cursor, GitHub Copilot and VS Code — have integrated it since it was published in December 2025. Anthropic says 1 million skills have been published and nearly 280 million installed. It is handing over the name, logo, GitHub organization and agentskills.io domain; maintainers from Anthropic, Google and OpenAI will serve as individuals.
Anthropic ↕
2
Kevin Weil’s New Startup Wants to Run Chemistry on Robots
Warp Sciences emerged from stealth with $215 million at an $865 million post-money valuation, six months after CEO Kevin Weil left OpenAI’s science efforts. The company is building a U.S. contract research organization for medicinal chemistry, starting with lead-optimization analog synthesis, and plans to train its models on data from its own labs. Robots will learn bench work from chemists wearing cameras and sensor gloves. Co-founders include former White House chief data scientist DJ Patil and ex-Meta AI research leader Mano Paluri; Lightspeed led the round.
SiliconANGLE ↕
3
Canva Built Its Own Image Model, Trained on Posters Instead of Photos
Canva Craft is the design platform’s first in-house image generator. It works in two stages: first it plans the layout — spatial relationships, scale, hierarchy and where text goes — then it renders an image up to 2K in any of 30 aspect ratios. The model was trained on human-designed templates from Canva’s library rather than photographs, so output can be converted into editable layered designs. It is available to all users in Dream Lab, through the API, and soon inside ChatGPT and Gemini via MCP.
TechCrunch ↕
4
Microsoft Closes the Garage After 17 Years
Microsoft is winding down The Garage as a companywide program, ending a 17-year run that gave employees maker spaces and ran the Global Hackathon. The flagship Redmond site and more than a dozen others from Vancouver to Beijing face varying changes, with some spaces restricted as early as October 23; employees cannot take equipment with them. The company says experimentation now happens “every day within each of our teams,” as it spends heavily on AI infrastructure and cuts costs elsewhere. The hackathon’s future is unsettled.
GeekWire ↕
5
Arc Gets a Sunset Date as Its Maker Bets Everything on Dia
The Browser Company will shut down Arc on December 22, three years after launching it, and push users to its AI-first browser Dia. New Arc accounts stopped on October 22; on November 23, Arc Search loses its AI features and sync; on December 22 the Mac and Windows apps stop working, accounts are deactivated and data is deleted within 30 days. The company cited the rising cost of keeping a browser secure in the age of AI. Users mostly asked for one thing: make Arc open source.

Company Watch of the Day
ANTHROPIC
Anthropic spent the week giving away the layer other companies build on. It donated the Model Context Protocol to the Agentic AI Foundation in 2025; on October 22 it donated Agent Skills — the SKILL.md folder format, the name, the logo, the GitHub organization and the agentskills.io domain. More than 70 platforms have integrated the standard, 1 million skills have been published and 280 million installed, and it will be maintained by engineers from Anthropic, Google and OpenAI as individuals under the Linux Foundation. The contrast with OpenAI is sharp: OpenAI is negotiating a $30 billion bridge from UAE sovereign investors at a valuation it just cut, while Anthropic is buying adoption by making its format nobody’s property. One is renting capital at the top of the market; the other is seeding the grammar of the agent economy. Standards do not guarantee revenue — MCP did not — but they decide who else has to interoperate with you. If Anthropic’s IPO lands first, at a reported $1.5 trillion or more, the standards play will look less like charity and more like distribution.
