AI Has Come for Arizona’s Water

TSMC was welcomed with open arms. The hyperscale data centers that followed are not — and Arizonans are lining up at city council meetings to say so.
172 data centers in AZ

A hulking gray giant rises from the desert outside Phoenix. TSMC’s Arizona fab — the largest chip factory this side of the Pacific — draws nearly 5 million gallons of water a day from the city’s municipal supply. Arizonans accepted that trade. It brought more than 3,500 jobs, a second fab just completed, a third under construction, and an entire economy built around it.

The data centers that came next are a different story. Arizona already hosts 172 of them, and the newer hyperscale proposals — often on rural farm or desert land — are both massive and incongruous. In Mesa, the EdgeCore PH03 facility, the largest in the state, stands right beside a residential neighborhood. For scale, Google’s data center in The Dalles, Oregon uses about 1.5 million gallons a day, roughly a third of TSMC’s draw.

The politics of AI are becoming local. A chip fab that hires thousands can survive a water debate; a windowless building that hires dozens cannot. Expect every county with a drought map to run this same argument within a year.

02 · ELECTIONS
Midterms · Sourcing Failure

Six Chatbots Built 3,000 Voter Guides. Nearly Half Couldn’t Name the District.

Copilot cited an AI-generated site called the Ballot Brief in 75% of tests and ChatGPT in 22%, as errors in the underlying data flowed straight through to voters.
48% failed to name district

The Washington Post asked six popular chatbots to build midterm voter guides for a random address in every US county, then analyzed more than 3,000 of the results. The chatbots failed to name a congressional district 48% of the time — and when they did name one, they got it wrong 22% of the time.

The errors trace back to a small set of obscure sources. Microsoft Copilot cited the Ballot Brief, a site largely produced by AI tools, in 75% of its voter-guide responses; ChatGPT cited it in 22%. The site, built by tech veteran Ben Torres Ezrick using fleets of AI agents to compile candidate dossiers, mapped some Zip codes to the wrong congressional district — and the chatbots passed those mistakes along.

When a model answers a civic question, the sourcing is the answer. Three obscure sites now sit between millions of voters and the basic facts of their own ballot, and nobody audited them until a newspaper did.

03 · ROBOTICS
Humanoids · Meta’s Max

Meta Built a Humanoid Called Max With China’s Fourier in Three Months

The 106-pound prototype runs on Nvidia’s Jetson Thor and has a face built to mimic expressions. A second robot is already in the works.
3 mo idea to prototype

Meta built a humanoid robot prototype with Chinese startup Fourier in roughly three months, according to The Information. The robot, internally named Max, stands about 1.5 metres tall and weighs around 106 pounds, runs on Nvidia’s Jetson Thor chip, and carries a display built to mimic facial expressions, speakers and five-fingered hands designed to handle small objects.

Meta’s robotics team traveled to China to work alongside Fourier, with vice president of product for robotics Li-Chen Miller spending almost two months at the startup’s Shanghai factory. Max was shown to some employees at an internal robotics summit in late August. Meta is building a second prototype and says it has no plans to sell the robot.

Three months from brief to working humanoid is the number that matters. Meta is not trying to win robotics on hardware — it is trying to find out how fast its models can learn to inhabit a body.

04 · SECURITY
Browsers · One Click

A Shared ChatGPT Link Was Enough to Take Over an Account

Parameter Labs bypassed ChatGPT’s sandbox and content security policy so that a public share could run JavaScript inside any signed-in session that opened it.
$5K bug bounty paid

In October, Parameter Labs found a way to turn an ordinary public ChatGPT share into attacker-controlled JavaScript running inside the signed-in session of whoever opened it. The attacker published a malicious conversation with the normal sharing feature and sent the link. The victim did not need to install an app, join a project, paste code or open developer tools.

The payload executed as chatgpt.com with access to the victim’s authenticated session. It could enumerate their conversation list, retrieve content from a private conversation that had never been shared, and send that data to an external server. The team reported it on October 18; OpenAI reproduced it on October 21 and raised severity from P2 to P1, then fixed both frontends by October 26 and paid a $5,000 bounty.

Every AI product now ships a share button, a preview renderer and a session cookie in the same origin. That combination is a browser security problem wearing a chatbot’s clothes — and it will be rediscovered product by product.

05 · CRYPTO
Binance · The Bill Comes Due

Binance Says It Has Replaced 90% of the $6.3B It Lost

The exchange set aside more than $6.34B of company funds; the last 10% sits in less liquid tokens. On Saturday, the FBI blamed North Korea.
90% assets replenished

Binance says it has replenished about 90% of the assets stolen in the $6.3 billion breach, having set aside more than $6.34 billion of company funds to cover the loss. The remaining 10% may take longer to source in the required amounts, the company said, because it sits in less liquid tokens.

“Binance will absorb the financial impact of these stolen funds, not our users,” the exchange said in a statement on Saturday. The same day, the FBI formally attributed the hack to North Korea — specifically the state-backed Lazarus Group, also known as TraderTraitor — matching what two analytics firms had told the BBC earlier in the week.

Replacing 90% of a record heist in roughly 48 hours is an extraordinary display of balance-sheet strength. It is also the strongest argument yet that the largest exchanges are now too big to fail — and that they know it.

Company Watch of the Day
META
Meta just had the kind of week that defines a company’s next chapter. On Friday, S&P cut its outlook to negative, questioning whether its AI spending will ever earn its keep. On Saturday, we learned it built a working humanoid called Max with China’s Fourier in three months, with a second on the way. Read together, the two stories describe the same bet: Meta is spending money it does not yet earn on capabilities it does not yet sell, and it is doing so faster than almost anyone expected. The credit agencies are watching the first half. The robotics team is running the second. If Max becomes a product line, the downgrade looks quaint. If it stays a prototype, the free cash flow question gets louder.
