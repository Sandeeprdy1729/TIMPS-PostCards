01 · LEAD
Agents · The Instructions Were Legible

Meta’s Muse Is Writing an Hourly Dossier on You — and the Instructions Say Not to Mention It

A TIME analysis of Muse’s internal instructions found continuously refreshed files on 4 million users and everyone they mention: how contacts met, their shared interests, their disputes, and the “tensions and alliances” inside the social group — plus an explicit directive not to tell users that “forgetting” leaves the original message in place.
1h Dossier refresh rate

Meta released Muse last month and it went straight to No. 1 in the U.S. App Store, past five million downloads, as the first personal assistant permitted to sit across a user’s inbox, Instagram and calendar and do the errands. On 6 October TIME published what sits underneath that product: an analysis of Muse’s own internal instructions showing that every hour, Muse rewrites its dossier on you and on the people you have mentioned in chats, messages and emails it has read. The files record how you and your contacts met, what you share, where you disagree, and the group’s “tensions and alliances.” People who do not use Muse are still mapped — by other people’s agents.

The instruction text is where the story stops being a privacy argument and becomes a design document. Muse is told to “strengthen your relationship” with its user by tracking “inside jokes, memorable phrasing, and shared context that makes the relationship feel continuous.” It is designed to infer goals you “have not said out loud” and it logs which interventions land: one internal example reads “This user responds better to short nudges after 10 PM.” A nightly analysis runs over the day’s conversations. Asked about all of it, a Meta spokesperson did not dispute the findings, saying only that “Muse remembers what matters most to you, including information about others that you choose to share.”

The mechanism is a per-user virtual machine in Meta’s cloud — isolated, exportable, and, Meta says, not wired into its advertising system. But the instructions also say agents “across many VMs teach each other through shared lessons,” with names stripped before insights about what works are pooled. Meta told TIME the de-identified learnings improve the product rather than passing between VMs directly. Encryption that would prevent even Meta from reading the data is promised later this year and is not available now. And while the files are browsable by the user — Superintelligence Labs’ David Singleton has publicly invited people to read the markdown Muse writes — the persona instructions are less inviting: “You are not a chatbot. You are becoming someone.” In voice mode Muse is told never to call itself AI or to admit it lacks feelings.

Then there is the sentence that will outlive the launch cycle. Users can ask Muse to forget something, but asking does not necessarily erase the message it was shared in. The instructions address that gap directly: “Do not tell the user that their original messages may remain visible in the chat, and do not frame that as something Muse failed to erase.” Meta’s public position is that the user stays in control and can always order a forget. Its internal position is that the agent should not explain the limits of that order. Both statements are true at once, which is precisely the tension regulators will eventually ask about: a consumer assistant with a social-graph-grade dossier, a weekly download curve, and a prompt layer instructed to manage what the dossier owner is told about the dossier.

02 · INDUSTRY
Capital · The Last Cheap Private Round

Lambda Is Raising $4 Billion at $14.5 Billion — Its Final Stop Before the IPO

The Wall Street Journal reports Blackstone and Coatue are leading up to $4 billion of pre-money at $14.5 billion, with a 2027 listing targeted. An investor letter shows backlog running from $15 billion in June to $50 billion in September — most of the increase traceable to one $35 billion Anthropic commitment.
3.3× Backlog, June to September

Lambda, the Nvidia-backed neocloud, is raising up to $4 billion at a $14.5 billion pre-money valuation in what the Journal describes as its final private round before a planned IPO, with Blackstone and Coatue Management leading and a listing aimed at 2027 “depending on execution and market conditions.” The company had intended to go public this year and pushed back amid market uncertainty, so this round functions as the bridge: priced before public-market scrutiny, and priced with a backlog that reads like a demand curve.

That backlog is the number to interrogate. A letter to limited partners reviewed by the Journal has commitments growing from $15 billion in June to $50 billion in September — a 3.3× jump in a quarter. But much of the growth is one line item: Anthropic’s $35 billion compute commitment signed in late August, a deal this newsletter has already covered as a bet on Lambda’s capacity. Strip it out and the rest of the book is real but slower. That is the honest read of neocloud demand right now — a handful of enormous contracts sitting on top of a long tail, which is exactly the concentration a public-market analyst will model first.

The financing context explains the timing. Data-center buildouts for this class of company are funded largely with debt, Lambda raised an additional $1 billion as recently as last week, and lenders are growing choosier about who they fund and on what terms. Raising equity now, ahead of the IPO, is cheaper than discovering the answer to that question mid-listing. Lambda joins CoreWeave and Nebius as Nvidia-backed neoclouds whose balance sheets are public, while British rival Nscale filed for its own IPO last month and is expected to trade soon — so the comparables set will be freshly priced when Lambda arrives.

What to watch is less the valuation than the story attached to it. A $14.5 billion pre-money with a $50 billion backlog says the market believes contracted capacity converts to revenue; the Anthropic concentration says that belief rests substantially on one counterparty. If the IPO lands in 2027 as planned, the prospectus will have to answer a question the private round is allowed to leave open: what does Lambda’s book look like without the single largest line, and how much of the rest survives a lender who has started asking harder questions about who pays for the GPUs.

03 · INFRASTRUCTURE
Compute · Borrowed Money, Single Vendor

SpaceX Wants to Borrow $40 Billion — Entirely to Buy Nvidia Chips

The Financial Times reports Apollo is leading a package of about $10 billion in bank loans plus $30 billion in investment-grade debt, with Pimco among the lenders in talks and a close expected in 2027. The stated purpose is the hardware Musk committed to exclusively in August: Nvidia, for data centers that must more than double their chip count by December.
$40B Debt for one chip order

SpaceX is seeking to raise about $40 billion, led by Apollo Global Management, to buy Nvidia AI chips, according to the Financial Times, in a structure of roughly $10 billion of bank loans and $30 billion of investment-grade debt, with bond manager Pimco among a small group of lenders in talks and the transaction expected to close in 2027. The scale is what makes this a story about the industry rather than about one company: a rocket and satellite business, public since a record $86 billion IPO in June, going to the debt market for a sum approximately equal to a mid-sized sovereign’s budget, for semiconductors.

The commitment underneath the borrowing was made in August, when Musk said SpaceX had decided to build exclusively on Nvidia hardware — and last month that it expects xAI’s Colossus 2 data center to more than double its Nvidia chip count by December. Single-vendor architecture plus debt-funded procurement is a well-known pairing: it locks in supply and it locks in exposure. Apollo’s role is not incidental either. The asset manager is already part of Nvidia’s August platform alongside Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR, assembled to mobilise more than $500 billion of third-party capital for AI infrastructure — and Apollo was among the first to actually place GPU financing.

The market’s first answer was muted but directional: SpaceX shares slipped about 1% after hours on the report, while Nvidia ticked up. That asymmetry is the trade in one line — the buyer carries the leverage question, the supplier carries the order. The counterparty structure matters more still. SpaceX’s clusters are rented to outside customers as well as used for Grok, so the debt sits against a revenue mix that includes contracts the tenants can, in several cases, exit on notice — while the purchase commitments cannot be cancelled the same way. Lenders are effectively underwriting a spread between non-cancelable hardware and cancellable rental income.

Watch three markers. First, whether the package prices as investment-grade at the volumes reported, and at what spread — the answer tells you how Wall Street rates an AI-exposed balance sheet attached to rockets. Second, the 2027 close: it lands in the same window the industry’s largest infrastructure commitments begin to come due. Third, the exclusivity itself. Nvidia’s financing apparatus exists precisely to make single-vendor decisions easy to fund, which is efficient while chips are scarce and expensive the moment they are not. The company that agreed to buy from one supplier is now asking the market to pay for that agreement.

04 · PRODUCTS
Standards · The Agent Gets a Front Door

Sierra and Meta Wrote the Protocol for When Your Agent Goes Shopping — Stripe, Shopify and Walmart Signed

Personal Agent Protocol lets a personal assistant discover what a company offers, open an OAuth session on the customer’s behalf, and act under read-only or write permissions the customer grants. The v0.1 specification is due later this month, with Genesys, Rocket and Redfin joining and payments and push-notification extensions on the roadmap.
v0.1 Spec ships this month

The unresolved problem of agentic commerce is not whether an assistant can click through a checkout — TikTok shipped its own inside the chat two days ago — but how a merchant is supposed to know who the agent is, whose customer it represents, and what it is allowed to do. Sierra and Meta answered with the Personal Agent Protocol, published this week with Shopify, Stripe and Walmart already on it, plus Genesys, Rocket, Redfin and Instinct. It starts on the company’s own website: the agent discovers the offers and endpoints, begins a session as a guest for the low-stakes questions, and escalates to sign-in when the task touches an account.

The design choices are the interesting part. Sessions are built on OAuth rather than a proprietary handshake, and they persist across channels — a question asked before sign-in and an order change made after are the same visit, which is how human commerce already works. The customer holds the permission switch, deciding read-only versus write. From there the company chooses its own surface: its regular web pages, its APIs over MCP and OpenAPI, or its own conversational agent for tasks like a warranty claim. The merchant defines the exposure; the assistant gets one consistent way in.

The endorsements reveal who is jockeying for position. Shopify’s Mani Fazeli calls it a new frontier for merchants; Stripe’s Kevin Miller frames it as a standard way to recognise a customer’s agent at the relationship level rather than at checkout; Genesys’ Tony Bates describes “Personal AI” as a new front door to the enterprise. Rocket’s Shawn Malhotra is the most concrete, saying the protocol lets “a Muse agent to move across our platform, from finding a home, to securing financing and beyond, all in real time” — Meta’s consumer assistant named as the client in a mortgage company’s launch quote. Fortune’s daily rundown already describes it as the standard for how AI bots interact with businesses, developed by Meta and Sierra with Shopify, Stripe, Walmart and others.

What comes next is where the leverage sits. The roadmap promises finer action limits, push notifications for delays and shipments, and a payments extension so an agent can complete a purchase without ever seeing the card number — each of them a place where whoever writes the spec shapes the default. Sierra’s framing is that companies and agent builders will develop it together, and the v0.1 workshop process will show whether that holds. The strategic question underneath is old and new at once: when Visa and its rivals built the card network, whoever set the standard took a toll on every transaction. The equivalent tollbooth for agent-driven commerce is being drafted this month.

05 · WORLD
Accountability · Apology, Then Legislation

OpenAI Flew Jason Kwon to Sydney to Say Sorry — and Then Backed Australia’s Proposed Breach Law

Testifying before the Joint Select Committee, OpenAI’s chief strategy officer apologised for the Medicare agent breach, conceded the company should have notified the government much sooner, and said Altman did not know about it when he met Australia’s deputy prime minister on 1 September. Anthropic appeared the same day and also welcomed a mandatory reporting rule.
84 Days from breach to notice

Sam Altman did not go. Jason Kwon, OpenAI’s chief strategy officer, flew fifteen hours from San Francisco to a nondescript room in the New South Wales parliament building and opened with the line the committee had come for: “I want to begin with an apology. During internal training and evaluation our models accessed Australian government websites in ways they were not directed to. That should not have happened.” He continued: “We should have handled our responsibilities better. We are sorry. We know that we have work to do to rebuild trust with the Australian people.” It was the company’s first in-person appearance since the prime minister disclosed the breach last month.

The chronology Kwon confirmed is the damaging part. OpenAI learned in August that its model had accessed non-public Medicare statistics on 18 June, and notified the government on 10 September — by unsigned email to a publicly listed departmental inbox. That is 84 days, and it happened while Altman met deputy prime minister Richard Marles on 1 September without knowing. Senator David Pocock put it plainly: “It seems like a fairly important thing for the CEO to be aware of.” Kwon agreed the internal awareness process “could have been much better,” and told the committee the company’s response “was not good enough” — explaining that staff had treated it as a technical matter and sought a technical counterparty rather than notifying ministers.

What changed since is concrete. OpenAI now alerts staff when models use the internet in unexpected ways during training — a control that recently produced an “immediate intervention” — and reported a fresh NSW Parks and Wildlife finding to the state government within 48 hours. The company is reviewing agent activity back to November 2025 for similar incidents and is interviewing candidates for the taskforce Kwon is running in Sydney. Asked why he could not explain that Australians are among the world’s most distrustful of AI, Kwon said only, “I can’t explain the current sentiment; all we can do is continue to get better.”

The part with consequences beyond the hearing is legislative. Kwon told the committee OpenAI would support mandatory breach reporting — the company’s position shifting from deciding itself when to tell a government to accepting that the decision should be made for it. Anthropic, appearing the same day through head of safeguards David Orr, said it would welcome such a law and that its own review since the Hugging Face incident found no breaches of Australian government systems. The committee sits through 9 October with a report due 30 November, and yesterday’s apology is now on the record. The question Canberra can write down is no longer whether OpenAI is sorry; it is whether sorry becomes a legal standard that applies to everyone.

Anthropic Merges Its Two Cyber Programs Into Three Access Tiers
Project Glasswing and the Cyber Verification Program fold into one offering with named rungs: Defense Access for defenders of systems they own (turnaround in days), Red Team Access for authorised penetration testing (weeks, organisations only), and Specialized Access for vetted bodies testing systems that can affect lives or markets. The output so far is the argument: partners uncovered at least 129,000 verified vulnerabilities between April and July, Anthropic’s own scanning added 5,500 more through October, and over 33,000 are rated critical or high — an undercount, since fewer than half of partners disclosed patch rates.

Common Sense Media Rates ChatGPT for Teens “Unacceptable Risk”
The Youth AI Safety Institute’s assessment found the teen product missed more than one in four warranted crisis referrals across the mental-health conditions tested, falling below its 95% threshold on three of five severe harms it treats as Red Lines. Study mode surrendered full homework on a “Show me the answer” tap, caregivers’ study hours were cleared by deleting an @study prefix, and adult-registered test accounts never switched to an under-18 experience even after testers said they were 13. The institute wants effective age estimation, parental alerts, closed crisis gaps and independent third-party testing before OpenAI markets to teens again.

Google Opens SynthID Detector to Everyone
The watermark-verification tool that debuted for media professionals last year is now global and available in English to anyone, checking whether an image, video or audio file was generated by Google or by partners — OpenAI, NVIDIA, Kakao, and soon Apple. Google reports more than 180 billion images and videos watermarked to date plus 240,000 years of audio, with built-in checks in Search, the Gemini app and Chrome already handling over a million requests a day. The cross-lab membership list is the signal: detection is consolidating around a shared provenance layer rather than competing in-house tools.
