Health & Consumer AI · The Waiting Room

Apple Opens a 200,000-Person Study to See How People Use AI for Health

The Apple Health AI Usage Study, run with OpenEvidence, will recruit up to 200,000 eligible adults in the US to ask everyday health questions inside a research app. Apple says the goal is to measure trust, comprehension and real-world behaviour, not to train models on the data.
200K eligible US participants age 21 and older on an Apple Intelligence-enabled iPhone

Apple launched the Apple Health AI Usage Study in the United States on Sunday, a virtual research project and companion app designed to watch how people actually use conversational AI for health questions. The company says it will enrol up to 200,000 eligible participants age 21 and older who have an Apple Intelligence-enabled iPhone (iPhone 15 Pro or later) running iOS 26 or later. The study is being conducted in collaboration with OpenEvidence, a clinical AI platform widely used by healthcare professionals.

Inside the app, participants can ask everyday health questions, explore complex medical information written in approachable language, and see linked, peer-reviewed clinical citations behind each response. Apple says OpenEvidence generates answers using medical information from established sources such as The New England Journal of Medicine, JAMA and Nature. Participants can also choose to share HealthKit data types — resting heart rate, activity, sleep or lab results — to test whether personal context changes relevance and understanding. The study will also look at whether the tool helps people prepare for clinical visits with their own doctors.

The framing is cautious by design. Sumbul Desai, M.D., Apple’s vice president of Health and Fitness, said the company believes “responsible innovation starts with scientific rigor and clinical grounding” and that a dedicated study is the most thoughtful way to understand how people engage with, comprehend and evaluate health AI in everyday life. Robert M. Wachter, M.D., chair of medicine at UCSF, argued that patients and clinicians are often using AI in separate worlds and that connecting trustworthy tools — and studying what works — is how those conversations improve.

Apple is explicit about the boundaries: the study app is not meant to diagnose or treat any condition or replace medical advice, and it includes safety guardrails intended to detect concerning questions and suggest follow-up with a provider or other resources. Apple also says study data will not be used to train AI models and that participants control whether to share HealthKit metrics or survey responses. That promise matters because the hardest part of consumer health AI is not producing fluent answers. It is earning trust when an answer is incomplete, unclear or confidently wrong, and then proving that the system behaves differently when the stakes are a lab result instead of a lunch recommendation.

Caveats. The study is virtual and opt-in, so the 200,000-person ceiling is an enrolment cap, not a guarantee of who participates or how representative they are. It is limited to US residents with recent Apple hardware and software, which skews the sample toward newer devices. Apple describes the collaboration and the privacy posture, but a consumer study is not the same as clinical validation of diagnostic accuracy. And the most important question — whether conversational health AI actually improves outcomes rather than just confidence — will take longer than a launch announcement to answer. Watch how Apple reports findings, what “trust” ends up measuring, and whether OpenEvidence’s clinician-facing credibility transfers cleanly to a consumer setting.

Frontier Models · The Max Release

Alibaba Unveils Qwen4-Max, a 2.4-Trillion-Parameter Model With a 1 Million-Token Context

Alibaba says Qwen4-Max activates 71 billion parameters per token, adds native vision, and cuts the average cost per task by about 60% on its Work360 benchmark versus Qwen3.8-Max. Smaller open-weight models from the Qwen4 series are promised soon.
2.4T total parameters, with 71 billion activated per token and a 1 million-token context window

Alibaba released Qwen4-Max on November 16, presenting it as the most capable model in the Qwen family to date. In its announcement, the company describes a sparse mixture-of-experts model with 2.4 trillion total parameters, native vision capabilities and a one-million-token context window. Alibaba says the model activates only 71 billion parameters per token — 25% fewer activated parameters than Qwen3.8-Max at the same 2.4T scale — as part of a broader architecture update across attention, residual connections and embeddings.

The company’s benchmark story mixes public leaderboards with internal tests. Alibaba says Qwen4-Max ranked 6th on Code Arena and posted strong scores on specialised benchmarks for agent workflows, workplace productivity and visual reasoning, including Agents’ Last Exam, Vals Index, JobBench and Chartography. On its proprietary Work360 benchmark, which evaluates 721 specialised fields, Alibaba claims Qwen4-Max outperforms Qwen3.8-Max overall while using 35% fewer tokens at high-effort reasoning and reducing the average cost per task by about 60%. It also highlights cloud resource scheduling and long-running agent demonstrations, including a 48-hour closed-loop run in a materials-discovery example.

Qwen4-Max is available now on QwenCloud via the Token Plan and API, and across Alibaba products including QwenWork and QwenStudio. Alibaba lists input with an implicit cache hit at $0.075 per million tokens. The company says open-weight, smaller-scale models from the Qwen4 series will be released to developers worldwide soon, a notable distinction given that this flagship is being offered as a hosted model rather than as downloadable weights today.

The interesting number is not 2.4 trillion by itself; it is the pairing of a huge parameter count with a smaller activation budget and a lower claimed cost per task. If those economics hold outside Alibaba’s own benchmark, the competition shifts from “who has the biggest model” to “who can make a big model cheap enough to run all day inside an agent loop.” The one-million-token window and native vision point the same way: this is being sold as a model that can stay with a job — codebase, document set, workflow — rather than answer one prompt and forget. The promise of smaller open-weight Qwen4 models is the other half of the strategy, because it keeps developers in the family even when they do not want to rent the flagship.

Caveats. Most performance and efficiency claims here come from Alibaba’s own announcement and proprietary benchmarks; independent evaluations will matter more than the launch copy. Code Arena rank is a useful signal but not a full capability audit, and “cost per task” depends heavily on workload, caching and tooling. The flagship itself is not open-weight at launch, so the openness question is deferred to the smaller releases Alibaba says are coming. Pricing and availability outside Alibaba’s platforms can differ by provider and region. Watch for third-party benchmark runs, the actual release of smaller Qwen4 weights, and whether the $0.075 cached-input price reflects typical real-world usage.

Bharat Semi Raises $24 Million to Put AI Inside the Image Sensor

The New Delhi startup is building programmable image sensors that combine capture, processing and AI on one chip. It has design wins, no revenue yet, and is targeting first commercial production in 2027.
$24M all-equity seed round to develop AI-enabled image sensors for global markets

Bharat Semi, a New Delhi-based semiconductor startup, has raised $24 million in an all-equity seed round to develop AI-enabled image sensors, TechCrunch reported. The round was led by Info Edge Group, South Park Commons and Indus Bridge Ventures, with participation from Valour Capital, Micromax, Gaingels and individual investors. The nearly five-year-old company is pre-revenue but says it has secured design wins with US defense technology company General Atomics and India’s Kaynes Semicon.

The startup is building programmable image sensors that combine image capture, processing and AI capabilities on a single chip, reducing the need to send visual data to separate processors. Co-founder Vrinda Kapoor told TechCrunch the goal is to move “from sensors to sense-making solutions,” and said the approach could cut system costs by at least 70% compared with conventional setups that use a separate image sensor, image signal processor and system-on-chip. Bharat Semi is targeting smart homes, industrial automation, surveillance and aerospace — use cases where a device that can interpret what it sees locally can be cheaper or faster than shipping pixels elsewhere.

The hardware is still on the way. Bharat Semi’s first-generation sensors are in prototyping, while second-generation sensors with more advanced AI capabilities are expected to reach prototype stage by mid-2027 and reach customers by the end of 2027. The company has about 75 employees across India and the US, including 10 to 12 in its American operations in Santa Clara, and plans to increase its US headcount to around 20 by mid-2027 while hiring in Europe. Kapoor said the new funding will go to engineering, product development and bringing sensors to market.

Image sensors are a quiet choke point in the AI hardware stack because so much machine perception still begins with a camera that captures everything and hands the problem to something else. Putting inference next to the pixels changes that trade: you can decide earlier what matters, move less data, and in some cases avoid sending sensitive imagery off-device at all. That is why the customer list here matters more than the seed headline. A defense and manufacturing pull suggests buyers who care about latency, power and reliability, not just a demo. The risk is that “AI on the sensor” is easy to pitch and hard to manufacture at yield, especially for a pre-revenue company promising a 2027 ramp.

Caveats. Bharat Semi has design wins but not shipped products or disclosed revenue, and commercial timelines in semiconductors often slip. The 70% cost-reduction figure is the company’s estimate, not an independently audited comparison across specific systems. A separate 2024 plan for a semiconductor fabrication project in India, involving a technology partnership with the US Space Force, remains in development and is distinct from this fabless sensor business; the new funding will not finance that facility. Watch for taped-out silicon, customer qualification milestones and whether the company can turn prototype interest into repeatable production.

Blue J Raises $176 Million to Turn Tax Research Into Agentic Workflows

The Toronto-based tax research platform says JMI Equity led a Series E valuing it at more than $700 million. The company plans to scale AI agents that can carry work from a client question to a partner-ready deliverable.
$176M Series E led by JMI Equity, valuing Blue J at more than $700 million

Blue J, a Toronto-based tax research and advisory platform, announced a US$176 million Series E led by JMI Equity, with participation from existing investors Oak HC/FT and Sapphire Ventures and from Intrepid Growth Partners. The company said the round values it at more than US$700 million (more than C$1 billion) and that it will use the capital to scale Workflows, its agentic AI for tax work, and to expand in the United Kingdom and internationally. Jack Duane, a principal at JMI Equity, will join Blue J’s board.

Blue J is betting that the valuable product is not the answer but the work that comes after it. Chief executive and co-founder Benjamin Alarie framed the shift as moving from answering a tax question to carrying the job — under a professional’s direction — to a finished, partner-ready deliverable. The company says Workflows builds on a research engine that has answered more than 14 million tax questions, and can help with tasks such as reviewing returns before filing, preparing IRS notice responses, analysing partnership, LLC and trust agreements, assessing multistate nexus and drafting client communications, with supporting sources and workflow steps available for inspection.

The round follows a year of reported growth. Blue J says annual recurring revenue grew 165% in the 15 months after its Series D, more than 6,500 organisations now use the platform, including Big Four firms and Fortune 100 companies, and it has doubled its team over the past year with expectations to exceed 200 employees by year-end. In October, it partnered with Legora to bring its tax research into Legora’s agentic operating system for legal work, which Blue J says is used by more than 140,000 legal professionals.

Tax is a useful stress test for the “AI agent” pitch because the work is rules-heavy, source-driven and unforgiving when wrong. A model that can draft is interesting; a system that can show its sources, expose its steps and fit inside a professional’s review loop is closer to something a firm can bill against. That is why Blue J is emphasising inspection and judgment rather than autonomy. The constraint is the same one every vertical agent company faces: once the workflow is valuable, it becomes sticky, but it also becomes responsible. In tax, a fast wrong answer is worse than a slow one, and “partner-ready” is a liability standard as much as a marketing phrase.

Caveats. Revenue growth, customer counts, question totals and usage figures are company-reported. The $700 million valuation is stated by Blue J and JMI in connection with the round, not an independent market price. Expanding from research into agentic execution means competing with both horizontal AI assistants and incumbent tax-software vendors that already own parts of the workflow. And the product still depends on practitioners reviewing outputs and making judgment calls, which limits how far automation can go without changing professional responsibility. Watch whether Workflows becomes a separate line item firms pay for, and how often “inspectable steps” hold up under audit pressure.

Mount Thor Raises $45 Million to Run Agentic AI on Racks of Apple Silicon

The San Francisco startup says it will use the Seed and Series A financing to expand a managed fleet of Apple hardware for research labs, agent platforms and enterprises that need macOS at data-centre scale.
$45M equity raised across Seed and Series A financings, plus non-dilutive capital for hardware

Mount Thor, a San Francisco startup building cloud infrastructure on Apple hardware, announced $45 million in equity capital across Seed and Series A financings. The company said CRV and Conviction co-led the Series A, while Chad Byers and Offline Ventures co-led the Seed. CSC Leasing, Enea Capital, Long Journey Ventures and Upper90 participated, along with strategic angels from Cognition, Core Automation, Daytona, Meta, OpenAI, Together AI and Vercel. Mount Thor also said it raised non-dilutive capital to support hardware purchases.

The pitch is simple: AI agents increasingly need to use computers the way people do, and many tasks require tools that only run on macOS. Mount Thor says it develops, deploys and manages rack-scale systems of Apple hardware with the security, scalability and availability customers expect from a cloud provider. Founder and CEO Peter Voell argued that decades of investment in accessibility and human-computer interaction make macOS a strong environment for agents to work and for humans to hold them accountable, and that chips like the M5 Ultra make Apple silicon an increasingly interesting platform for production inference.

The company lists use cases that span the agent stack: reinforcement-learning environments for frontier computer-use models, secure macOS sandboxes for agent infrastructure providers, long-running macOS instances for agentic products, faster build-and-test loops for Apple-platform software, and enterprise inference on open-source models optimised for Apple silicon. Mount Thor says it deployed its first data centre in less than six months and plans to expand capacity, with a particular focus on building an inference lab for open-source models on Apple silicon. The team includes people who previously built infrastructure at Cerebras, CoreWeave, Fluidstack, Nebius, OpenAI and xAI.

This is a bet that “computer use” will create a real market for macOS as infrastructure, not just as a developer laptop. GPUs dominate model training and most large-scale inference, but agents that need to click, test, sign in, build or automate inside Apple software have a different requirement: they need the operating system, not a substitute. If that demand grows, a managed fleet of Macs becomes a specialised cloud layer in the same way bare-metal GPU clouds became a layer. The counterargument is obvious too — Apple controls the hardware roadmap, the licensing boundaries and the economics, so any company renting Apple silicon at scale is building on someone else’s platform in a very literal sense.

Caveats. Mount Thor was founded in 2026 and is early; claims about fleet size, customer traction and cost or power advantages are company statements, not independent benchmarks. Apple’s terms, supply and product cadence will shape what “datacentre-scale Apple hardware” can actually mean. The strategic-angel list is notable but not a customer disclosure. And the market depends on agent workloads needing persistent macOS environments often enough to justify a dedicated provider. Watch whether the company publishes utilisation or performance data, how it prices against conventional GPU and VM clouds, and whether Apple itself moves to serve this demand directly.

Company Watch of the Day
APPLE
Apple shows up in two very different places today. In the lead, it is asking up to 200,000 US iPhone users to help it study how people ask health questions, understand answers and decide what to trust — with OpenEvidence providing medically grounded responses and Apple insisting the data will not be used to train models. In infrastructure, Mount Thor is raising $45 million to turn Apple silicon and macOS into a rented layer for agentic workloads. The through-line is that Apple’s hardware and software are becoming environments other people build on: a phone as a health-research instrument, a Mac fleet as a place for agents to work. The constraint is also the same. When Apple sets the rules — device eligibility, operating-system access, privacy posture, platform terms — it decides how far that ecosystem can stretch. Watch what the health study actually measures, and whether “macOS in the cloud” stays a niche or becomes a standard runtime for computer-using agents.
