Accountability · Who Owns the Findings

Anthropic and OpenAI Sought Veto Power Over the Safety Reports They Promised to Leave Alone

A Reuters investigation found both labs pushing for strict editorial control over outside evaluators’ findings as recently as October 5 — three weeks after their CEOs publicly pledged “editorial independence.” Contracts the wire reviewed bar evaluators from publishing confidential material without the company’s consent.
200+ Evaluators & researchers who signed the independence letter

In September, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei made a clean promise: outside evaluators should be able to publish their core findings “without editorial control.” Sam Altman agreed days later, and OpenAI publicly backed what it called “editorial independence” for assessors. On October 13, Reuters reported that the pledge did not survive the negotiating table. Two people familiar with the talks said that as recently as October 5, both Anthropic and OpenAI were seeking strict editorial control over what evaluators could publish — concerned that internal reviewers could leak trade secrets to rivals.

By October 8, one source said, Anthropic’s discussions with some evaluators appeared to have evolved — though whether that is a real policy shift, a narrowly-scoped exception, or a retreat after Reuters began asking is unclear. The two companies frame it differently. Anthropic says the “seeking significant control” characterization did not fit all its talks and that reviewers it has worked with before control their methods, criteria, conclusions and publications — but it reserves the right to comment on and approve reports from new evaluator firms while they learn how its models work. OpenAI says it does not seek approval over whole reports and only redacts confidential information, letting evaluators flag major cuts.

The stakes are structural, not procedural. Evaluators issued their own minimum conditions in a letter now signed by 200-plus researchers, including AI pioneer Geoffrey Hinton: independent control of findings, broad access to the labs, and protection from retaliation. Anthropic itself acknowledged in its September Accenture–Faculty announcement that directly funding the evaluator “creates an apparent conflict of interest,” and said it ultimately envisions pooled or government funding — a solution that remains notional. As Humane Intelligence’s Rumman Chowdhury put it, evaluators understand that access “comes at a cost” even without an explicit ban in writing. With no law mandating third-party access or publication rights, every frontier safety rating stays negotiable at the lab’s discretion.

02 · ENGINEERING
Infrastructure · Machine-Paced Development

Anthropic Deleted Git: Seven Engineers Rebuilt Version Control for a Repo Merging 30,000 Times a Day

Merges into Anthropic’s monorepo climbed from about 2,500 a day in May to over 30,000 by September — a 12x surge Claude wrote code into existence. So the company replaced git with an in-house system called Scion and moved human review from every line to the intent behind it.
12× Rise in daily merges in four months

The bottleneck, Anthropic wrote in an October 13 engineering post, is no longer the code. It is “all of the developer infrastructure built for human scale: source control, builds, and CI.” Its monorepo’s main-branch history is nearly 100 GB, a compressed working copy tops 25 GB, and the company runs on the order of 25 million code checkouts a day for CI alone — fetched naively, that is more than 600 petabytes of git traffic daily. At peak it operated several hundred internal git mirrors just to keep up. So it asked what source control would look like if you assumed the clients were agents that are always online.

The answer is Scion, a server-centric, API-first system with no fully materialized copy of the repository anywhere — built by a team of seven and rolled out across the whole monorepo in five months, alongside a code-review surface called Antique. Anthropic reports each cache tier running above a 99% hit rate, a CPU-and-memory footprint roughly 200× smaller than the old mirror fleet, and CI and research checkouts that take seconds instead of minutes. In a nice recursive touch, Claude helped build the very system that handles Claude’s output: handed a hand-written ingestion prototype with no further instructions, an early internal model rewrote it to use 20xs less memory and run 4x faster.

The most revealing detail is not about storage. Anthropic did not replicate git’s global merge lock — impossible to scale here — so it leans on post-merge and pre-deploy testing instead, and acknowledges concurrent changes can conflict, relying on Claude to resolve them quickly. Engineers now review the intent of each change rather than every line. The company is blunt about scope: “Scion is by no means a suitable replacement for git for general use,” makes sense only for enormous monorepos with tens of thousands of engineers or swarms of agents, and will not be released — Anthropic says it will work with GitHub and GitLab instead. The gate in front of main moved; how safe that is for anyone who does not own the model remains the open question.

03 · SECURITY
National Security · The Bioweapon Question

MI5 Opens an AI Threat Centre — and Names the Fear It Weighs Most: Bioweapons

Britain’s spy chief says MI5 is devoting more resource to countering state threats than during the Cold War and has already caught terrorists using AI. A new AI Threat Assessment Centre will pool AISI, NCA and NCSC analysts to “separate the bluster from the realities.”
4× Rise in MI5’s state-threat caseload in a decade

In his annual threat briefing at Thames House on October 13, MI5 Director General Sir Ken McCallum announced the launch of the AI Threat Assessment Centre (AITAC), a new unit that will draw on expertise from the UK’s AI Security Institute, the National Crime Agency and the National Cyber Security Centre. Its job, he said, is to produce all-source assessments that “separate the bluster from the realities” of AI risk — and to stop different departments issuing “competitive analysis, slightly conflicting and confusing messages.” It is the third such centre MI5 has stood up, after units for terrorism and state threats.

McCallum was unusually specific about the threat he weights most. The danger is “not yet realised,” he said, but of every misuse case he attaches the greatest weight to AI’s potential to “change the stakes on existing or even new bioweapons.” One concern is that comparatively low-sophistication actors could see a capability uplift if guardrails fail — putting dangerous knowledge in “the hands of people we would all rather didn’t have that knowledge.” A second, distinct risk: AI could help generate novel pathogens not previously seen. He said the government is taking additional steps on biological weapons.

The speech lands the same week Westminster put four frontier labs on the stand — and on the same day MI5’s framing shifted from “AI as a tool” to AI as a force multiplier for attackers. McCallum said the agency has caught terrorists seeking to exploit AI to improve reconnaissance and attack methods, with AI-enabled drones adding “a new twist,” and confirmed MI5 is devoting more resource to state threats than it did during the Cold War. The realistic read is a security service moving from watching AI to budgeting against it: AITAC is not a research body, it is an intelligence-production line, and bioweapons are now on the same assessment stack as terrorism and hostile states.

04 · ROBOTICS
Physical AI · A Robot’s Brain

NEURA Gives a Humanoid a Brain With Touch, Sound and Memory — Then Sets It to Do the Laundry

The German robotics unicorn unveiled Neuron AI, a Perception-Language-Action foundation model that fuses 3D vision, touch, force, audio, robot state and language with memory. At GTC Berlin, its humanoid 4NE1 will run an eleven-minute laundry sequence with no human help.
11 min Fully autonomous laundry run at GTC Berlin

Most robot foundation models see the world through cameras. NEURA Robotics, the Metzingen-based unicorn, is betting that is not enough. On October 14 it introduced Neuron AI, a multimodal model built on a Perception-Language-Action (PLA) architecture that encodes 3D vision, touch and force, audio, robot state and language into one shared, time-aligned space and turns them into a single stream of physical action. The pitch is direct: a robot should be able to grasp a box it cannot see, hear when a connector clicks into place, and feel whether a screw has caught — the way a human does.

The other half of the design is memory. A robot acting in a continuous physical world has to track objects and the consequences of its own actions even when they leave its field of view — object permanence. NEURA adapted its architecture specifically to add memory and trained it on dedicated datasets, then trained the whole model across five NEURA embodiments and their grippers and hands, on real-world robot data, simulation-amplified data and human demonstrations captured by sensor suits. Customers fine-tune it for their own tasks and deploy skills across fleets via the Neuraverse platform, with “Time-to-Skill” as the central metric. It runs locally on the robot, not in the cloud.

The proof arrives next week, and it is deliberately mundane. At NVIDIA GTC Berlin (October 20–22), NEURA’s humanoid 4NE1 (“For Anyone”) will complete an eleven-minute laundry-handling sequence — sorting, loading a washing machine, closing and reopening it, unloading, and transferring to a dryer — with no human intervention. The company says the hard part is not any single movement but holding context and memory across the whole task. The launch follows a €1.2B Series C led by Tether with Nvidia and Amazon, and data partnerships with Schaeffler and Bosch — positioning a European challenger against Google’s Gemini Robotics work and Nvidia’s Isaac GR00T just as humanoid shipments are scaling.

05 · PARTNERSHIPS
World · Scam Shield in Your Pocket

Google DeepMind Built a Scam Detector That Never Leaves Your Phone — and Singapore Is First

A fine-tuned Gemma model runs entirely on-device: a lightweight classifier screens incoming messages, then the model reads the whole conversation to confirm a real scam or dismiss a false alarm. Google says neither it nor Singapore’s government can see the encrypted chats it guards.
0 Conversations sent to the cloud

Announced October 14 with GovTech Singapore under Google’s National AI Partnership, the tool attacks a specific problem: scam messages arriving inside encrypted apps, where servers — including the platforms’ own — cannot read them. DeepMind fine-tuned Google’s lightweight Gemma model on local scam conversations: impersonations of public institutions, banks and public figures, plus regional schemes. Because the model runs on the device, it can analyze conversations inside encrypted apps without the Singapore Government or Google accessing them.

The system is a two-stage pipeline. A lightweight classifier screens every incoming message first, at minimal power cost, to catch likely threats. Messages it flags are escalated to the fine-tuned Gemma model, which reads a larger snapshot of the conversation to confirm a scam or clear a false alarm. When it detects manipulative patterns, it fires a real-time on-device warning telling the user to pause before sharing money or sensitive information. The privacy claim is structural rather than a policy promise: there is no server round-trip to leak.

The caveats matter as much as the build. The announcement names no shipping app, platform partner or rollout date; deployment will be phased with the Singapore Government, and the model only accesses conversations with user consent under an app’s terms. DeepMind published no accuracy figures — so the scam detector should be judged on the numbers that eventually arrive, not the architecture. Still, it points at a pattern worth watching: the most privacy-sensitive AI safety features are moving onto the device precisely because the encrypted-messaging layer cannot be inspected from the cloud — and governments, not just app stores, are becoming the first integration partners. The same day, separately, Anthropic opened its fifth APAC office in Singapore and signed its own AI-safety MOU with the country’s MDDI.

ANTHROPIC
Anthropic is everywhere in today’s stack, and the three appearances tell one story about a lab scaling faster than the ground beneath it. In Scion, its code output outgrows human-era tooling so completely that a seven-person team deletes git and rebuilds version control in five months — and moves human review from every line to just the intent. In the Reuters story, the same lab that pledged evaluators “editorial independence” negotiates to approve their reports for new firms, fighting over what “confidential” means. The through-line: a company writing code faster than anyone has before, while the rules for who gets to check that code are still being written at the same speed.
