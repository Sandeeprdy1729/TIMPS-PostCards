Nvidia · The Reversal

The Board That Said Never Reinstated Jensen Huang. Nvidia Added a Trillion Dollars.

Eleven days after ruling him out, the board voted unanimously to bring its founder back, citing input from senior US officials. The market responded with the chipmaker's best day since 2003.
31.48% Nvidia's closing gain on Monday — nearly $1 trillion in market cap added back in one session

On Monday, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had been reinstated as president, CEO and a board director, effective immediately. The board said it voted unanimously after “a series of discussions and meetings” and was satisfied with Huang's “candor and ability to lead NVIDIA.” The line that matters came next: in reaching its decision, the board “received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang's reinstatement poses no national security risk.” Harvey C. Jones stays as chair. Colette Kress, who served as interim CEO, returns to her duties as chief financial officer.

Huang had resigned on November 13 after reports of several extramarital affairs, including one with a woman US officials say was in contact with an officer of China's Ministry of State Security. Last week the board said it did not intend to reinstate him. On Monday it said he was “the right person to lead NVIDIA.” Shares closed up 31.48%, their best day since May 2003, recovering nearly $1 trillion in market capitalisation in a single session. The stock had slumped more than 40% over the previous month and had shed nearly 33% from the day Huang disclosed the affair.

The rally was not just about one company. Arm rose 16.08%, Dell 20.46% and Micron 19.04%; Meta gained 6.55%, Alphabet 6.34%, AMD 6.85%. The Nasdaq 100 jumped about 7%, snapping a brutal week for the AI trade. President Trump had publicly argued that Huang deserved a second chance, and said after the decision that there was no reason to believe he posed a national security risk. The board's statement did not name which senior officials weighed in, what they said, or what conditions — if any — were attached to the founder's return.

A board can reverse itself in eleven days when the stock and the White House are pointing the same way. The market has repriced the founder; the national-security question has been asserted, not answered. “We received input from senior levels of government” is not a finding, and a confidence vote from the people who just lost a trillion dollars is not independent evidence. Huang is back because the alternative was judged worse — for the stock, for the AI buildout and for the administration. Watch what the next earnings call sounds like, and whether anyone in Congress ever gets the name of the official who made the call.

Caveats. The “no national security risk” judgement is the board's, based on government input that was neither named nor published. The 31.48% is one session, and the stock is still down sharply over the month. The rally was market-wide, so it is not a clean verdict on governance. CNBC reports the affair and the timeline; Nvidia's press release is the source for the board's wording. Watch for congressional hearings, any conditions placed on Huang, and whether the separate investigation is ever published.

02 · MODELS
Models · The Decider

AWS Built a Model That Doesn't Write. It Decides. And It Beat Google's.

Five open Strands Decider v2 models built on Gemma 4 return probabilities, not prose. The biggest averages 82.1 across six benchmarks.
82.1 average score of the 31B Strands Decider v2 across six public text benchmarks, ahead of Gemini Decision 1's 79.4

AWS launched Strands Decider v2 on Monday: five open decision models built on Google's Gemma 4 and released under the Apache 2.0 licence. The sizes run from 2 billion parameters to a 31-billion dense model, with a 4B and a 26B-A4B mixture-of-experts model in between. They do not generate text. A decision model takes a state — a support ticket, an agent trace — plus a question and a fixed set of possible answers, then returns a probability for each answer.

AWS says the 31B model averages 82.1 across six public text benchmarks, ahead of every rival it tested, including Google's Gemini Decision 1 at 79.4. It wins JevBench 93.1 to 92.2. But it loses on three of the six: Perplexity's Decider v1.3 is better on PubMedQA and When2Call, and vLLM Semantic Router's open d3-ultra wins a test built from New Yorker cartoon captions. The 26B-A4B model averages 79.9; the E4B jumps from 62.7 in v1 to 74.4. Calibration error falls to 0.027 for the new 31B from 0.062 in v1, though Gemini still leads at 0.025. On multimodal tests it scores 90.1 on Mind2Web Multimodal and 70.2 on Video-MME, just behind Perplexity.

The architecture is the quiet story. Most language models use causal attention, where each token sees only what came before it. Because a decision model never writes a sentence, the Strands team let every token attend to the whole state in both directions. That means asking 20 questions about one agent trace costs little more than asking one — the encoded state gets reused. The models are available from the Strands project on GitHub, but not yet on Amazon Bedrock.

The unglamorous layer is the one agents actually need. A system that has to choose among fixed options, thousands of times a day, is better served by a calibrated probability than by a paragraph of plausible reasoning. Apache 2.0 means anyone can run these locally, and the bidirectional trick is a real cost advantage. Treat 82.1 as AWS's number, not a crown — rivals win three of the six benchmarks — but the direction is clear: the model that decides is becoming a separate product from the model that talks.

Caveats. All benchmarks come from AWS, which chose the rivals and the tests. The models are not on Bedrock yet, so adoption is unknown. Decision models only help when the answer set is fixed; an open-ended question still needs a generator. Calibration still trails Gemini, and a benchmark lead on Monday is not a production result. Watch for third-party evaluations, a Bedrock launch, and whether Anthropic or Google ships a comparable open decider.

03 · EARNINGS
Infrastructure · The AI Bill

HPE Posted Its Fastest Growth Ever. Its Stock Fell 6% Anyway.

Revenue jumped 50% to $14.55 billion and guidance went up. Investors wanted more from the company selling the servers underneath the AI boom.
$14.55B HPE's fourth-quarter revenue, up 50% year over year — its fastest quarterly growth on record

Hewlett Packard Enterprise reported on Monday fourth-quarter revenue of $14.55 billion, up 50% from a year earlier and above the Street's $14.47 billion estimate. Adjusted earnings were $1.43 per share against a $1.26 consensus, and net profit came in at $1.82 billion, versus just $175 million in the year-ago quarter. CEO Antonio Neri told analysts the raised outlook reflects demand for AI servers extending well into next year, as the industry shifts from training models to supporting inference.

HPE lifted its full-year revenue guidance to a range of $59.4 billion to $63.1 billion, having previously guided for growth of 13% to 17%. For the current quarter it sees $14 billion to $14.6 billion, roughly 54% growth at the midpoint. That is a company seeing the AI buildout in its order book. It was not enough: even as a Nvidia-fuelled rally lifted the rest of the sector, HPE shares fell more than 6% after hours.

Read that against yesterday's story about suppliers repricing signed AI server contracts. HPE is both a beneficiary of that squeeze and the company that made it official in March, when Neri said the company had amended its quoting terms to allow repricing of existing orders for commodity cost increases. Record growth and a higher forecast are what the top line looks like when everyone wants a server. The after-hours drop is what the margin question looks like when memory prices are still climbing.

The AI infrastructure trade has moved from “is there demand?” to “who keeps the margin?” HPE's numbers answer the first question emphatically. The market's answer to the second was a 6% haircut. When a company can grow revenue 50%, beat on earnings, raise guidance and still get sold, the bar has moved — investors are pricing the cost of satisfying that demand, not the demand itself. Watch whether the repricing controversy turns into a margin story on the next call.

Caveats. The after-hours move is not a full trading session and may reverse. The new guidance range is wide, and the comparison with the previous 13%–17% growth figure comes from HPE's own disclosure. Results are reported figures, not audited segment-level profitability, and the link to contract repricing is our read across two stories, not something management said. Watch the next session, the full 10-Q, and whether other server vendors report the same margin pressure.

04 · CAPITAL
Fintech · The Prediction Portfolio

A $20 Million Bet That Your Market Take Should Become a Portfolio

Supertake's seed was led by Adjacent, with Coinbase Ventures and USV joining. Its agents turn a prediction into actual trades.
$20M seed round led by Adjacent, with Coinbase Ventures and Union Square Ventures participating

Supertake raised a $20 million seed round led by Adjacent, with Coinbase Ventures and Union Square Ventures joining. Angels include Nat Friedman, Vercel CEO Guillermo Rauch, Nikita Bier, Scott Belsky and former Coinbase executive Nemil Dalal. The round follows a $2 million pre-seed. The startup was incubated at USV by Michael Mignano, co-founder and former CEO of Anchor, who is now executive chairman.

The pitch is unusually direct. Users state a view about a market; the service uses frontier models and trading agents to turn that idea into a real portfolio, executing through Coinbase and Robinhood. The day-to-day team came from Coinbase: co-founder and CEO Lincoln Murr and CTO John Peterson led AI product and engineering there, including Coinbase advisor, Coinbase for agents and agentic wallets. Early customers have entrusted the system with “thousands” of dollars; Mignano says he has “hundreds of thousands” under its management. The new money goes to engineering, design and growth.

This is where agentic AI stops writing and starts holding the wallet. Until now most consumer finance agents have summarised, compared or recommended. Supertake wants to act, continuously, on a thesis the user expressed in plain language. That puts it at the intersection of two crowded trends — prediction markets and automated investing — and squarely inside the regulatory question of who is responsible when an agent trades.

The interesting number is not the $20 million; it is “hundreds of thousands” of real dollars already routed through the system. A bot that writes a research note is a feature. A bot that rebalances a portfolio is a fiduciary question. The founders know the plumbing — they built agentic wallets at Coinbase — which is why a small seed can buy a serious option here. Watch what happens on the first bad trade, and whether the product can explain its decisions to someone who has to file a tax return.

Caveats. Assets-under-management figures come from the founders and are not independently verified; there is no performance data, no drawdown history and no named model. Agent-led trading carries custody, suitability and regulatory exposure that a seed round does not remove. Coinbase and Robinhood integration is described by the company. Watch for a published track record, a clear answer on who is the adviser of record, and whether the SEC or FINRA says anything.

05 · SECURITY
Espionage · The Hidden Recorder

He Used OpenAI's Own Coding Tool to Improve a Hidden Recorder in Its Conference Room

Prosecutors say the ex-engineer added a voice-detection skill with Codex and stored dozens of recordings — some likely trade secrets — in Dropbox.
Dozens recordings of OpenAI meetings in September found on the engineer's personal computer, prosecutors say

Federal prosecutors say former OpenAI engineer Abdulrahman Alfozan used the company's own coding product to improve a hidden recording device inside one of its executive meeting rooms. In a motion filed Monday, US attorneys say he prompted Codex to add a voice-detection skill to a recorder of the kind OpenAI found tucked behind a TV, then used the tool to organise the resulting files in Dropbox. He has pleaded not guilty, according to the reporting.

The filing says agents found audio files on his personal computer containing “dozens of recordings of conversations” at OpenAI throughout September, including meetings that involved “what appears likely to be confidential trade secret material.” They also found a photograph on his phone of a document marked “CONFIDENTIAL” describing a “strategic partnership” with another company. The disclosure is part of a fight over whether Alfozan should remain detained while he awaits trial.

The mechanics are the lesson. This is not a zero-day or a state-sponsored implant. It is a small device in a room where people speak freely, a mainstream coding assistant to make it more reliable, and a consumer cloud folder to sort the output. The same agent tools that labs sell to help developers ship faster also help an insider exfiltrate more efficiently. The hardest security boundary turns out to be the one a badge gets you through.

The weapon was not exotic. It was a hidden recorder, an AI coding assistant and a Dropbox account. If one of the most security-conscious labs in the world can be bugged from inside its own conference room, “trusted insider” needs a new definition. Codex did not create the intent, but it did make the execution tidier — a useful reminder that productivity tools are dual-use by default. Watch the detention ruling, and whether any of the recorded material turns up in a competitor's hands.

Caveats. These are allegations in a prosecution motion, not findings of fact; Alfozan has denied wrongdoing. The descriptions of the device and the recordings come from OpenAI records cited by prosecutors. The filing says the material “appears likely” to be trade secret material — that is the government's characterisation, not a court ruling. No verified evidence here that the recordings were passed to a foreign party. Watch for the detention decision and the trial timeline.
