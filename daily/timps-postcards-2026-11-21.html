Geopolitics · The Chinese Model

Cursor Built Its Coding Model on a Chinese AI. Now Washington Wants It Gone.

A House investigation finds SpaceX's $60 billion coding subsidiary plans to phase out Chinese open-weight models — the same models that made its own Composer 2 competitive.
$60B the implied equity value when SpaceX acquired Cursor in August

On Friday, the House Committee on Homeland Security and the House Select Committee on China published a report, shared exclusively with CNBC, saying Cursor had told lawmakers it plans to “explore phasing out PRC-developed open-weight models because of geopolitical risk.” The awkward part is that Cursor's own coding model, Composer 2, was built on top of Moonshot AI's Kimi K2.5, a Chinese model. SpaceX announced a deal to buy Cursor in June and closed the acquisition in August at an implied $60 billion equity value. Cursor has also been involved in training SpaceX's Grok family of models. The company that might now drop Chinese models is the same one that built its flagship product on them.

The report follows a months-long investigation into how Chinese AI models entered the American tech stack. It finds that cost, customization and near-frontier performance are driving adoption, and that the United States lacks a competitive open-weight alternative at “price and performance the market demands.” Airbnb and DoorDash also engaged with the investigation about their use of Chinese models. “Beijing is now seeking to turn those technological gains into global market share and long-term dependence,” the report says. Cursor, SpaceX and DoorDash did not respond to CNBC's requests for comment; Airbnb declined; the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security did not immediately respond. China's embassy in the UK and its foreign ministry also did not respond.

The recommendations go further than Cursor's voluntary phase-out. The committees want a “national American open-weight initiative” combining public computing power, shared evaluation infrastructure and “lawful access” to American frontier models for training. They also want an October order that banned DeepSeek from federal civilian agency systems extended into a blanket prohibition on “PRC-developed AI models” in federal information systems and in the performance of federal contracts. Separately, BIS announced an interim final rule on Monday designed to stop Chinese companies from accessing Nvidia chips remotely through overseas data centers, part of a wider worry about distillation — newer models using older ones to train faster.

This is the first clear case of a US company preparing to unbuild part of its own product to satisfy a geopolitical worry. The report's most revealing line is not the ban recommendation but the admission that America has no competitive open-weight alternative at the price and performance buyers want. You cannot legislate a substitute into existence. If Cursor really phases out Kimi K2.5, someone else's model fills the gap — and the team that built Composer 2 on it has to start over. Meanwhile the same Congress that wants the phase-out is proposing a government-funded replacement, which is an acknowledgement that the market chose Chinese models for a reason.

Caveats. “Considering” and “explore phasing out” are not a decision, and no timeline or replacement model was named. The report comes from two House committees following a months-long investigation; the companies did not confirm its account. It is also unclear what “PRC-developed” would cover — a model built partly on Chinese weights, for example — and whether an American open-weight initiative would ever be funded. Watch for an actual Cursor announcement, how the BIS remote-access rule is enforced, and whether Airbnb or DoorDash follow.

02 · SECURITY
Government · The Second Breach

The FBI Got Hacked Again. This Time It Was the Nation's Crime Database.

The National Crime Information Center — the system police use for warrants and background checks — is the second FBI system breached in months, discovered days after the director touted the bureau's cyber record on television.
NCIC the nationwide law-enforcement database at the centre of the apparent intrusion

The FBI is investigating an apparent breach of the National Crime Information Center, two people briefed on the investigation told CNN. NCIC is a lifeline between federal, state and local law enforcement, used nationwide to share information on warrants and background checks. The intrusion was discovered in recent days, and it is the second such breach in recent months. The FBI did not immediately respond to a request for comment. A former FBI official who led investigations into cybercriminal groups told CNN that officers rely on NCIC every day to identify wanted fugitives and check for outstanding warrants, and that the concern goes beyond whether hackers got in.

The timing is awkward. On Thursday, after bureau officials learned of the latest incident, FBI Director Kash Patel appeared on Fox News to tout the bureau's cybersecurity work. He discussed Operation Winter Shield, an operation completed months ago aimed at urging companies to update their systems so hackers could not reach sensitive data, and spoke about it as if it were still underway. “Other administrations could have made these updates, but they didn't,” Patel said. “We are staying ahead of the curve and making up for lost time.” The appearance came as the Trump administration has been trying to reassure the public about cybersecurity after a run of high-profile outages affecting banks, the National Security Agency and the FAA's air traffic control system.

This is a pattern, not an isolated event. In September, a group called ShinyHunters targeted sensitive employee personal data from an FBI jobs website; a Canadian cybersecurity researcher has been arrested and charged in connection with that breach, and some FBI employees later said they felt left in the dark about whether they were affected and underwhelmed by the security resources offered to victims. In a 2023 breach, hackers compromised a computer system the FBI's New York field office used in child sexual exploitation investigations, including a system storing images related to the Jeffrey Epstein probe. Each time, the affected system turned out to be one that other people depended on.

The pattern is worse than the single breach. A database that every police officer in America uses to decide whether to pull someone over is apparently permeable, and the bureau found out about it days after its director went on television to say the grown-ups were back in charge. The question is not whether the FBI can be hacked — every large institution can. It is whether the people running the country's most sensitive law-enforcement system can tell when it happened, and whether they will say so before a reporter does. Patel's “staying ahead of the curve” line will now be read against a database that was apparently not ahead of anything.

Caveats. CNN describes an “apparent breach” based on two anonymous sources; the FBI has not confirmed it, and there is no public detail on what was accessed, whether data was copied, or how long the intruders were inside. There is no confirmed connection to the September ShinyHunters attack or the 2023 New York field office breach. The criticism of the director's timing is a matter of public record, but he was discussing a different, completed operation. Watch for a formal FBI statement, notifications to state and local agencies, and any change in how officers are told to treat NCIC results.

03 · CAPITAL
Chips · The Trillion-Dollar Maybe

Masayoshi Son Tried to Buy SK Hynix for $1 Trillion. The Answer Was No.

The SoftBank chief sounded out Wall Street about the biggest buyout ever attempted, then watched his own company say the idea was dead. Memory — the one piece missing from his AI empire — was the reason.
$1T the price SoftBank's founder explored for the world's second-largest memory chipmaker

The Information reported on Friday that SoftBank chairman Masayoshi Son recently approached major investment banks about how to finance a roughly $1 trillion buyout of SK Hynix, the world's second-largest memory chipmaker. A SoftBank spokesperson says the idea is “no longer under active consideration” and that the company reviews a range of opportunities; SK Hynix says it has no knowledge of the matter. Seoul Economic Daily reports the deal could have reached $1 trillion, or about 1,400 trillion won, but appears to have been dropped at an early stage because it was unlikely to be financeable.

The arithmetic explains both the number and the collapse. SK Hynix has a market value of about $577.7 billion, or 777 trillion won, down sharply from a peak reached about a month earlier. The $1 trillion figure floated as a purchase price reflects that peak market value plus a 40% control premium. In other words, the bid was priced off a market that no longer exists. Even so, the approach reveals the motive: memory is the one piece of the AI supply chain SoftBank does not already own. SK Hynix is one of three suppliers of high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators, alongside Samsung and Micron, and the accelerators that need it are effectively a Nvidia monopoly.

What SoftBank does own is considerable. It is the largest shareholder in Arm, the chip-design IP company, and in SB Energy, which operates data center and power businesses. It owns Ampere Computing, a server chip designer, and DigitalBridge, a data center investment firm, and has taken large stakes in Intel and OpenAI. Son has form for audacious financing: he funded the OpenAI stake by pledging Arm shares as collateral, borrowed against his Alibaba holding before selling it to cover the $32 billion purchase of Arm, and in 2017 assembled Saudi and UAE sovereign wealth money for the $100 billion Vision Fund. An industry official told Seoul Economic Daily that the approach, feasible or not, showed Son has a plan to take control of the entire AI value chain.

A $1 trillion buyout that never happens is still a useful document. It shows where the AI gold rush has moved: from models to the physical stuff they sit on. Son has spent years assembling design, servers, power and data centers, and the dependency he cannot control is memory — the part that HBM made strategic overnight. He is not alone in noticing. The reason this was dismissed as unrealistic is not that the ambition is strange; it is that the price of the thing he wants rose faster than his ability to borrow against the things he already owns. That is a financing problem, not a vision problem, and financing problems have a way of resolving themselves when the underlying asset keeps appreciating.

Caveats. The story originates with The Information citing people familiar with the matter; Reuters and others picked it up, and the financial detail here comes largely from Seoul Economic Daily's Korean-language reporting, translated. No bank has confirmed being approached. SoftBank says the idea is shelved and SK Hynix says it knows nothing. The 40% premium and $577.7 billion market cap are the outlet's calculations, not a disclosed offer. An industry source also notes the Korean government was unlikely ever to approve a foreign takeover of a company like SK Hynix. Watch for a smaller SoftBank stake instead, for HBM pricing, and for any bank that admits it took the call.

04 · M&A
Security · The Security Mega-Deal

CrowdStrike Is in Talks to Buy Zscaler. That Would Be a $265 Billion Security Giant.

The two companies guard different halves of the corporate network — the endpoint and the connection. If this closes, one company would do both, at nearly 300 times the size of CrowdStrike's biggest purchase to date.
$235B CrowdStrike's market cap, versus $32 billion for Zscaler, before a premium

Bloomberg reported on Friday that CrowdStrike is in talks to acquire Zscaler, in a deal that would be among the largest in the history of cybersecurity. Zscaler had a market value of $32 billion at the end of regular trading Friday and jumped 18% in extended trading on the news, while CrowdStrike — valued at $235 billion — fell more than 8%. Together they would be a company worth roughly $265 billion. Sources say the talks are ongoing and could come together soon, but caution they may still fall through. CrowdStrike declined to comment; Zscaler did not immediately respond.

The two companies cover different ground, which is the logic of the deal. CrowdStrike's Falcon platform detects and blocks malicious activity across cloud instances, on-premises servers and employee devices, and automates tasks like finding sensitive records stored insecurely. Zscaler sells zero-trust network access, software that limits which applications workers can reach inside the corporate network, replacing VPNs that are slow and hand employees the keys to everything. It also sells Zscaler Deception, which plants bogus applications, passwords and files as sensors that trigger an alert when an attacker touches them, plus a ZDX Agent that generates troubleshooting advice. Together, one company would watch the device and the path into the network.

The scale is what makes this remarkable. CrowdStrike's biggest acquisition to date was the $970 million purchase of SGNL earlier this year; this month it announced plans to buy agentic observability platform Fiddler AI and AI trust platform Dreadnode, both small. For comparison, Google bought Wiz for $32 billion last year, Cisco paid about $28 billion for Splunk in 2024, and Palo Alto closed its $25 billion purchase of CyberArk earlier this year. CrowdStrike's stock has about doubled in 2026 to its $235 billion market cap, while Zscaler is down 13% to $32 billion; Palo Alto closed Friday near $282 billion. Because software buyouts usually carry a premium to the last unaffected market cap, CrowdStrike would likely pay more than $32 billion, pushing the combined company above $250 billion.

The AI boom turned endpoint detection into a must-have, and CrowdStrike's doubled stock is the receipt. Buying Zscaler is a bet that the next security budget gets spent on controlling connections rather than only watching devices — a zero-trust platform with an AI troubleshooting assistant inside it is what an enterprise buys once it has decided the VPN is the problem. The risk is the usual one for mega-deals: pay a premium for a company whose stock is down 13%, then spend two years reconciling two very different sales motions. The market's reaction — Zscaler up 18%, CrowdStrike down 8% — is a clean vote on who gets the better end of that trade.

Caveats. Talks are preliminary, the sources are people familiar rather than the companies, and no price or timetable has been reported. The 18% jump and 8% drop are after-hours snapshots, not closing prices, and other outlets reported slightly different figures at different moments. The roughly $265 billion combined value ignores the premium a buyer would pay, assumed debt and integration costs. CrowdStrike's “biggest deal to date” depends on whether you count announced or closed acquisitions. Watch for a confirmation, for regulatory scrutiny of the combined platform, and for whether CrowdStrike's stock keeps sliding.

Frontier · The Looped Model

A 26-Billion-Parameter Model That Runs on Your Laptop Matches a 27-Billion One

Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini runs its middle layers three times per token instead of getting bigger. It fits on a MacBook, costs a sixth as much per task — and is harder to read.
5.4B active parameters that match a 27B model on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index

Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini, an open-weights looped transformer with 26.4 billion total parameters, 3.4 billion active, and 5.4 billion weight activations per token once the three passes are counted. Twelve of its 24 layers run three times for every token, adding compute without adding output tokens. Artificial Analysis scores it 20 on its Intelligence Index, tying Qwen3.8 27B (xhigh) and Nanbeige4.3-33B while using roughly five and six times fewer active parameters respectively. It sits on the Pareto frontier for intelligence against both total and active parameters, and leads open-weights models under 100 billion total parameters on agentic coding benchmarks such as DeepSWE v1.1, where it scores 48.2%.

The efficiency numbers are the story. Artificial Analysis finds Inkling 2 Mini uses about 2.8 times less compute than Qwen3.8 27B for the same score, is roughly six times cheaper ($0.59 versus $3.53 per Intelligence Index task) and about four times faster (9.0 minutes versus 38.2). It does use about 38% more output tokens than Qwen3.8 — roughly 152,000 per task versus 110,000 — because looping reasons by running the same layers again instead of writing a longer chain of thought. Most weights are trained and deployed natively in NVFP4, with the last four layers in MXFP8; the total footprint is about 20GB, small enough for a laptop. A researcher took it on a flight and had it build a playable flight simulator offline on a MacBook Pro in a few minutes.

Looping is also an experiment in oversight. Thinking Machines says reasoning without writing it down makes chain-of-thought monitoring harder, so it released a research-only variable-loop version to study the trade-off. On that model, the no-CoT time horizon improved from 16 seconds at one pass to 37 seconds at three, and performance on a serial-reasoning benchmark kept improving out to ten loops — twice as many as it was trained with. Inkling 2 Mini is strong on search-and-tool benchmarks including BrowseComp, DeepSearchQA and MCP Atlas; knowledge is still mostly a function of parameter count, so its factuality score is in line with similar-sized models and it compensates by using tools. It ships under Apache 2.0 with a one-million-token context, text and image input, and is available on Tinker.

The interesting thing here is not that a small model is good — small good models are becoming routine. It is that the industry is trading output tokens for internal compute, and the side effect is a model you cannot read as easily. Chain-of-thought monitoring is one of the few tools anyone has for catching a model doing something it should not. A model that thinks by looping instead of writing is a model that hides its reasoning inside the math. Thinking Machines released the variable-loop version partly so researchers can study exactly that, which is a more useful contribution than another benchmark win. The lab that built this is also the one telling you it may be harder to watch.

Caveats. Benchmarks come from Thinking Machines and Artificial Analysis, whose evaluation setups vary by model, and the cost and speed figures use the lab's own pricing plus a compute proxy rather than measured wall-clock on your hardware. The 20 score ties Qwen3.8 27B but GPT-6 Luna (max) scores 28 for $0.15 a task, so the frontier is still ahead. The monitoring claims are the lab's own experiments on a research variant, not an independent audit, and the 614 GB/s decoding figure is a theoretical ceiling. Watch whether looped architectures get adopted elsewhere, and whether monitoring research catches up with them.
