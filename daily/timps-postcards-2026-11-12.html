Governance · The Board Meets Wednesday

Nvidia’s Board Is Preparing to Remove Jensen Huang

Nvidia’s board met on Wednesday evening to decide whether to oust the chief executive of a $4 trillion company that has no succession plan. The stock fell more than 9% after hours and touched a 52-week low. Some directors favour making CFO Colette Kress interim CEO while an outside search firm looks for a permanent one. The board has already briefed the White House.
$600B market value Nvidia has lost this week, including Wednesday’s after-hours selloff

Nvidia’s board spent Wednesday evening deciding whether to remove Jensen Huang, the founder who turned a graphics-card company into the reference asset of the artificial-intelligence trade. CNBC confirmed the meeting. Semafor had reported earlier that the board was preparing to oust him as soon as Wednesday, and two people familiar said some directors favour naming chief financial officer Colette Kress as interim chief executive. The full board has not voted. Directors were still meeting late on Wednesday.

The shift happened in four days. On Sunday the board said it had confidence in Huang and his leadership team, and Nvidia said it had “no reason to believe” that company secrets or U.S. national security had been compromised. On Wednesday the directors were weighing his removal. The trigger was a pair of reports that landed minutes apart. The Wall Street Journal reported that Huang had two extramarital affairs, one with Alice Huang, a Chinese citizen whom U.S. intelligence believes was recruited to gather information for China’s Ministry of State Security. The New York Times then reported a second relationship, with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia’s vice president of health care — a role that reported to the chief executive. Huang told staff on Sunday: “I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did.” Beijing has rejected the espionage allegation.

The mechanics of a removal are unusually clean here, which is part of why the market believes it. Nvidia has 11 directors and considers all but Huang independent. Its bylaws let a majority remove the chief executive “with or without cause.” The board has no chair; it is led by an independent lead director, Stephen Neal, the former chief executive of the law firm Cooley. Huang owned 3.58% of the shares as of March and, unlike the founders of Meta and Alphabet, holds no special voting rights, so he cannot block a vote. Most of his stock sits in trusts co-owned with his wife, Lori Huang — roughly 800 million shares — while he holds about 70 million outright. This is not a founder monarchy. It is a normal board with an abnormal amount of exposure to one person.

The exposure is the problem, because there is no plan behind him. Huang has run Nvidia for 33 years as co-founder, president and chief executive, with an unusually flat structure — around 60 executives report directly to him — and he has said he does not believe in succession planning. The company has retained Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz for an independent investigation, overseen by the independent directors, into whether secrets or national-security interests were compromised. It has engaged an outside search firm, and Bloomberg reported that candidates under discussion include Kress, CUDA architect Ian Buck, Arm chief executive Rene Haas and Apple’s hardware chief Johny Srouji. D.A. Davidson’s Gil Luria told Reuters that removing Huang immediately “could be challenging for Nvidia since it does not have a succession plan in place.”

The tape already voted. Nvidia fell 7% this week before the after-hours drop, on track for a sixth consecutive weekly decline, and Wednesday’s news pushed it to a 52-week low — roughly 33% below its early-October peak. Then something stranger happened: the companies that would benefit from Nvidia’s stumble rallied. AMD closed up 7.3% and added more than 4% pre-market; Broadcom rose 4% and another 2.4% pre-market, according to the Financial Times. That is not a vote against Nvidia’s business. It is a vote that a succession is a de-risking — that the demand is institutional rather than personal, and that the company is worth more without the headline risk than with it.

What makes this a market event rather than a scandal is that Nvidia is now a financial institution as much as a chip company. It has signed billions in financing agreements underwriting the buildout, and its customers have built multi-year capital plans around a road map only it can credibly promise. Removing the chief executive during a financing cycle is not a governance formality; it is a credit event until proven otherwise. That is why the search firm was engaged before the vote and why the board briefed the White House before it met. The directors are trying to make a founderless Nvidia legible to the same counterparties who priced it as a founder’s company.

Keep the caveats in view. No vote had been taken as this went to press, and every account of the board’s intentions is sourced to people familiar with the discussions. An interim appointment is not a succession, and Kress — a finance operator by background — would be a stabiliser, not a strategist. Srouji and Haas are sitting chief executives of Apple silicon and Arm respectively, which makes them expensive and slow to hire. Nvidia also faces a proposed class action accusing the company and Huang of defrauding shareholders, which gives the directors a second reason to move quickly and a second set of lawyers to satisfy. The affairs are a personal matter; the investigation is about whether they became a security matter, and those are different questions with different evidence. Watch whether any announcement comes before next week’s Trump–Xi meeting.

02 · DISCLOSURE
Accounting · The Line That Vanished

Nvidia Stopped Reporting Singapore, and That Line Was 22% of Revenue

Singapore was Nvidia’s second-largest region by billings, and it was 22% of revenue in the quarter ended July 2025 — the last quarter the company disclosed it separately, after placing Megaspeed on a blocklist. The line disappeared at exactly the moment the geography became interesting.
22% Singapore’s share of Nvidia revenue in the quarter ended July 2025 — the last quarter the company reported it separately

Nvidia stopped breaking out Singapore revenue after the quarter ended July 2025, when the city-state accounted for 22% of its revenue, according to the Wall Street Journal. The change followed the company’s decision to place Megaspeed — the Singapore-based distributor where Alice Huang worked — on a blocklist. Nvidia described the shift as a change in regional reporting standards. Whatever the intent, the effect is measurable: the single geography most associated with chip diversion stopped appearing in the accounts, and the people who were tracking it lost their line item.

The gap it hid was never subtle. Seoul Economic Daily, citing the filings, notes that Singapore was Nvidia’s second-largest region by billed revenue in fiscal 2025 at about 18% of total billings, while chips physically shipped into the country accounted for roughly 2%. Billings and end-use are different measurements, and the distance between them is where the entire export-control argument lives. A chip billed to Singapore and installed in Malaysia, or Vietnam, or anywhere with an onward route, is compliant on the invoice and not compliant in fact.

The most aggressive number came from a short seller. Culper Research estimated in May that more than 20% of Nvidia’s revenue for the year ended July 2025 — about $43.2 billion — came from China through illegal GPU diversion and Southeast Asian intermediaries. Separately, The Information reported that Alice Huang helped facilitate at least $7.5 billion in Nvidia chip shipments to China. Neither figure is audited, and neither is a finding of wrongdoing. But they are the numbers that turned a compliance question into a board question.

Megaspeed’s response has been procedural rather than combative. The company says it cooperated with U.S. inspections and takes export-control law seriously, and it has retained an American law firm to lead what it calls a “comprehensive census” of its GPUs and independently verify its compliance practices. That is the right instinct and also the tell: a distributor that needs to count its own inventory to prove where it went is describing a control environment that depended on trust.

The accounting point matters more than the scandal. For two years, investors used regional revenue disclosure as a proxy for end demand, and Singapore was the tell — a small country with a large share, sitting between the world’s biggest chip designer and the world’s biggest buyer of restricted chips. When a company reduces the granularity of a disclosure at the exact moment that disclosure becomes load-bearing, analysts do not conclude that the risk went away. They conclude that it moved somewhere they can no longer see, and they price it with a wider band. That is a permanent cost, independent of who runs the company.

Caveats. Billings are not end use, and a large share of Singapore billings is legitimately regional: cloud capacity, contract manufacturing and data-centre tenants that resell compute across Asia. Culper is a short seller and its estimate should be read as advocacy with arithmetic attached. Nvidia says it complies with export controls, and no regulator has alleged that the Singapore line was falsified. Megaspeed has not been charged and denies wrongdoing. But the disclosure gap is a fact, the blocklist is a fact, and the two happened in the same quarter. Watch whether the board restores regional detail as a confidence measure. That would cost nothing and say a great deal.

03 · POLICY
Export controls · The Seven Countries

Washington Is About to Make the Data Centre the Control Point

Commerce is finalising a rule that would add Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, Norway and South Africa to the export-control list, requiring approval for Blackwell-class chips and reporting on end users. It regulates remote access, not shipping. That is the loophole the hardware rules never closed.
7 countries the Bureau of Industry and Security wants to add to the advanced-chip export-control list, per The Information

The Commerce Department is in the final stages of a rule that would stop Chinese AI companies from reaching advanced chips through data centres in third countries, The Information reported. The department’s Bureau of Industry and Security wants to add seven countries — Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, Norway and South Africa — to the list covered by semiconductor export controls. Imports of chips at or above the level of Nvidia’s latest Blackwell GPUs would need U.S. approval, and importers would have to report the end user and intended use and accept continuing oversight. The rule could be announced as early as this month.

The timing is not subtle. Reuters reported on Thursday that Chinese AI companies are rushing to rent Nvidia-powered capacity at data centres outside China, because they expect the route to close. That is the classic shape of a control that has been pre-announced: the behaviour it is meant to prevent accelerates in the window before it takes effect, and the industry builds a stockpile of contractual access instead of hardware. You cannot seize a reservation.

Congress is moving on the same seam. Three bills — the AI Overwatch Act, the Chip Security Act and the MATCH Act — are being pushed into this year’s National Defense Authorization Act, which is the legislative vehicle that almost cannot be vetoed. Chuck Schumer called for their passage; House Select Committee on China chair John Moolenaar called for their inclusion. Eurasia Group’s David Meale told CNBC that Huang had been “the industry’s most effective lobbyist against tighter rules,” and that the scandal had weakened Nvidia’s “singular ability to slow the progress of those bills.” Trump’s weaker grip on congressional Republicans after the midterms removes the other brake.

Inside the administration the fight is more personal. Axios reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent confronted Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick over the department not adding Megaspeed to its entity list; Commerce denies it happened. The same reporting says Lutnick stalled curbs on companies suspected of diverting Nvidia chips to China, and that Trump authorised a crackdown for a third time in 2026 on November 5. On Wednesday, Senators Chris Coons and Elizabeth Warren asked Lutnick, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and chief of staff Stephen Miller to preserve all documents and communications relating to Huang and the operative he is alleged to have been involved with.

The structural change is what matters. Export controls have always governed the movement of objects across borders. A rule that requires approval for computing capacity accessed remotely governs a service, and that is a different legal animal: it needs end-use monitoring, cloud-provider cooperation and an enforcement mechanism that does not depend on a shipping manifest. If it holds, the control point moves from the port to the data centre — and every sovereign AI programme that planned to rent its way around the leading edge has to re-plan. If it does not hold, the United States has announced a rule it cannot police and taught the market exactly which jurisdictions to route through.

Caveats. Draft rules get rewritten, delayed or split; a seven-country list is a proposal, not a policy. Enforcement is the hard part, and BIS has a thin record of verifying end use inside third countries. Norway and Brazil are in the list for reasons unrelated to China routing — sovereign AI ambitions and data-centre incentives — which will invite a lobbying fight in Washington and capitals alike. Nvidia has not commented on the draft. And next week’s Trump–Xi meeting could produce a deal that supersedes the whole thing. Watch the Federal Register, not the press conference.

04 · INFRASTRUCTURE
Silicon · The Record Foundry

SMIC Grew 34%, Doubled Profit, and Ran Its Fabs at 97%

Revenue of $3.19 billion, gross margin of 29.9% against guidance of 26% to 28%, and utilisation of 97.3%. China was 90.8% of the business, and industrial and automotive demand nearly doubled its share in a year. The most sanctioned foundry in the world just posted its best quarter.
97.3% utilisation in the September quarter, up from 93.7% in the June quarter

SMIC reported third-quarter revenue of $3.19 billion on Thursday, up 33.9% from $2.38 billion a year earlier and 6.1% from the June quarter, according to the company’s filing. Gross margin came in at 29.9%, against 25.3% sequentially and 22.0% a year ago, and well above the 26% to 28% it had guided. Profit attributable to shareholders was $428.4 million, up 123.4%, beating the $418.4 million LSEG consensus. Utilisation reached 97.3%. Capacity rose to 1.117 million standard eight-inch-equivalent wafers a month and shipments rose 5.7%.

The mix is the interesting part, because it does not look like a phone recovery. Smartphones fell to 14.3% of wafer revenue from 21.5% a year earlier. Consumer electronics slipped to 40.6% from 43.4%. What grew was computing — computer and tablet to 18.3% — and industrial and automotive, which jumped to 19.3% from 11.9%. China accounted for 90.8% of revenue and the United States 7.8%. This is a foundry shifting toward the components that go into power, vehicles, networking and edge computing, which is what a market cut off from the leading edge actually needs.

The guidance is the caution flag. SMIC expects fourth-quarter revenue up 4% to 6% sequentially but gross margin back down to 26% to 28%, which tells you the 29.9% was a utilisation-and-mix peak rather than a new baseline. Capital expenditure also fell to $1.09 billion from $1.84 billion in the second quarter, in a quarter when the fabs ran at 97.3% of capacity. A foundry that is full and spending less is a foundry that cannot get the tools it wants. Depreciation is $1.27 billion a quarter against $953 million of gross profit, so the operating leverage is thinner than the margin line implies, and other operating income — largely government funding — contributed $155 million.

Put this quarter next to the policy story and the two halves explain each other. Restrictions at the leading edge push Chinese demand down into mature and trailing nodes, where SMIC is the dominant domestic supplier. That demand shows up as 97% utilisation, rising industrial and automotive mix, and a customer base that is 90.8% domestic. SMIC is not closing the gap to TSMC on the frontier. It is becoming the industrial base for a parallel stack, which is a slower and in some ways more durable kind of progress.

The number to hold onto is 97.3%, not the 123% profit growth. Profit growth off a depressed base is arithmetic. Utilisation above 97% is a physical statement: the constraint on China’s chip supply is now capacity and tool access, not demand or design. That is why capex falling is bearish rather than disciplined — it implies the bottleneck is upstream, in the equipment it cannot buy. Every export control debate in Washington is really an argument about how long that bottleneck holds, and SMIC’s income statement is the scoreboard.

Caveats. SMIC does not disclose advanced-node revenue or yields, so the part of the business that matters strategically is the part you cannot see. Government support flatters operating income and varies by quarter. Ninety-one percent China concentration is a policy exposure as much as a strength, and a demand shock in Chinese consumer electronics would hit utilisation quickly. The company guides margins down next quarter, which management would not do if it thought 29.9% was durable. And a full fab is not a profitable fab: depreciation is running ahead of gross profit. Watch the fourth-quarter utilisation line and the capex ramp, not the headline profit jump.

05 · LABOUR
Enterprise · The Half-Staff Bet

Tencent Wants Half Its People on AI-Native Business, and Denies That Means Layoffs

Pony Ma told staff the industry has entered an “Age of Discovery” and that he is willing to redeploy 50% of the workforce from the “army” to the “navy.” Chinese social media read it as a 50% cut, crashed a career app, and forced a public denial within a day.
50,000 people Ma is willing to move to AI-native work — roughly half of the 115,927 Tencent employed in June

Tencent founder Pony Ma used the company’s 28th anniversary address this week to describe an “Age of Discovery,” and he reached for a military metaphor. Tencent’s core businesses are the army; its AI efforts are the navy. The army must use AI to maximise efficiency, he said, while the company redeploys 50% of its personnel “to the navy to support AI-native businesses.” People familiar with the event told Bloomberg that the intent is a push for every employee to work with AI rather than a single sweeping reorganisation, and that Ma also called for a flatter structure with less mid-tier management.

The reaction is the story. Chinese social media read “half” as a layoff, a claim that mutated into specific numbers and, according to Chinese media, briefly crashed Maimai, the career-gossip network where tech workers trade salary and headcount rumour. Tencent’s public relations director, Zhang Jun, posted a denial on Weibo: the framing was a misunderstanding and a rumour, and the split means half the staff raising efficiency in core businesses while half explore AI-native products. A person close to the company told Chinese media the layoff claim was “completely untrue.” No filing was made, which is worth noting: the correction rests on an unnamed source and a PR post.

The scale is real even if the plan is vague. Tencent had 115,927 employees as of June, up from 111,221 a year earlier, so “100,000” is rhetorical rounding and half of the workforce is closer to 43% of actual headcount. The company has not said which units move, on what timeline, or with what budget. Its last full reorganisation was in 2018, when it created a dedicated cloud arm that is now the substrate for its AI push. It has been explicit about being behind: Ma said at a May shareholder meeting that Tencent was aboard a “leaky ship,” Yuanbao failed to displace ByteDance’s Doubao, and its model development lagged. Its enterprise assistant, WorkBuddy, has been the one clear win.

The comparison that matters is not Tencent against Tencent; it is China against the United States. Meta and Amazon have cut tens of thousands of roles explicitly in the name of AI efficiency. Tencent, at comparable scale, is describing the same adjustment as internal redeployment rather than separation. One mechanism routes the shock through the labour market and shows up as severance and utilisation; the other routes it through internal transfer and shows up as slower margin expansion and lower productivity per head during the transition. Both are the same technology shock. Only one is politically legible as a layoff, and only one generates a headline the company has to deny.

Read the metaphor carefully, because it is a strategy document written as a speech. An army defends territory and is judged on efficiency; a navy projects power into new territory and is judged on growth. Ma is telling 100,000 people that the company’s future revenue will not come from defending WeChat and games. That is the useful signal, and it does not require a single redundancy. If half of Tencent’s staff can generate new AI-native revenue, Tencent is a different company in three years. If they cannot, the reallocation is a slow-motion cost cut with better branding — and the announcement, not the structure, was the deliverable.

Caveats. There is no timeline, no unit-level allocation, no capex figure and no product target; treat any precise budget attributed to this as invented. The denial came from a PR director and an unnamed source, not a disclosure. Redeployment can be attrition by another name, and headcount will show that before any press release does. Tencent has also been here before with reorganisation announcements that produced org charts and not revenue. Watch the fourth-quarter employee count and whether Tencent begins breaking out AI-native revenue at all. A company that will not segment the business it is moving half its people into has not committed to measuring it.

Foxconn’s Profit Rose 34% and Its Chairman Said Supply Will Stay Tight
Foxconn reported September-quarter net profit of NT$77.5 billion, about $2.4 billion, up 34% year on year and above the NT$69.6 billion consensus, on revenue that rose 47% to roughly $95.4 billion. Chairman Young Liu said he expects “high double digit growth” in AI rack shipments in 2027 and warned that chips, memory and key components are tight — with demand likely to exceed supply next year even as new capacity comes online. The company is building AI-server plants in Mexico and Texas for Nvidia.

2
CoreWeave Is Trying to Swap 16% Junk for a 7% Investment-Grade Loan
CoreWeave plans to raise more than $4 billion through an investment-grade loan as soon as next week, with pricing that could land at 7% to 7.5% — roughly half the yield on its junk bonds, which recently topped 16%. This is the same company that reported 168% revenue growth and a $132.5 billion backlog while its equity fell more than 40% in a month. If the loan prices, the duration mismatch that spooked the bond market gets refinanced into something longer and cheaper. If it does not, the market has answered.

3
AWS Shipped a Tool Its Engineers Did Not Write or Review a Single Line Of
AWS released the AWS Emulator, a free local program that serves an AWS API endpoint so developers can test code without touching the cloud. It is written in Rust, supports more than 1,300 API models, and publishes “fidelity scores” for how closely each one behaves. It also does not evaluate IAM policies and behaves as a single account with no cross-region semantics. The part AWS is selling: thousands of autonomous agents wrote every line, and the team did not write or review the code.
The Register ↕
4
Google Sells Decisions by the Thousand at Ten Cents a Million Tokens
Google launched a Gemini Decisions API built on a new Gemini Decision 1 model that returns predefined answers with confidence scores — up to 1,000 yes/no or multiple-choice questions in a single call — priced at $0.10 per million input tokens. It is a different product category from chat: batched classification with a calibrated confidence number attached, sold at a price that makes it cheaper than routing the same work through a general model. The interesting question is not whether it is accurate but whether a confidence score is auditable.

5
Young People Whose Photos Grok Sexualised Are Going After xAI Directly
A growing number of young victims whose photographs were turned into sexualised images using Grok are seeking to hold xAI legally accountable for allowing users to generate them, Bloomberg reports. The cases turn on a question model providers have avoided in court: when a product generates unlawful imagery on request, is the harm the user’s conduct or the product’s design? Section 230 will be tested, and the plaintiffs are minors, which changes both the politics and the settlement math. These are allegations, not findings.

Company Watch of the Day
MEGASPEED
Every thread in this issue runs through a distributor most readers had never heard of a week ago. Megaspeed is the Singapore company where Alice Huang worked, the entity Nvidia put on a blocklist, the name Scott Bessent and Howard Lutnick allegedly argued about, and the reason a 22% revenue line stopped being disclosed. The company says it cooperated with U.S. inspections, takes export-control law seriously, and has hired an American law firm to conduct a “comprehensive census” of its GPUs and independently verify compliance. That is the correct response and also the indictment: a distributor whose defence is that it is now counting its own inventory is describing a business that grew faster than its controls. Megaspeed has not been charged with anything. But the interesting detail is structural rather than legal — a single intermediary sitting between the most valuable chipmaker on earth and its most restricted customer is exactly the kind of node that exists because the legal route is narrow and the demand is not. The scandal will end. The node will be rebuilt somewhere else, under a different name, in whichever country the next rule does not cover.
