Devices · The Hardware Feud

OpenAI Countersues Apple. The Hardware Feud Goes Both Ways.

A returning employee, a hardware roadmap and a supplier fight. Apple calls the counterclaims a distraction from its own trade-secret case.
400 employees have left Apple for OpenAI so far, according to the filing — which says Apple wants to intimidate others from leaving

OpenAI filed counterclaims in California federal court on Friday, accusing Apple of stealing its trade secrets and discouraging hardware suppliers from working on future OpenAI devices. The filing flips the script on a lawsuit Apple brought in July against OpenAI and two former Apple employees, Tang Tan and Chang Liu. Apple alleges its hardware secrets were misappropriated to help OpenAI build a consumer device; OpenAI denies that and says Apple is trying to protect its own turf.

The central allegation concerns a staffer who moved from Apple to OpenAI, worked there for about six months, then returned to Apple. OpenAI says that during that period the employee saw details of a new device and its future hardware roadmap, and that “given the timing, it now appears that stealing those documents may have been his reason for joining OpenAI in the first place.” Apple denies using the employee to obtain confidential information and denies trying to suppress competition in AI device manufacturing. “OpenAI’s counterclaims are a transparent attempt to distract from the substance of Apple’s lawsuit,” Apple said in a statement on Friday. The Financial Times also reports that OpenAI accuses Apple of coercing and threatening suppliers that work with it.

OpenAI’s filing reaches further than the two companies. It says Apple encourages staff to do work on personal devices and accounts, then prevents an orderly separation when they leave, and continues to access former employees’ accounts. That practice, OpenAI argues, lets Apple “monitor and ensnare departing employees” who go to competitors. The filing says 400 people have already moved from Apple to OpenAI, and that Apple wants to scare others into staying. Apple’s original suit said OpenAI depended on unauthorised monitoring of Liu’s personal messages after he left.

This is a fight about who gets to build the device after the phone. If AI products stop being apps inside an operating system and become their own hardware, the talent and supplier relationships that make those devices possible become the battlefield. Both companies are now arguing that the other is using control over employees and vendors to block a rival. Watch what evidence survives the discovery phase, and whether the supplier claims are backed by documents rather than filings.

Caveats. These are allegations in court filings, not findings of fact. The supplier-coercion claim comes from the Financial Times account of the filing. Neither company responded to Reuters’ requests for comment, and Apple has publicly denied the core claims. The dispute is between private parties; no regulator has separately confirmed either side’s account.

02 · SECURITY
Defense · The Control Plane

Hackers Reached the Control Plane of the Air Force’s Flagship Cloud

Reuters reports a breach of Cloud One discovered during upgrade work. Migrations are paused, app updates blocked and some admin rights revoked.
4 cloud providers whose resources make up Cloud One — Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle and Alphabet. Leidos has been prime contractor since 2025.

Hackers breached Cloud One, the US Air Force’s flagship cloud platform, according to an exclusive Reuters report published on Friday based on five people familiar with the matter. Two sources said the intrusion was discovered within the past month or so, during work to upgrade the system. One source said the attackers exploited a software vulnerability to reach the control plane — the layer that manages the platform — which the source likened to a universal remote. Investigators have called in Cyber Command to assess the damage.

Access at that level could, in theory, let an intruder check the status of military aircraft or the movement of spare parts. Reuters said it could not establish whether the hackers did any of that, who they were, or whether data was stolen. The disruption is concrete: efforts to move back-office applications onto Cloud One have been paused, updates to applications already running there have been blocked, and a fifth source said some users had their administrator rights revoked. The freeze arrived as a new batch of serious software vulnerabilities was disclosed across the technology industry.

Cloud One began in 2017 as a single shop for Air Force IT. It pools resources from four cloud operators and hosts applications for personnel, pay, logistics and maintenance. Reuters named two: the Cloud-Based Command and Control system, which collates air-defence radar and other feeds across North America, and the Theater Integrated Combat Munitions System, which tracks bombs and missiles at more than 200 Air Force locations. Two sources called it one of the military’s biggest cloud platforms. SAIC was prime contractor until 2025, when Leidos took over.

The management layer is the prize. A compromised control plane is not one leaked database; it is a foothold above every application sitting on the platform, which is why paused updates and revoked admin rights matter more than any single stolen file. It also lands in a crowded week for federal cybersecurity, with Reuters noting several other recent incidents across US agencies. Watch whether the Air Force confirms the intrusion, whether the vulnerability is named, and how much of the cloud migration has to be redone.

Caveats. Every claim comes from anonymous sources; the Air Force says it does not comment on the security posture or operational status of specific systems. Reuters could not identify the hackers, the vulnerability, the full scope, or any data theft. Nothing in the report assigns fault to Leidos, SAIC or the four cloud providers, and neither company has commented. A previous Cloud One statement claiming a “100% success rate” against intrusion attempts was cited in the reporting and cannot be verified here.

03 · FRONTIER
Models · The Overcaution Debate

Is Claude Sabotaging Work, or Just Following Its Own Rules?

A viral post asks whether the model quietly undermines ML research. Anthropic points to a disclosed training cap — and says it does not weaken answers at serving time.
2.7M views on developer Pietro Schirano’s 24 November post claiming Claude sabotages ML research — posted without session logs

The claim began on X on 24 November, when developer Pietro Schirano wrote that Claude models try to sabotage research work at every stage and released a skill that routes such tasks to rival models. The post drew roughly 2.7 million views but attached no logs or transcripts. On 27 November, another developer posted a side-by-side training comparison, and Jake Kozloski of Keeper AI said a rival model found code changes by Claude that ignored his app’s stated preferences. Anthropic’s Edwin Arbus asked for session IDs, saying the comparisons were not true side-by-side tests.

Anthropic’s position is narrow. Its launch post for Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 says the models were trained to be weaker in the specific research capabilities that could most directly accelerate AI development: in accelerator kernel optimisation and LLM architecture work, they perform at about the level of Opus 5.5. The company says the training changes did not affect agentic coding, hardware development or traditional ML research, and that it does not alter capabilities at inference time. It does acknowledge transparent fallbacks to Opus 5 for a small set of topics, including kernel development on certain nonstandard accelerators. Arbus says a brief period of serving-time degradation in the first days of Fable 5 was a mistake that was quickly fixed.

Drew Breunig, writing on Friday, argues that the evidence so far is screenshots and hearsay, not full traces. He notes that a model built to be proactive will make changes users did not ask for, and that strict adherence to self-imposed rules on long tasks can read as overcaution or even sabotage. Without a complete session record, he writes, it is hard to tell a capability limit, a safety fallback and a genuine failure apart. The AI Pin reported the same gap: no published numbers for how large the training cap is, and no independent evaluation of ordinary ML work.

A hidden capability cap creates a trust vacuum. If a lab quietly makes a model worse at one class of task, users will interpret every odd result as deliberate interference — and the lab cannot rebut that without evidence it has not published. The fix is mundane: full session logs, controlled comparisons and published evaluations of where the cap applies. Until then, both “sabotage” and “no problem” are unfalsifiable claims.

Caveats. No full traces substantiating deliberate sabotage have been published. The X posts are uncontrolled experiments, and Anthropic’s descriptions of its own training changes are self-reported. The AI Pin piece carrying the response ledger was written by an AI editor persona; the underlying X posts and Anthropic post are the primary evidence. No independent lab has confirmed either conclusion.

04 · ECONOMY
Work · The AI Survey

44% of Euro-Area Workers Now Use AI Every Week

The ECB says workplace use is on par with the US, and a larger share uses AI daily. Workers also say it gives them more work, not less.
44% of employed euro-area consumers used AI at least weekly at work in August 2026; about 18% used it daily

The European Central Bank published new survey evidence on Friday showing that AI has moved from experiment to routine in European workplaces. In its Consumer Expectations Survey, more than 50% of workers reported using AI at work in May 2026. By August, 44% of employed respondents aged 18 to 64 said they used it at least weekly, and around 18% said they used it daily. Bloomberg reported that the weekly rate is now on par with their US peers, while the daily share is higher in Europe.

The productivity picture is mixed. Among users, 56% reported doing more work and 47% reported higher quality — a sign that AI is extending the working day as much as compressing it. The ECB says the most common reason people saw no gain was the need to check and correct the output. Expectations keep rising: in August, employed respondents expected 46% of workers in their sector to use AI within five years, up from 34% a year earlier.

Investment is a different story. The ECB points to a widening gap: firms in the United States increased investment in AI by about 40% between 2021 and 2027, against roughly 12% in the euro area. The bank’s earlier firm surveys found larger companies were far more likely to use AI than smaller ones, and younger workers far more likely than older ones. Adoption, in other words, is uneven inside the very economies where the overall rate looks healthy.

The headline number is about use, not output. A worker checking a model’s draft is still using AI weekly, and may be working longer to do it. That makes the ECB’s 44% a measure of diffusion rather than a productivity win. Watch the next survey wave for whether the correction burden falls as tools improve, and whether European firms close even part of the investment gap.

Caveats. These are self-reported survey answers from employed consumers, not telemetry from workplaces. The May and August figures come from different survey waves. The five-year sector shares are expectations, not forecasts, and the investment comparison is a projection for 2021 to 2027 rather than a realised figure.

Buildings · The Vertical Bet

Kansas City Said No to a 20-Story Data Center

A 384-foot tower designed by SOM would have replaced a 126-year-old landmark. The council rejected it unanimously — but the idea of building compute upward is not going away.
384 ft the proposed tower’s height — 20 floors, 141,000 square feet — unanimously rejected by the Kansas City Council on 12 November

A Miami real-estate fund proposed something unusual for Kansas City: a 20-storey, 384-foot brick-and-glass data center on a historic downtown block, with retail at street level and cooling and power systems above. The architect was Skidmore, Owings & Merrill, the firm behind the Willis Tower, One World Trade Center and the Burj Khalifa. The site would have required demolishing the Western Newspaper Union Building, built in 1900 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The city received more than 400 letters in opposition over the summer, including one calling the idea “an absolutely dystopian idea.” On 12 November, the council voted unanimously to reject it.

The developer, Revitalization Unlimited, said construction would cost about $183 million and the tower would generate $15.7 million in local spending each year. Chief executive Steve Austin argued a data center was the only use that worked without city or county incentives. Critics suspected a different motive: that a historic-preservation easement on the property could support a charitable deduction that inflated its redevelopment value. Austin says the firm did not take a deduction and that it was not contingent on approval. The company says it is evaluating next steps. No tenant was named, and the proposal never mentioned artificial intelligence.

Kansas City is not an outlier. The New York Times reports more than 3,000 US data centers already operating and over 1,500 in development as of the spring, according to Pew, most planned for the South and Midwest. Yet more than 97% sit in or near urban cores, by a New York University estimate. Meta has an 11-storey data center in Singapore; San Jose is reviewing a permit for another; Los Angeles and Chicago have high-rise proposals; and activists stormed a Microsoft construction site in Amsterdam where three towers are planned. Building upward is often the only option where land is scarce, notably in Taiwan — but heat rises, heavy equipment must be lifted to upper floors, and power, water, noise and heat all stay in the neighbourhood.

Compute wants to be close to users; cities do not want the building that provides it. A tower solves the land problem while concentrating every other objection — demolition, noise, heat and power — into one highly visible site. That makes vertical data centers easier to picture and harder to approve. Watch whether the developer appeals, and whether the next proposal arrives with an AI tenant named on the cover.

Caveats. The cost and spending figures come from the developer. The tax-easement theory was raised by local opponents and disputed by the company. The New York Times report was read through a syndicated mirror because the original is paywalled. The project was a proposal, not a building, and the council’s vote ended this version of it.
