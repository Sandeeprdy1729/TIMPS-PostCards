Compute · The Moat Is the Compiler

Amazon Takes Its Best Shot at Nvidia’s Software Moat

AWS released TorchNeuron, a tool that moves PyTorch apps onto its Trainium chip by changing one line of configuration — and it is pushing changes upstream so PyTorch stops defaulting to Nvidia.
85% of AI apps are written with PyTorch

On October 19, AWS released a tool called TorchNeuron that lets developers move AI apps written in PyTorch onto Trainium, Amazon’s custom AI chip, by changing just one line of configuration — no rewrite required. Amazon says about 85% of AI apps are built with PyTorch, the free toolkit Facebook created and handed to the Linux Foundation in 2022. For years Nvidia ensured its CUDA software supported those PyTorch apps seamlessly, which is how its hardware became the default by embedding itself in the toolkit.

TorchNeuron is adapted from PyTorch itself, so existing knowledge carries over. Amazon is not the first to try this — PyTorch users can already port to AMD GPUs or Apple silicon with a configuration change — but it is arguing that Trainium also gives startups access to the whole AWS stack, rather than a single-purpose neocloud tied to Nvidia. Colin Brace of AWS Annapurna Labs told TechCrunch that Deepgram moved to Trainium using native PyTorch support and now processes 70% more audio per dollar at production accuracy.

The part with real teeth is upstream. Amazon says it is contributing changes to PyTorch to remove the code that ties the framework to specific chips, with the goal of making it hardware-agnostic for everyone — not just Trainium. That is a slow, structural attack on the layer that has kept developers from switching. If it works, the GPU giant’s advantage stops being the chip and starts being only the chip. “The real story is removing the lock-in that kept developers from having a real choice at all,” Brace said. The moat, it turns out, was never the silicon alone — it was the compiler in front of it.

Policy · The Open Model Meets the Procurement List

Washington Orders DeepSeek Out of Every Civilian Agency

The Department of Homeland Security moved to bar DeepSeek from all federal civilian systems and contracts — days after the company shipped its strongest model yet.
100% of civilian federal agencies barred from DeepSeek

The Department of Homeland Security posted a notice on October 19, scheduled for the Federal Register on October 20, ordering all US civilian federal agencies to remove DeepSeek products from their IT systems and bar the company from future procurement. The order, issued under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act and signed by Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, applies to DeepSeek’s parent, Hangzhou DeepSeek Artificial Intelligence, and to third-party services built on its models.

Mullin cited “multiple threat vectors”: DeepSeek stores data on servers in China, where national-security law gives the government broad power to demand it; models hosted by DeepSeek collect personally identifiable information and trace users’ digital fingerprints; and, like any prompt-and-response system, it “may result in the transfer of sensitive data” to the Chinese government. In combination with public records, he wrote, that could produce actionable intelligence on US persons and government programs. Agencies may request exceptions, but must file 60 days in advance with a “compelling justification.”

The timing is the story. The ban lands days after DeepSeek released V4.1-Flash-1017, its strongest coding model yet, and its low-cost open-weight models keep pressuring US labs on price. Some agencies and states had already restricted DeepSeek on their own; this makes it uniform across the civilian enterprise and reaches any contractor on government systems. The order was reportedly signed in the summer and is only now surfacing, which suggests the policy was waiting for a reason to be public. The open model’s advantage — that anyone can run it — is exactly what a procurement blacklist is built to cancel. The cheapest model in the world is worthless if the buyer is forbidden to procure it.

03 · MODELS
Diffusion · Speed Is the New Benchmark

Inception’s Mercury 3 Writes a Thousand Tokens a Second

A diffusion model more than 40× the size of Mercury 1 now runs at 1,068 tokens per second with a million-token context — and the vendor says it beats GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5.
1,068 tokens per second on NVIDIA GPUs

Inception released Mercury 3 on October 19, calling it the most capable diffusion-based language model yet. Instead of writing one token at a time, a diffusion LLM starts from a fully masked sequence and refines many positions in parallel over a handful of passes, so a pass can settle dozens of tokens at once. Inception says Mercury 3 generates 1,068 tokens per second on NVIDIA GPUs and has grown its context window to 1 million tokens, up from 260,000 in Mercury 2.5. It is more than 40× the parameters of Mercury 1.

The vendor’s benchmark table puts Mercury 3 at 89.23 on GPQA Diamond, 76.84 on IFBench and 51.08 on SWE-Bench Atlas — above GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5 at matched reasoning effort — while running four to eight times faster. Not every column goes its way: it trails Haiku 5.5 on DeepSWE v1 (46.31 versus 51.62) and on MRCR v2. All figures are company-reported and not yet independently reproduced, which is the right way to read a launch like this. Pricing is $0.20 per million input and $0.70 per million output tokens, half off at launch, with cached input 90% cheaper.

What Mercury 3 actually tests is whether diffusion scales, not just whether it is fast. Inception claims both: 40× the parameters without giving up speed, and intelligence that rose about 3× from Mercury 1 mini. If that holds up outside the vendor’s own harness, the speed ceiling that has shaped agent design — short steps, frequent calls, careful latency budgets — starts to move. For a year the benchmark war was about who was smartest; Mercury 3 asks whether being fast enough can be its own kind of smart. It ships on the Inception API, OpenRouter, Baseten and Vercel, OpenAI-compatible.

04 · SCIENCE
Security · Trust Nobody’s Agent

Microsoft Says Your Own Agents Are the Insider Threat

Guardian Agents watches the AI agents a company deploys, running on the premise that frontier models “cannot be trusted” — not because they are malicious, but because they are unpredictable.
60 types of agent Defender can now detect

Microsoft on October 19 introduced Guardian Agents, a feature inside its Project Perception product that monitors the AI agents a company runs. Project Perception launched in June with three agent classes — a red team that attacks, a blue team that detects, and a green team that fixes — built on the assumption that AI hackers come from outside. Guardian Agents flips that: the danger may be the agents the enterprise already deployed and pays for, behaving like a rogue employee.

The framing came from the top. In a private blog, chief executive Satya Nadella warned that model providers’ safety assurances do not relieve a company of responsibility, and that the industry must “separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it.” Microsoft security chief Hayete Gallot put it more bluntly: “Frontier models, regardless of their origin, cannot be trusted” — not because they are malicious or easily hacked, but because they are trained to complete tasks, not to follow organizational norms or judgment, and are often handed the keys to sensitive data and tools.

Alongside Guardian Agents, Defender can now detect up to 60 types of agent employees bring into work, including Claude Code, Codex and OpenClaw; it can limit what agents reach and adjust permissions dynamically, requiring human approval when an agent appears to be making a risky decision. Microsoft is not alone in this read: it was an inaugural member of Nvidia’s Open Agent Safety Platform, which isolates agents and can stop them through hardware like a kill switch. The message to enterprises is uncomfortable but coherent. You cannot outsource responsibility for what the intelligence does on your behalf — so you had better put something between the model and the keys. Trust is becoming a product category.

05 · BUSINESS
Silicon · The Challengers Get Real Money

Tenstorrent Doubles Its Valuation to $6.2 Billion

The open-weight chip challenger raised $1.5 billion, more than doubling its 2024 mark, betting that inference — not training — is where Nvidia’s grip can be pried loose.
$6.2B post-money valuation, up from $2.6B

Tenstorrent raised $1.5 billion at a $6.2 billion post-money valuation, the inference-focused chip startup announced on October 19. That more than doubles the $2.6 billion it fetched in late 2024, and comes five months after reports that Intel and Qualcomm had looked at acquiring the company at valuations up to $5 billion. The round was led by Trace Capital and Innovation X, with Cadence Ventures, Eclipse, Fidelity, SanDisk and T. Rowe Price participating.

Chief executive Jim Keller, the veteran microprocessor engineer, told Bloomberg the company chose to stay independent and raise more after showing that its Galaxy servers could run DeepSeek models. Tenstorrent designs processors and software around RISC-V, the open instruction set that lets designers build custom chips without proprietary licensing, and the new capital goes toward scaling silicon tuned for serving open-weight models and video generation.

The timing is not accidental. On the same day, Amazon shipped TorchNeuron to pull PyTorch away from Nvidia, and Washington moved against DeepSeek — two more signs that the stack is fragmenting at exactly the layer Tenstorrent sells into. Cheap, open models need cheap, open hardware to serve them, and the money is starting to follow that logic rather than the training arms race. Tenstorrent is still small next to Nvidia, and one round does not break a monopoly. But a challenger that doubles its valuation while its rivals’ customers are being told to shop around is a different kind of signal.

Nscale and Anthropic Eye a Second $45 Billion Deal
Bloomberg reports the two are close to announcing another $45 billion compute agreement, a month after their first deal of the same size. Talks are ongoing and details could change. Both are pursuing IPOs, and Nscale is reportedly meeting investor pushback over a proposed $30 billion valuation. The recurring number is becoming the story: the size of a compute commitment now signals the size of the ambition behind it.

Meta’s Muse Gave a Buyer a Seller’s Home Address
Toronto creator Matt Robb let Meta’s Muse run a Facebook Marketplace sale as an experiment. The agent accepted an offer, agreed a pickup, shared his home address and sent “Yep, I’m here!” from his account — and a stranger arrived late at night. Robb traced it to “always allow” permissions he had set himself; nothing was bypassed, which is the point. Meta acknowledged the agent made an error.

3
One Hacker, an Army of Agents, 100+ Broken Sites
Security firm NorForge found exposed control panels for Cairn, an open-source system that points teams of AI agents at a target. A Chinese-speaking cluster ran more than 1,100 attack projects across 14 panels and breached over 100 websites, including John Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins. Each successful intrusion cost roughly $50 in model usage; the worker was labeled claudecode_deepseek-v4.1-flash.
The Verge ↕
4
Huang: Two Gigawatts Is Worth $80 Billion to Nvidia
Jensen Huang said two gigawatts of AI capacity is now worth about $80 billion to Nvidia, up from roughly $18 billion per gigawatt in the past, because each gigawatt carries more, and more expensive, compute. He said 43 new gigawatts are coming in 2027. It is a reminder that the unit of AI ambition has quietly shifted from chips to electrons — and that Nvidia is repricing its business per unit of power.

5
OpenCode Breach Runs Up Users’ AI Bills
Attackers broke into an older system behind OpenCode’s online Console, affecting 1.4% of users, and used stored API keys to rack up charges on OpenAI, Anthropic and Google accounts — hundreds of dollars each, over $1,000 for a few. Anthropic and Google blocked keys; OpenAI refunded affected credits. The open-source app and CLI were not affected. A concrete lesson in why agent tooling now holds the most valuable keys in the building.

Company Watch of the Day
AMAZON
Amazon spent the day attacking the layer Nvidia actually owns. TorchNeuron is a modest-sounding developer tool with a structural goal: let PyTorch apps run on Trainium with one line of configuration, then push upstream changes that make the framework hardware-agnostic so it stops defaulting to CUDA. It is Amazon’s clearest shot yet at the software moat that has kept the GPU giant the default choice, and it fits the rest of the issue. Tenstorrent raised $1.5B to serve open-weight models; Washington blacklisted DeepSeek, the cheapest open model; Mercury 3 showed a speed-first alternative to the frontier race. Read together, the through-line is that the stack is fragmenting at the layers beneath the model — compiler, chip, procurement — and the company that controls where code runs still controls more than the company that writes the code. Amazon just made its bid for that layer.
