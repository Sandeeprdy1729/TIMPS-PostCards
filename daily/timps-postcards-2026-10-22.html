Security · The Foundation Is the Attack Surface

AI Found the Flaw in 70% of the Web. The Attackers Found It First.

A decade-old memory bug in PHP gave unauthenticated attackers remote code execution on WordPress, Drupal and Joomla. The patch arrived Tuesday evening; exploitation had begun four days earlier.
70% of websites run on PHP

Wiz Research, working with Calif and OpenAI, disclosed two critical vulnerabilities in the core of PHP on October 21: CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, with a severity score reaching CVSS 9.2. The flaws are not in an application. They are in PHP itself, the language that powers 70% of the world’s websites, including WordPress, Drupal, Joomla and Wikipedia’s MediaWiki. An unauthenticated attacker could corrupt process memory, execute code, or read the memory of other requests — where passwords, session tokens, API keys and TLS certificates sit in plaintext.

The scope is the story. Every PHP version released in the past decade is affected by at least one flaw, going back to 5.4. The PHP project shipped fixes in 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35 — which means only the maintained branches got patches. Older installations, including the long tail of appliances and hobby servers that never upgrade, remain exposed. Wiz demonstrated working pre-authentication exploits against the latest container images of WordPress, Drupal and Joomla, and noted that even a “Hello World” script on a vulnerable build is enough.

Then came the part that changes the threat model. AWS said its MadPot honeypot network logged an exploitation attempt on October 17 — four days before public disclosure — from an IP on a commercial anonymisation network. Proof-of-concept exploits appeared within hours of the embargo lifting, and researchers recorded millions of attempts as governments in Asia and Europe pulled services offline and Korea raised its cyber alert level. The technical lesson is that frontier models collapsed the cost of turning a decade-old memory-safety bug into a working exploit chain: Wiz says the work that once demanded elite researchers now takes hours. The policy lesson is less comfortable. A web that depends on one language, maintained by a small foundation and patched only on supported branches, has a single point of failure — and the attackers no longer need to be the best to use it.

02 · MODELS
Agents · The Agent Comes to You

Claude Duet Wants Your Inbox, Your Calendar and Your Permission

Anthropic’s new standalone agent runs on Opus 5.5 across 6,000 connectors, checks your accounts every few hours, and waits for approval before it sends, pays or signs.
6,000 connectors and plugins available to Duet

Anthropic opened a research preview of Claude Duet on October 21, a standalone app for iOS, Android and the web at claude.ai. It is a different posture from the Claude app: instead of waiting for a prompt, Duet sends a morning brief, watches connected apps, and messages you only when something needs attention. It can draft replies, chase unpaid invoices in QuickBooks or Stripe, prepare payroll in Gusto, post a job listing, or assemble a deck. The model underneath is Claude Opus 5.5, and the product draws on the more than 6,000 connectors and plugins in the Claude Marketplace.

The limits are explicit. Duet is available on a waitlist to Pro and Max subscribers in the US; Team and Enterprise plans are not included yet, and background checks consume the same plan limits as ordinary Claude usage. Before it sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document or deletes anything, Duet shows what it is about to do and waits for approval. Some actions are off-limits even with consent, including trading securities and moving money between accounts. Credentials follow Anthropic’s managed-agent design, so Duet never directly sees passwords or sign-in tokens — a detail that matters more now that agents are being handed the keys to real accounts.

The launch lands in a crowded week. Meta’s Muse, OpenAI’s Dots and xAI’s Grok Bot are all chasing the same always-on assistant, and each is making a different bet about how much autonomy users will tolerate. Anthropic’s pitch is judgment: knowing what can wait, and asking before the irreversible step. That is also the product’s biggest open question. The winner of the personal-agent race may not be the one that does the most, but the one users trust to act when they are not looking.

Courts · The Bill for the Data Decade

New Mexico Hands Meta a $25 Billion Bill for Cambridge Analytica

A judge found the statutory math supported $219 billion, cut it to $25 billion, and sent the money to schools. Meta says it will appeal.
$25B penalty ordered against Meta

Judge Francis Mathew ordered Meta to pay New Mexico $25 billion on October 21, after a jury found the company misled users about who could access their data and how it handled hate speech and misinformation. The jury, which returned its verdict on September 25, found 26 of 29 statements by Meta and its leadership misleading and concluded the company had committed more than 43 million violations of the state’s consumer protection law. The statute allows up to $5,000 per violation, and the money is directed to New Mexico’s school system.

Mathew wrote that the law supported a penalty above $219 billion, then reduced it to $25 billion. Meta had argued for a cap of $3.45 billion, saying the evidence showed it does not sell user data and that New Mexico had not proved any consumer was actually misled. The company plans to appeal. Attorney General Raúl Torrez called the ruling a landmark; Meta’s response is that the penalty is disproportionate and that the underlying claims were already addressed in earlier settlements.

The timing is awkward for reasons beyond the courtroom. Meta reported third-quarter results the same afternoon and said net income came in well below expectations, dragged down by a $9.9 billion settlement charge; in August it agreed to pay $17 billion to resolve a nationwide lawsuit by state attorneys general over addictive products. State AGs have become the most aggressive privacy regulators in the United States, and per-violation statutes give them a lever that federal penalties do not. The Cambridge Analytica decade is finally producing a bill — and it is being written by the states, one violation at a time.

04 · LABOR
Economy · The Efficiency Dividend

Barclays Cuts 5,000 Jobs and Calls It AI Efficiency

Britain’s bank will remove about 5% of its global workforce by the end of 2026, with 60% of the cuts in support functions — including developers.
5,000 jobs to go by the end of 2026

Barclays said on October 22 that it will cut around 5,000 jobs — roughly 5% of its global workforce — by the end of 2026, including about 1,000 already removed in the first half of the year. The bank described the programme as “organisational simplification and technology change efficiency driven by AI.” About 60% of the reductions fall in support functions, including developers, where the bank says AI makes staff more productive. It expects the measures to deliver more than £1.1 billion in savings.

The numbers arrived alongside a decent quarter. Profit attributable to shareholders rose to £1.65 billion from £1.46 billion a year earlier, equities income jumped 47%, and group income climbed 3% to £7.37 billion. Barclays also announced a £500 million buyback. Shares still edged lower in London, reflecting a weaker fixed-income trading result and two clouds analysts flagged: an expected increase in the UK bank tax in next week’s budget, and a Bank of England examination of whether Barclays understated risks in its private credit exposure.

Barclays is not the first bank to tie headcount to AI, but it is one of the largest to put a specific number and deadline on the table. The industry has spent years promising that technology investment would eventually show up in the cost base; this is what that looks like when it does. The first measurable dividend of the AI boom is not a new product. It is a smaller payroll.

Sovereignty · The Switch You Can’t Reach

Palantir Says NATO’s AI Has No Kill Switch. Europe Isn’t Reassured.

After Kyiv accused Washington of slowing Starshield, Palantir’s Europe chief insists no one can switch off Maven. The question is whether “no kill switch” is the same as control.
€480M NATO tender won by Palantir in 2025

Louis Mosley, Palantir’s executive vice president for the UK and Europe, told Reuters on October 21 that neither Palantir nor the US government can switch off the Maven Smart System used by NATO’s military headquarters and several European armed forces. “There are no kill switches,” he said in Brussels. “There’s no ability for Palantir or the US government to reach in and switch it off.” Where customers have bought the software, he said, they are in full operational control.

The reassurance was prompted by events next door. On October 13, Kyiv accused Washington of deliberately slowing Starshield, the military-grade satellite service Ukrainian forces depend on; reports later said the White House had ordered SpaceX to limit Ukraine’s access. Elon Musk said Starshield is owned and controlled by the US government and that limits are decided by the US military and ultimately the president. Donald Trump replied that “it wasn’t me, and I don’t think it was Elon.” The lack of a clear answer is precisely the problem: Europe can see that the switch exists somewhere, even if no one will say who holds it.

Palantir won the €480 million NATO tender for Maven in 2025. The system is deployed at NATO’s Allied Command Operations at SHAPE and reached “full technical operational capability” in June. France and Germany have opted out, alarmed at a private company’s role in battlefield decisions; Germany’s Vice Admiral Thomas Daum said allowing industry personnel access to national datasets is “out of the question,” and France has built its own service, Arcadia. The real kill switch is dependence: a system you cannot replace is a system someone else can always influence.

Briefs that did not make the cards — each worth an afternoon of reading.
1
China-Linked Hackers Used an AI Pentest Framework on 2,250 Microsoft 365 Tenants
Microsoft attributed the ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain attack to Storm-0940, a China-state-aligned group active since 2021. Starting as early as September, the group used ARTEX, an agentic AI penetration-testing framework, to automate scanning, password spraying and OAuth abuse. Microsoft counted more than 2,250 tenants reached at 2,900 sign-in attempts per minute, after a four-day campaign that produced 151,000 failed logons across 327 accounts at eight organisations. The AI did not make the group stealthier; it made it faster and cheaper at scale.

2
Walmart Opens a Door for Shopping Agents. Amazon Is Still Bolting It Shut.
Walmart opened a beta of its Agent Gateway, an MCP-based route that lets approved AI agents shop on a customer’s behalf without scraping the human storefront. Agent Profiles define what each agent can do; purchases require customer approval by default; agents never receive card numbers and cannot add or change payment methods; agents that break the terms are blocked. Walmart says it can surface a customer’s “usuals” without sharing their full purchase history. The contrast with Amazon, which has been blocking rival agents, is now explicit strategy.

3
Railway Tells Every Customer to Rotate Their Keys After a Breach
Developer platform Railway confirmed that intruders “may have accessed encrypted environment variables and, for some of them, obtained the decrypted contents.” Those variables are the passwords and API keys that let apps reach databases and cloud services. Railway paused customer deployments and told all users to revoke and rotate keys, even though its initial analysis suggests only a subset was affected. The company said the attack was likely part of a broader campaign aimed at platforms used by AI inference providers.

4
1Password Invents a Login for Agents That Never Sees the Password
1Password for Agents gives each agent a unique digital key. When the agent hits a login page, the request tells the user which account is wanted and why; nothing proceeds until the person approves it on their own device, at which point 1Password types the password into the page. OpenAI’s ChatGPT Work and Dots agents, Meta’s Muse and xAI’s Grok Bot are already connected; Anthropic’s Claude Duet is next. More than 200,000 businesses use 1Password, and nine more integrations are in development.

5
Hugging Face’s Carbon-X Reads DNA for 1/1,800th the Cost
Hugging Face Bio released Carbon-X, a 14.8 billion-parameter decoder trained on DNA and natural language in one token stream, with a tokenizer that reads DNA in six-base chunks. Across five DNA benchmarks it averages 66.7% with reasoning off — 27 points above its Qwen3-14B starting point and within a point of GPT-6.1 Sol at roughly 1/1,800 the inference cost. Analysing the 2.4 million variants of uncertain significance in ClinVar would cost about $300, versus $32,000 to $4 million for frontier models. It is a benchmark result, not wet-lab validation — but the cost curve is the point.

Company Watch of the Day
PALANTIR
Palantir spent the day as the test case for Europe’s dependence on American software. Its €480 million Maven contract put its AI at the centre of NATO’s battlefield decision-making, deployed at SHAPE and declaring full technical operational capability in June. Then Washington was accused of slowing Starshield for Ukraine, and Palantir’s Europe chief had to insist that no kill switch exists — a reassurance that only makes sense because everyone can now imagine the switch. France and Germany have already opted out of Maven; France built its own system, Arcadia. The through-line with today’s PHP crisis is trust in infrastructure that nobody fully owns. PHP is maintained by a small foundation and patched only on supported branches; Palantir’s Maven is maintained by a public company and ultimately answerable to one government. In both cases, the people depending on the system are discovering that control and ownership are not the same thing. The question for Europe is no longer whether it trusts Palantir. It is what it can replace, and how quickly.
