Diplomacy & Superintelligence · Two Superpowers

Trump and Xi Meet in Washington — With Superintelligence at the Center of the Table

A state dinner for a visiting president, held against an AI backdrop: Trump says "Super Intelligence will be a big topic of discussion," Xi insists AI "must always be under human control," and the world's AI chief executives sit in the same room where the next export-controls decision will be made.
2 Power Systems, One Dinner Table

Geopolitics has gone to the machine head. Donald Trump opened a two-day Washington summit with Chinese President Xi Jinping by announcing that "Super Intelligence will be a big topic of discussion" — and then adding he wants to "leave it exactly where it is," a position he attributed to Beijing as well. The summit comes as the two governments test whether over-calibrated AI competition can be traded, truce by truce.

The table is an AI who's-who. In and around the state dinner sat the CEOs of the world's leading AI labs and platforms — the executives whose export-control news cycles have become a focus of negotiations — while Xi, for his part, framed the official line: Beijing believes AI development "must always be under human control". Analysts watching the summit expect no sweeping AI architecture deal, but they are looking hard at side-effects: whether rulemaking, chip-trade terms, and a formal dialogue stay intact or get rewired.

What to watch beyond the pomp: US export controls on advanced chips are widely expected to remain in force regardless of the summit's tone, which means the practical measure of this meeting is whether the two sides build a channel — a way to deconflict AI incidents before they escalate. The "superintelligence" framing, whether real or rhetorical, has moved the most important bilateral conversation on the planet onto explicitly AI-shaped ground — and the decisions made over caviar and control charts are the ones that will shape what either country's frontier is allowed to build.

02 · WORLD
Global Governance · The UN's AI Moment

Altman and Amodei Ask the UN Security Council for Global AI Rules — and Washington Says No, for Now

In a first-of-its-kind UN Security Council meeting convened by France, the CEOs of OpenAI and Anthropic told the world's top body the industry cannot regulate itself — warning that the danger of superintelligent AI is "real and imminent" — while the US "totally rejected" the premise.
2 AI CEOs Address the Security Council

The UN Security Council — the body that decides on war and peace — has just hosted its first formal conversation about artificial intelligence. At a session convened by France, Sam Altman (OpenAI) and Dario Amodei (Anthropic) both argued the industry should not be left to police itself, telling the council the arrival of more powerful systems demands global rules.

Both executives went further than any earlier platform-positioning. Altman warned that "if AI is to be democratic, the most important decisions cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," framing governance as a question of sovereignty for every country; Amodei said that, handled poorly, "AI could be a risk to humanity as a whole," while AI pioneer Yoshua Bengio pressed that the dangers are "real and imminent". The messaging was coordinated enough to feel like the industry's genuine preferred stop: an enforceable international framework, with a seat for labs, not just states.

The counterweight was immediate and unambiguous. US officials, including chief AI adviser Michael Kratsios, told the council the US "totally rejects" global regulatory control at this stage, arguing for voluntary and national frameworks instead. The split could not be more geometric: American refusal to bind its frontier labs, European and UN pressure for governance, and two of the industry's leading founders now openly asking for the constraint that Washington refuses. Whether or not rules follow, the Security Council floor just became a permanent AI venue — and the industry's most senior figures chose the dove's seat.

03 · MEDIA
Threat Research · Malware With a Committee

CLOSEDQUORUM: The First Windows Malware Where a Committee of AIs Votes on the Next Move

Cisco Talos discloses an implant whose command-and-control is a rotating panel of four commercial LLMs — DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral, Gemini — that vote steal, inject, persist, or move before the malware acts. A 16.4 MB Go binary, and a preview of how AI-native espionage will operate.
4 LLMs Voting on Each Action

Malware has learned to think in committees. Cisco Talos disclosed CLOSEDQUORUM — what it calls the first reported Windows implant whose command-and-control is handled entirely by an AI panel: a committee of four commercial large language models — DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral, and Gemini — that vote on what the malware should do next before it does anything.

The tradecraft is the innovation. Rather than phoning a fixed C2 server (which defenders can block), the implant asks its AI committee to pick from a toolset — steal, inject, persist, or move laterally — and the panel's "silent vote" becomes the next command. Built as a 16.4 MB Go binary under Talos's CAIRN project (presumed China-aligned), the design removes the classic kill-chain's single point of failure: no hard-coded server, no command channel to seize, just a bot that consults four models and acts.

Circumstantial evidence keeps the stakes human. Researchers have not confirmed in-the-wild deployment, but the intent layers are plain — credential harvesting to enable later intrusion and cryptocurrency-wallet theft were among the candidate payloads. CLOSEDQUORUM matters beyond its own binary: it collapses the distance between "AI agents make decisions for defenders" and "AI agents make decisions for attackers," and the voting panel is the first design that is almost impossible to pin to a single command-and-control IP at all.

04 · PRODUCTS
Consumer AI · The Pocket Accessory

Meta Pivots to the Pocket: Camera-Free Audio Glasses at $349, and a Keychain AI That Holds Your Chips

Follow-up reporting on Meta's Connect reveals the strategic turn underneath the launches: Ray-Ban Meta Audio — glasses with six mics but no camera — ships October 13 for $349, while the "Muse Charm" keychain AI accessory is timed for the holidays, carrying Zuckerberg's bet that the smartphone's successor is a smarter accessory.
$349 Ray-Ban Meta Audio, Ships Oct 13

Meta's AI moment is moving off the face and into the pocket. Following its Connect keynote, Meta confirmed the Ray-Ban Meta Audio — a camera-free take on its smart glasses — will ship October 13 at $349, leaning entirely on a six-microphone array and 12-hour battery instead of a lens, and paired it with "Muse Charm," a keychain-sized AI companion with a fingerprint sensor, aimed at the December holidays.

The smaller product is the strategy. Muse — the smartphone companion app born from Meta's AI accessories push — has now seen roughly 730,000 downloads, putting it in the neighborhood of a top iOS app, a signal that users actually want a wearable AI that sits in the pocket rather than on the wrist. The Charm is Meta extending that: a device small enough to hang from a keyring that unlocks authentication and surfaces the assistant without pulling out a phone.

Why the pocket is the new battlefield: Zuckerberg has spent a year arguing the AI accessory — not the phone — is where the assistant should live, and Audio Glasses + Charm are that thesis shipped in two form factors at once. Whether "the pocket" wins against "the phone" is still an open question, but Meta just made it market-tested, at a price and weight (43 g) that make the glasses a genuinely everyday carry — and the follow-up coverage shows Connect was less a glasses launch than a pivot announcement.

05 · GEOPOLITICS
Diplomacy · The First AI Hotline

The Deal Beneath the Pomp: A US–China AI Dialogue, With an Incident Hotline at Its Core

Beside the summit optics, Treasury Secretary Bessent proposed a mechanism that could be the summit's enduring artifact: a US–China channel where either side notifies the other of an AI-related incident before it spirals — plus a trade-truce extension of roughly two months past the November deadline.
~2 mo Truce Extension Past the Nov 10 Deadline

If the summit's rhetoric was "superintelligence," the operational core was a hotline. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pressed for — and appears to have won a commitment to — a new US–China AI dialogue whose first function is an incident-notification mechanism: if an AI event turns dangerous on one side, the other side learns before it escalates.

The design is borrowed from the nuclear age, adapted to the compute age. Cold War hotlines let two adversaries deconflict an accident in minutes; Bessent's proposal extends the same logic to AI-associated alarms — hacks, rogue agents, or a model behaving beyond its guardrails that spill across borders. Even a hand-shake-sized mechanism, won as a non-treaty commitment, creates the missing thing in AI geopolitics: a sanctioned, official channel that both governments already owe each other for.

The clock, meanwhile, keeps both sides honest. Analysts see the truce extending roughly two months beyond the Nov 10 tariff deadline as the summit's realistic yield on trade — enough runway to keep negotiating, not enough to declare victory. Despite the fanfare, expectations on both sides remain deliberately low, but "low expectations" has a useful companion now: the first standing AI incident channel between the world's two AI superpowers, ready on the shelf the day a real incident needs it.
