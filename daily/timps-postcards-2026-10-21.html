Supply · The Order Book Is the Tell

China Bets $35 Billion on the One Chip It Can Still Buy

ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent ordered more than 3 million export-compliant RTX Pro 5500 cards — a book larger than Nvidia’s entire 2027 production plan for the card.
$35B in RTX Pro 5500 orders from three Chinese firms

ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent have placed orders worth more than $35 billion for over 3 million Nvidia RTX Pro 5500 workstation GPUs, according to a Reuters report published on October 20. Two sources put the price at roughly $11,400 a card. The RTX Pro 5500 is not a frontier accelerator: it carries the same number of CUDA cores as the consumer flagship RTX 5090, paired with 84GB of GDDR7 memory. It is simply the most capable Nvidia chip that US export rules still allow into China, now that the H200 and B300 remain banned or granted only case by case.

Nvidia has told customers it cannot fill the orders. Sources say the company may be able to deliver only 5% to 10% of the demand, and that it can produce just 3 million RTX Pro 5500 cards globally in 2027 — meaning this single Chinese order book would consume an entire year of world production. An Nvidia spokesman blamed a combination of “outdated” US export controls, which he said still cover gaming products released nearly half a decade ago, and China’s own limits on American imports. Neither Nvidia nor the three buyers has confirmed the figures on the record.

The important shift is what the order is for. These are not training clusters; the buyers want them for inference, where memory capacity matters more than peak throughput, and where a legally exportable card is better than an illegal one that cannot be serviced. That makes the order book a hedge against the next round of restrictions as much as a capacity plan. It also shows where the export-control line has actually landed: the question is no longer whether China can buy AI chips, but which ones the rules still permit, and whether the West can manufacture enough of them. When the legal chip is the scarce chip, the order book becomes a policy document.

Finance · The Landlord Becomes the Rival

Anthropic’s $750 Billion Tab, and Its Landlords Are Now Rivals

A leaked S-1 shows an $80.7 billion first-half loss, a $750 billion cloud ambition, and a company that needs AWS, Google and Microsoft precisely as they become competitors.
$80.7B net loss in the first half of 2026

A draft S-1 leaked to The Wall Street Journal shows Anthropic losing $80.7 billion in the first half of 2026 on $16.3 billion in revenue, with just two customers accounting for about a fifth of sales. The filing also says the company believes it could spend more than $750 billion on cloud contracts, some of which are still being negotiated. Last week Anthropic told prospective investors that third-quarter revenue alone reached $16.8 billion, up 45% from the previous quarter, and it is reportedly targeting a November IPO.

The strategic problem is written into the same document. Anthropic is struggling to secure enough capacity from AWS, Google and Microsoft because those companies increasingly see the Claude maker as a competitor rather than a customer. That is a strange kind of dependence: the three firms that supply its compute also sell competing models, and every dollar Anthropic commits to them funds the rivals it is trying to beat. The $750 billion figure should be read as a statement of intent about scale, not a signed purchase order — but the direction is unambiguous.

The loss deserves context. A figure that large is not simply cash leaving the building; it reflects accounting treatment of long-dated obligations as much as operating burn. The more revealing number is the commitment itself. Anthropic is betting that demand for intelligence will compound faster than the cost of serving it, and that it can reach public markets before the arithmetic is tested in earnest. Anthropic is no longer a startup buying cloud capacity. It is a customer large enough to be a threat to its own suppliers. The IPO will be the first time outside investors get to price that tension.

Public Sector · The Government Becomes a Customer

Google Opens Gemini to the Government

A preview for federal, state and local agencies arrives with connectors into the systems government already runs — and HHS and Missouri are already using it.
No. 3 packaged Gemini industry suite, after legal and finance

Google Public Sector unveiled Gemini for government services on October 20 at its Public Sector Summit, an enterprise-grade, purpose-built agentic product for federal, state and local agencies. It is initially available in preview, and Google says select agencies are already using it, including the State of Missouri and the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. It is the third packaged industry suite after Gemini for legal and Gemini for financial services, both launched in August.

The bundle has four parts. There are pre-built skills for government work: digesting federal and state regulations, running acquisition and procurement, and evaluating vendor qualifications. There are connectors into Google Workspace, Microsoft SharePoint, Outlook 365, Salesforce, ServiceNow, the Federal Register and regulations.gov via BLEN, and Oracle Fusion. There is a partner ecosystem of third-party agents and integrators including Accenture Federal Services, Deloitte, SAIC, Slalom, Clutch and Onix. And underneath sits the governed Gemini platform, with inherited identity and access controls, audit logging and a control plane for IT and risk teams.

The pitch is grounded retrieval: answers pulled from official regulations and the agency’s own files, linked back to the exact page and paragraph, so a civil servant can check the source before acting. Google says agency data, prompts and outputs stay inside the organization’s cloud perimeter, and base models are never trained on customer data without consent. “To deploy agentic capabilities in the public sector requires non-negotiable standards for security, confidentiality, and data sovereignty,” said Google Cloud chief executive Thomas Kurian. The agent race is moving from benchmarks to purchase orders — and government is the buyer every vendor wants to name first.

04 · SECURITY
Supply Chain · The Install Path Is the Perimeter

GitHub Puts a Firewall in the Install Path

The new package firewall sits between coding agents and the npm registry, inspecting packages before they run — which is exactly where credentials were being stolen.
npm the only ecosystem supported at launch

GitHub launched a package firewall in public preview on October 20 for all GitHub Enterprise Cloud customers, including data-resident deployments. The firewall sits between endpoints — Actions runners, developer workstations and coding agents — and the upstream package registries. It inspects both metadata requests and the actual package downloads against an organization’s policy, and blocks anything that looks malicious before the package is installed.

The placement is the point. GitHub notes that a growing number of malware attacks exploit systems at installation time, harvesting credentials on CI/CD hosts or developer machines without ever running the package’s code. Scanning source after it lands misses that entire class of attack. Administrators configure endpoints to route registry traffic through the firewall; if a package is blocked in error, users can request an exception through a workflow similar to other GitHub Advanced Security features. The service is free during the preview and will be charged on metered usage at general availability, similar to Actions billing.

The limitation is scope: at launch the firewall only supports npm, the JavaScript ecosystem that is also the most frequent target of supply-chain attacks. That is a sensible place to start and a reminder of how much of the modern software supply chain runs through a handful of registries. It also reframes the agent boom: an AI coding agent that installs dependencies on its own is a privileged endpoint with credentials, not a text generator. Security spent a decade scanning code after it was written. The new front is the moment before it exists.

The AI That Runs the Skies Raises $325 Million

ASI, the Boston company behind an $875 million FAA air-traffic contract, is now worth $3 billion — and it is pushing forecasting into grids, logistics and the military.
$875M 12-year FAA air-traffic contract won in June

ASI, the Boston company formerly known as Air Space Intelligence, raised $325 million in a Series C that values it at $3 billion, it announced on October 20. Spark Capital led the round, with affiliates of Citadel Securities, Meritech Capital, Craft Ventures, Energy Impact Partners and Andreessen Horowitz participating. The company’s software models air traffic, power grids and military operations hours, days and weeks ahead, so operators can act before a problem spreads.

The anchor customer is the Federal Aviation Administration, which awarded ASI an $875 million, 12-year contract in June to manage US air traffic with AI. ASI beat Palantir and Thales for the work. The system combines weather, flight paths and controller staffing to predict congestion and suggest ways to cut delays; the FAA began using it in September around Washington and plans to expand it nationwide. The US Army added a five-year, $99 million contract in October for next-generation command and control, and the company says it supported more than 4,000 Air Force operators during Operation Epic Fury.

The new money goes into energy, logistics and military field operations, plus research and engineering hiring in Boston. What makes ASI interesting is not the model but the contract structure: multi-year, mission-critical, and expensive to rip out once an agency’s workflows are built around it. That is a different business from selling tokens. The most valuable AI companies of the next phase may not write text at all. They will schedule the things that move.

Briefs that did not make the cards — each worth an afternoon of reading.
1
SpaceX and Tesla Form Committees to Study a Merger
The boards of SpaceX and Tesla have each formed special committees of independent directors to review a possible merger, created at shareholders’ request. Elon Musk, the largest individual shareholder of both, says he is a “conflicted party” and cannot join the discussions, though he supports the idea and cautions it is not certain. SpaceX’s IPO left him with more than 50% voting power; Tesla would need approval from 50% of all shareholders. He framed the logic around AI software, robotics, orbital data centers and a joint chip fab.

2
OpenAI Buys Orchestra Bio for Its Biotech Push
OpenAI acquired Orchestra Bio, a four-year-old startup whose AI agents handle the operational minutiae of drug discovery — tracking finances, coordinating clinical-trial scheduling and the like. Terms were not disclosed; the company had reportedly raised about $20 million from Andreessen Horowitz, Atria and 8VC. The team and technology will be folded into Rosalind, OpenAI’s life-sciences model, and the Rosalind Workbench tool. It is the lab-as-software-company pattern again: buy the workflow, not just the model.

3
Meta’s Muse Hits 10 Million US Downloads in 40 Days
Meta’s AI companion Muse has been downloaded 10.9 million times since its September debut, 10.2 million of them in the United States, according to Sensor Tower. Only 11 other apps have ever reached 10 million US downloads in their first 40 days; the last was Threads in 2023. For comparison, ChatGPT drew 3.7 million US downloads in the same window, Grok 500,000 and Claude 200,000. Meta put 39% of its October ad spend behind Muse, up from 23% in September.

Reliable Robotics Raises $115 Million to Automate Flight
Reliable Robotics, founded by former SpaceX engineers, added $115 million — $90 million in equity led by Nimble Partners and $25 million in debt from Silicon Valley Bank — extending its Series D to $275 million and pushing its valuation past $1 billion. The Mountain View company installs autonomous flight systems on existing aircraft and is working through four FAA certifications. Chief executive Robert Rose expects the first, a continuous autopilot that handles taxi, takeoff and landing, in 18 to 24 months.

5
VideoGen Promises 10-Minute Films From One Prompt
VideoGen unveiled Model 1, its first video-generation model, which it says turns a single text prompt or image into multi-shot films up to 10 minutes long. A built-in planning layer writes the screenplay, dialogue and shot list; the model accepts up to 16 reference images and runs joint audio-video attention to keep characters and locations consistent across shots. A limited partner rollout began October 20 alongside $1 million in free credits. Final pricing, benchmarks and third-party validation are all still pending — treat the 50%-cheaper claim as a claim.

Company Watch of the Day
NVIDIA
Nvidia spent the day at the centre of every story without announcing anything. Chinese buyers placed $35 billion in orders for the one card it can legally sell them, a book Nvidia says it can fill only 5% to 10% of — against a 2027 production plan of 3 million units worldwide. At the same time, Anthropic disclosed a $750 billion cloud ambition and an $80.7 billion first-half loss, a reminder that the largest AI labs are now enormous purchasers of the compute Nvidia sells. ASI raised $325 million to put AI in charge of air traffic and grids; Google packaged Gemini for government buyers; GitHub moved to guard the npm install path that feeds every agent. Read together, the through-line is scarcity at the physical layer. The model layer gets the headlines, but the constraint — fabs, memory, power, and the licence to ship — still sets the pace. Nvidia’s problem is no longer whether demand exists. It is whether the world can build enough of what demand is allowed to buy.
