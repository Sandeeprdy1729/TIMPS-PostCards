Privacy & Regulation · The Consent Layer

Italy Fined Meta €805 Million for Reading Android Photo Galleries Without Asking Properly

The Garante says one app-wide permission let Facebook scan photos, videos and metadata on roughly 20 million Italian Android phones, and the feature was on by default. Meta must switch it off within 60 days and ask again, separately. The regulator is still deciding whether to open a file on AI.
€805M about $887 million, or 0.47% of Meta’s global revenue — the largest fine the Garante has imposed on a tech company

Italy’s data protection authority, the Garante, fined Meta Platforms Ireland Limited €805 million on Saturday over a Facebook feature called “Get suggestions from gallery when browsing Facebook.” Available since 2023, it showed users photos and videos pulled from their phone’s camera roll as prompts for posts. The regulator found Meta accessed those files and their metadata without specific consent and without explaining what it was doing, in breach of Article 122 of Italy’s privacy code, which implements the EU’s ePrivacy Directive.

The mechanics are the whole case, and they will be familiar to anyone who has tapped through an Android permission dialog. Access to the gallery was requested through a single authorisation valid for the entire app and several unrelated features. The screen before it carried a generic notice — “enable other features” — that did not tell the user they were also switching on gallery suggestions. And the feature itself was pre-set to active. Grant that permission once and the app can read the phone’s entire photo archive, in practice permanently, while neither the user nor the regulator can tell which files were actually used.

The investigation started in July 2025 after a complaint from the Bolzano Consumer Protection Centre and relied on the authority’s own technical analysis. The fine reflects four aggravating factors: roughly 20 million Italian Android users affected; a violation still ongoing for people who never updated the app or who granted the permission; a previous 2019 decision against the company; and what the Garante called “limited cooperation” during the inquiry. Late fixes made after the complaint counted only as partial mitigation. Meta has 60 days to seek consent separately and specifically, and to keep the feature off by default even for users who already agreed. It must report its initiatives in 30 days and its measures in 90. After that, the feature cannot be switched on for anyone without valid consent.

The jurisdictional fight matters more than the money. Meta argued the Irish authority should handle it under the GDPR’s one-stop-shop, the mechanism that normally lets a company answer to a single EU regulator. The Garante rejected that, on the grounds that ePrivacy rules do not provide a one-stop shop. If that reading holds on appeal, every national regulator in Europe regains a lever over device-level data collection, which is exactly where the consent problem lives. It is the same argument that has made Ireland’s outsized role in policing US platforms a live political question for a decade.

The AI question is the one everyone will ask and the one this decision deliberately did not answer. The Garante said explicitly that the proceedings did not cover whether the gallery content was used to train artificial intelligence, a practice Meta denies. But the authority reserved the right to open a separate file on a new feature launched in 2026 that lets gallery content be processed in the cloud. That is the real target. A local gallery scan is an awkward product feature; a cloud pipeline that moves a person’s entire camera roll through a model-training stack is a different kind of business. Every regulator in Europe is now looking for the boundary between “a feature that helps you post” and “a dataset that teaches a model,” and the honest answer is that the boundary is not in the product description. It is in the logs nobody outside the company can read.

Caveats. Meta will appeal and disputes the facts, the law and the size of the penalty; a fine is not a final finding until the appeals run out. 0.47% of global revenue is a rounding error against Meta’s earnings, so the deterrent value is almost entirely reputational and procedural. The 20 million user figure is the regulator’s estimate. The ePrivacy jurisdiction argument is genuinely contested and could be narrowed. And the most important limitation is the one stated in the decision: this case says nothing about AI training. Watch three things — whether the appeal survives on jurisdiction, what the Garante does with the 2026 cloud-processing feature, and whether other national authorities copy the template now that the one-stop shop has been challenged.

Data Centre Finance · The Grid as Collateral

Applied Digital Wants Oracle and Meta to Guarantee Billions Before It Will Build Them a Campus

Bloomberg reports the developer has demanded multi-billion-dollar parent guarantees from investment-grade tenants since at least last year, before signing AI leases. It also wants Nvidia and AMD to backstop utility interconnections. The tenant now underwrites the landlord.
70 / 30 the tenant mix Applied Digital says it prefers: investment-grade to non-investment-grade

Applied Digital has been asking its largest AI tenants for multi-billion-dollar parent guarantees before it will sign new data-centre leases, according to Bloomberg. The practice has been in place since at least last year and targets investment-grade counterparties such as Oracle and Meta. The reason is physical. “With most new sites, you have to build the power and transmission lines yourself,” president Jason Zhang said. A guarantee is what turns a company’s credit rating into a place in the interconnection queue.

The structure is a chain of risk transfer. A utility no longer wants to fund substations and gas plants on the assumption that a large customer will eventually show up; it wants a parent guarantee covering the infrastructure tied to the load. An independent developer signing that guarantee may then require its own tenants to backstop it, because the liability dwarfs its balance sheet. Applied is also seeking guarantees case by case from Nvidia and AMD to support utility interconnections. It has set an upper limit on how much capacity it will rent to non-investment-grade neoclouds such as CoreWeave, and chief executive Wes Cummins said he prefers a mix of roughly 70% investment-grade to 30% non-investment-grade. Meta, Nvidia and AMD declined to comment. Oracle did not comment. CoreWeave said the partnership is strong and it wants to expand it.

The context is not one developer’s caution. Oracle is already carrying a $7 billion letter-of-credit obligation in Wisconsin after the local utility raised its credit threshold to A- at S&P and A3 at Moody’s. Grid Strategies estimates data centres could pay $35 billion more in transmission charges over the next decade. More than half of US states have moved, or considered moving, to make data centres carry more of the grid’s costs. And the November election made this sharper: voters angry about household electricity bills backed Democrats from Ohio to Texas who promised to shield homes from data-centre demand. Bloomberg ties that political shift directly to the new financial requirements landing on Oracle and Amazon.

The Louisiana file shows what the model looks like when it is written down. A November 9 meeting packet for the England Economic and Industrial Development District proposes up to $4.6 billion of revenue bonds to fund Delta Forge 1, a 300 MW campus in Boyce, with repayment coming from Applied’s Meta lease — a 15-year agreement reported at about $7.5 billion, or roughly $500 million a year against Applied’s $611 million of revenue in all of fiscal 2026. There is also a property-tax arrangement of about $575 million through 2052. Louisiana taxpayers are not on the hook in the structure as drafted; the Meta lease is. The bonds have not been sold, and there is no rate, maturity or final approval yet.

Read the direction of the leverage, because it has inverted. For two years the story was that hyperscalers held all the power and neoclouds competed for their contracts. Now the scarce input is a grid connection, and the party that can get one is the party that can pledge an investment-grade balance sheet against it. That is why a landlord can demand billions from Meta, and why Meta’s own numbers explain the concession: it guides 2026 capital expenditure, including finance-lease principal, to $135–$145 billion, and at September 30 it had $355.7 billion of signed leases that had not yet started. Every one of those needs a landlord who can raise money. Borrowing against Meta’s rent is far cheaper than the neocloud debt market, where a cost of capital near 15% makes projects uneconomic. The guarantee is not a sign of weakness on either side. It is the price of speed.

Caveats. The specific guarantee amounts have not been disclosed, and the reporting rests on a person familiar with the arrangements. Applied’s $5.1 billion of debt in fiscal 2026 and its near-quintupling of diluted shares since fiscal 2022 mean it needs cheaper capital regardless of who signs. The Louisiana bonds are a proposal, not a financing, and a single-tenant campus concentrates risk on one lease and one build timeline. Utility credit thresholds are set per jurisdiction and can change again. And a parent guarantee moves risk onto a balance sheet without removing it from the system — if the tenant walks, the developer, the bondholder and eventually the ratepayer find out what the guarantee was really worth. Watch whether Oracle, which normally guards its off-balance-sheet commitments, actually signs multi-billion-dollar backstops, and whether state regulators start treating guarantees as a condition of service.

03 · MONEY
Public Markets · The Maturity Wall

Trump Media Has Until November 30 to Find $1 Billion, and Most of Its Bitcoin Is Already Spoken For

The $1 billion of 0% convertible notes sold to fund a bitcoin treasury can be put back for cash on November 30. They convert at $34.72; the stock trades near $4.60. About 73% of the bitcoin is pledged to an options programme whose counterparty can reuse the coins.
$34.72 the notes’ conversion price, against a share price near $4.60 — roughly seven times out of the money

In May 2025, Trump Media & Technology Group sold $1.0 billion of 0% senior secured convertible notes due May 2028 and used the money to build a bitcoin treasury. The notes carry a conversion price of $34.72 a share. The stock now trades near $4.60, down more than 60% this year, and the notes can be put back to the company for cash at full principal on November 30, 2026. If every holder exercises, the bill is about $1 billion. Nobody converts a bond into stock at seven times the market price; they ask for the money.

The balance sheet at September 30 is the problem in one table. Trump Media held $182.3 million in cash, $31 million in restricted cash and $211.1 million in short-term investments — about $425 million of liquid assets against a $1 billion obligation. Total financial assets were roughly $2.3 billion, of which about $1.7 billion sat in bitcoin and bitcoin-related holdings. The company’s own quarterly report says it may need to sell bitcoin or other digital assets, or raise new debt, and that additional financing may not be available on acceptable terms. It has ruled out selling shares.

Then comes the detail that makes the treasury less available than it looks. Of 15,649 bitcoin held at quarter-end, 11,385.20 — about 73% — were pledged as collateral to a bitcoin yield management programme, with a fair value of $951.4 million. The filing states the counterparty can rehypothecate those coins at its sole discretion, and that Trump Media’s ability to sell or redeploy them is restricted while the trades stay open. A further 4,260.73 bitcoin, worth $356.1 million, is locked as collateral for the notes themselves until the indenture’s conditions are met. Together the two buckets cover nearly the entire stack. The company also holds 7 million shares of BlackRock’s IBIT and about $50.3 million of Cronos tokens.

Interim chief executive Kevin McGurn has said the plan is a “mix of cash and credit” rather than new equity, and that the company wants to keep as much bitcoin as possible to continue the yield strategy and let the asset recover. General counsel Scott Glabe said notices have gone out and holders have until near the end of the month to respond, so the company cannot yet size what it will owe. That uncertainty cuts both ways: a partial exercise is manageable, a full one is not, and holders have every incentive to wait until the last days to see whether a refinancing appears. The Financial Times frames the deadline as a choice between selling assets and taking on expensive new debt.

The business underneath makes the refinancing harder. Third-quarter revenue was $3.0 million, up 205% year on year, against an adjusted EBITDA loss of $12.3 million. The quarter’s $409.7 million profit came from marks on crypto and equity holdings, not from Truth Social. Similarweb data put website traffic down 22% in October from the month before. Bitcoin itself has fallen from about $87,300 in October to roughly $66,000. And the politics have turned: Democrats took the House and the Senate, which makes investigations into the company’s businesses — including a service that gives high-frequency traders earlier access to the president’s posts — more likely, not less. A treasury company is a bet that the asset outruns the cost of the debt that bought it. When the asset falls and the debt comes due early, the structure stops being a treasury and becomes a forced seller.

Caveats. The company has not disclosed how much will actually be put back, and holders may not all exercise. A refinancing could close before November 30 and the filing explicitly contemplates a new credit facility. The 15,649 coin count and pledged amounts are as of September 30, and both the bitcoin price and the share price have moved since. Reports that wallets linked to the note collateral moved roughly 4,259 bitcoin to an exchange in early November describe a transfer, not necessarily a sale, and the company has previously described such moves as custody or trading management. The account is also worth reading with the source in mind: the FT deadline reporting is solid, while several aggregator pieces in circulation have mixed Trump Media’s figures with MARA’s separate bitcoin sales. Watch the notice period, whether a credit facility is announced, and the first disclosure after November 30.

One Router Took Down the Sky: The FAA’s Ground Stop Came From a Copper-to-Fiber Swap

Transport Secretary Sean Duffy said a contractor replacing copper line at a remote site left a router flooding the network with 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. Disruption reached 17 of the FAA’s 22 air traffic centres. Officials say it was not cyber, and not AI.
1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds from the affected router, per the FAA’s acting CTO

The November 13 nationwide ground stop — the first of its kind since September 11, 2001 — began with routine maintenance at a remote FAA site. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said a contractor was migrating telecommunications circuits from copper wire to fiber, moving from TDM to IP, analog to digital. The contractor was L3Harris, which has worked for the FAA for more than 20 years and, in Duffy’s words, actually owns the network the agency uses to control the airspace.

The trigger was mundane. The copper line was so old the carrier was discontinuing it, so the switch was not optional. During the work, a newly installed router began flooding other routers in the air traffic control system — more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, according to acting FAA chief technology officer Rebecca Guy. That degraded communication with aircraft and disrupted radar feeds. The FAA disabled the two sites it saw pinging the network at high rate and stabilised, but by the time departures were halted, officials had detected disruption at 17 of the agency’s 22 centres that manage aircraft in flight. Officials told Reuters the two problem locations may have been in Arkansas and Texas.

The safety call was correct and expensive. The Department of Transportation says controllers and pilots grounded more than 3,500 flights during the stop and that over 8,000 operations resumed the following night; other tallies put the cancellations near 10,000 with more than 7,000 delays, and the disruption is expected to ripple for days. Duffy was explicit that the cause is not yet established: “What we’re working to determine was whether we had incompetence — meaning, was it user error or install error from L3Harris? Or did they install a faulty router?” Both the secretary and FAA administrator Bryan Bedford said there is no evidence of a cyber attack. Bedford added: “We see zero evidence of anything nefarious, and zero evidence of anything AI related.”

The modernisation arithmetic is where this stops being an accident story. Bedford said the FAA spends about $1 billion a year sustaining its 1980s TDM telephone network and is constantly replacing 2,000 Cisco 600 routers with Juniper routers — ordinary maintenance, he stressed, not part of the Peraton contract the agency values at $12.5 billion. Duffy listed what the money has bought so far: 69% of copper communications replaced with fiber, surface-awareness displays at 143 towers, 469 radio sites converted, 25 towers on electronic flight strips instead of paper, 224 IP voice switches, 18 tower simulators and 9 surface movement radars. L3Harris said it has completed more than 70% of the planned airspace communications conversion and is supporting the investigation.

A system that can be grounded by one misbehaving router is not fragile because the router was cheap. It is fragile because a forty-year-old architecture has a broadcast-style failure mode and a single vendor owns the pipes. The honest lesson is not “test your changes” — the FAA does, and this was a routine swap performed at a remote site precisely because remote sites are supposed to be low-consequence. The lesson is that the blast radius of a maintenance window is a property of the network topology, not of the change. In a hub-and-spoke telecom estate where a stateful routing protocol propagates reachability, a router that starts advertising aggressively is not a local fault; it is a rumour that spreads faster than anyone can isolate it. Duffy’s own summary of the fix is the giveaway: he is “stuck with L3Harris, who owns this network,” until a new architecture from AT&T, Verizon and Lumen exists. Until then, every copper-to-fiber cut is a small bet on a very old system — and the airline passenger pays when the bet loses.

Caveats. The root cause is not determined; installer error, configuration error and faulty hardware are all still open, and L3Harris has not conceded fault. The Arkansas and Texas locations are as officials described them to Reuters, not confirmed in the department’s written statement. Flight totals differ by source and definition — grounded versus cancelled versus delayed — and the 3,500 figure comes from the DOT while ~10,000 cancellations comes from other reporting. Duffy and Bedford disagree in emphasis about whether this counts as modernisation; Bedford says it does not, and that framing is worth remembering when the next outage is blamed on ageing equipment. Watch the root-cause report, whether the DOT changes when it schedules cutovers, and whether the $12.5 billion architecture programme survives contact with a Congress that just watched the airspace close.

Agent Infrastructure · The Neutral Layer

LangChain Raised $300 Million at a $3 Billion Valuation to Be the Switzerland of AI Agents

CapitalG led the round, 13 months after a $1.25 billion valuation. LangSmith is used by more than 7,000 organisations, and the company says its open-source projects are downloaded 350 million times a month. The pitch is a model-neutral runtime for agents after they ship.
$3B post-money valuation, up from $1.25 billion in October 2025; total funding now $460 million

LangChain raised $300 million in a Series C at a $3 billion valuation, led by CapitalG, Alphabet’s independent growth fund. The round included GIC, DFJ Growth, IVP, Sapphire Ventures, Salesforce Ventures, Insight Venture Partners, QuantumLight, MongoDB Ventures, Sequoia, Amplify and Benchmark. The company last raised at $1.25 billion in October 2025, so the price has more than doubled in about 13 months.

The product that is actually being bought is LangSmith, a framework-agnostic platform to run, evaluate, monitor and govern agents. The company says more than 7,000 organisations use it, from startups such as Harvey and Rippling to Nvidia, Toyota and Salesforce, and that Workday cut the time to deploy a new agent from five months to under a day. Its open-source projects are downloaded more than 350 million times a month. The money is earmarked for three layers: the runtime (LangGraph, Deep Agents, LangSmith Agent Server and sandboxes); improvement (observability, evals, and an in-platform agent called LangSmith Engine that proposes code and prompt fixes); and governance through model and tool gateways.

That list is a strategic argument disguised as a product roadmap. A year ago most enterprises treated agents as “promising science projects,” as CapitalG partners Mo Jomaa and Alice Yang put it. Now agents handle customer support, back-office work and software engineering at very large companies, and the hard problems have moved from “can it act” to “can we see what it did, prove it was allowed to, and reproduce it when it goes wrong.” Observability and evals sound like plumbing until an agent sends the wrong email to a customer; then they are the only thing between an incident and a lawsuit. LangChain’s bet is that this layer is bought separately from the model, and that buyers will pay to keep it that way.

Which is why the phrase “model-neutral” is doing so much work. CapitalG’s thesis, as the firm wrote it, is that enterprises want an agent layer they control, rather than one supplied by the model labs or the hyperscalers. That is a direct challenge to OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Amazon, each of which would prefer that the runtime, the evals and the gateway be theirs. It also explains the investor list: a fund that belongs to Alphabet is paying for neutrality in a category where Alphabet is a competitor, because distribution through the neutral layer is worth more than exclusivity. New LangSmith features are due at a release event on November 20, and the company is listed at AWS re:Invent on November 30 — the conference run by one of the hyperscalers building rival agent tooling.

The defensibility question is the same one every developer-tools company faces at a $3 billion price: the open-source project is not the moat, and the model vendors keep absorbing features. What LangChain actually owns is the record — traces of what agents did, eval suites, prompt versions, gateway policies. That is sticky in the unglamorous way that billing systems are sticky: nobody wants to migrate it, and the data gets more valuable every month. The risk is equally clear. If a lab ships first-party tracing that is good enough and free with the subscription, the neutral layer becomes a premium option rather than a default. And if enterprises decide that governance belongs inside the cloud account they already trust, the buyer for a separate control plane shrinks. LangChain is not being valued on downloads. It is being valued on the assumption that the boring layer stays separate.

Caveats. Almost every operating metric here is company-reported: the 7,000 organisations, the 350 million monthly downloads, the Workday example and the valuation all trace back to LangChain or its investors, with limited independent verification. Open-source downloads are not revenue and not users. “Neutral” is a positioning, not a contractual guarantee — gateway products can favour models without anyone noticing. CapitalG’s participation raises an obvious conflict question that the company will have to answer in enterprise sales. And the competitive set is the best-funded in software. Watch three things: whether LangSmith pricing survives contact with free first-party tooling, whether the governance gateway becomes a real budget line rather than a feature, and whether the November 20 release is a product or a repackaging.

Five of the Six Biggest AI Labs Now Buy Their Market Data From a 39-Person Company
Databento raised $100 million in a Series C at a $1.1 billion valuation, led by Fifth Down Capital with Fitch Ventures as a strategic investor, four months after a $97 million Series B. The Salt Lake City company delivers tick-level futures, options, equities and index data through one API, says revenue has more than doubled and that it is profitable with 39 employees. It also says it has enterprise contracts with five of the six largest frontier AI labs. The read-through: labs are no longer just buying compute. They are buying clean, timestamped, machine-readable reality, and the vendors that own the ingestion path get paid twice — once by traders, once by models.

2
OpenAI Gave Its Agents a Wallet, and It Still Asks You Every Time
OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet with American Express, Mastercard and Visa, letting its “dots” agents complete purchases for ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the US. Every purchase needs a biometric check, the network issues a per-transaction payment token, and neither the agent nor the merchant sees the card number. OpenAI says it is not taking a cut to start, and that it expects users to eventually delegate purchases within preset spending limits and categories. The design choice is the story: the first mass-market agent payment rail ships with a human in the loop for every transaction. Autonomy is being rationed, not granted.

3
Latin America Is Quietly Becoming a Dollar Economy, One Stablecoin at a Time
USD-pegged stablecoins are taking a growing share of Latin America’s crypto activity, according to Bloomberg, functioning both as a store of value and as a cheap cross-border payment system — including for users who do not know or care that a blockchain is involved. The pattern is familiar from every high-inflation economy: people adopt the unit of account that holds its value, and the rails follow the unit of account. What is new is that the rails are now programmable, which makes them the natural settlement layer for agents that need to pay across borders without a bank account.

The Open-Model Movement Just Lost Its Patron
Nathan Lambert argues in Interconnects that Jensen Huang’s exit from Nvidia is a setback for US open models, because his funding gave open-source developers time to find their footing and will now “very likely dry up.” He calls it “a decapitation” and says it is time to rebuild. The point is structural rather than personal: American open weights have depended on a small number of wealthy patrons whose strategic interest was commoditising the layer above their hardware. Remove the patron and the field does not disappear, but its runway shortens sharply — which is exactly when Chinese open releases tend to gain share.

llama.cpp Reached 1.0, Which Means Local AI Finally Has a Stability Promise
Three and a half years after it began as a MacBook experiment, Georgi Gerganov’s llama.cpp shipped v1.0.0 alongside ggml, with semantic versioning for the public C API: breaking changes now require a major bump. The release itself is breaking — 35 deprecated functions removed from llama.h — and adds FP8 KV caches, a host-memory cache that lets large mixture-of-experts models offload some work to the CPU, and support for more than 150 architecture variants. The version number is the least interesting part. The promise is what lets someone ship a local model inside a real product instead of pinning to a commit hash forever.

Company Watch of the Day
META
Today’s issue is mostly about who carries the risk when something is assumed to be fine. Meta appears twice, from opposite directions. In Italy it paid €805 million — about 0.47% of global revenue — because a permission dialog was too broad, a notice was too vague, and a gallery feature was on by default for roughly 20 million Android users. In Louisiana it is the counterparty whose ~$7.5 billion, 15-year lease is expected to repay up to $4.6 billion of proposed municipal bonds for Applied Digital’s 300 MW campus, which is the same trade in reverse: a balance sheet so strong that other people borrow against it. Both stories come down to the same question the rest of the issue keeps asking. Trump Media has until November 30 to find $1 billion because it assumed bitcoin would outrun its debt. The FAA assumed a routine copper-to-fiber cut at a remote site was low-consequence, and one router grounded the airspace. LangChain is selling the layer that lets an enterprise prove what its agents did after the fact. The Garante’s most consequential line is the one nobody quoted: the case does not cover AI training, but a separate investigation is reserved for the 2026 feature that processes gallery content in the cloud. That is where consent stops being a dialog box and becomes a data-supply question — and where Meta’s two appearances meet. Watch the appeal on jurisdiction, the Louisiana bond approval, and whether any regulator follows Italy past the one-stop shop.
