Governance · Ownership Is the New Mission

Anthropic Pledges 5% of Itself to the Public Good

The lab is committing 5% of its shares to a new Public Benefit Fund that grows as the company does — and says roughly 20% of Anthropic is now pledged to the public, on the eve of an IPO as soon as November.
20% of Anthropic shares pledged to the public good

Anthropic said on October 18 that it will commit 5% of its shares to a new entity, the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund. Subject to stockholder approval, the board can add 2% for every 50% rise in the company’s value above a set baseline — so the fund’s stake grows as the company grows, rather than being fixed on the day it is written. Anthropic chose a wholly-owned LLC over a 501(c)(3) so it can move between forms of giving, from large donations to traditional nonprofits to direct distributions to specific populations, and it says the equity will be barred from lobbying or political giving anywhere.

The headline number is the one Anthropic leads with: because its co-founders pledged at founding to give away 80% of their equity, and about half of employees take part in a program where Anthropic matches the equity they donate, the company says roughly 20% of its shares will now be dedicated to the public good. It already committed $200 million to studying the labor-market effects of AI, and names that as the kind of problem the fund is built to scale. Anthropic is a public benefit corporation, so much of this work already lives inside the company — the fund is meant to give it a bigger, more flexible vehicle.

The structure is also the reason to read the announcement twice. A vehicle owned by the company is not an independent charity, and “adjustability between different forms of giving” is a two-way door: the same flexibility that lets the fund make bold distributions is flexibility the company still controls. Anthropic says it will publish the fund’s governance and the valuation milestones in the coming months — and on an IPO timeline reported as soon as November, those details will arrive under a spotlight. The test is not the pledge but the plumbing. Still, in a month defined by who captures AI’s upside, a lab moved a slice of its own cap table toward the public on purpose — and asked to be held to it. That is a different move from the usual promise to do good later.

Supply Chain · The Memory Is the Moat

NVIDIA Asks Its Memory Makers to Share One Fab

NVIDIA has told Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron to build the base dies for its custom HBM on TSMC’s 3-nanometre line — and Samsung, which has always built its own, agreed.
3nm TSMC node NVIDIA wants for HBM base dies

South Korea’s Seoul Economic Daily reported on October 18 that NVIDIA has asked its three memory suppliers — Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron — to make the logic base die for its custom high-bandwidth memory on TSMC’s 3-nanometre process, while the memory core die uses the sixth-generation 1c DRAM node. NVIDIA’s reasoning is consistency: if each supplier fabricates the base die on a different process, the resulting NVHBM can vary in performance and each variant has to be validated separately. Samsung reviewed the requirement and agreed to it, the paper said.

The detail that matters is what Samsung gave up. Samsung has historically run its memory business as a one-stop shop, making even the HBM base die inside its own foundry. Agreeing to route that silicon through TSMC is the first time it has extended its collaboration with the Taiwanese foundry beyond packaging compatibility — where the two have cooperated for years to make HBM play nicely with GPU packages — into the actual manufacturing of the memory chip’s logic layer. For its custom HBM supplied to other customers, such as AMD, Samsung keeps its in-house process.

NVHBM is the custom memory NVIDIA detailed in August, based on the seventh-generation HBM4E architecture, with NVIDIA itself defining the controller and interface spec. By standardising the base die on TSMC, NVIDIA is not just buying memory — it is specifying the process node its suppliers must use to make it. That concentrates more of the AI stack’s most contested layer into a single foundry and makes TSMC the arbiter of how uniformly the world’s HBM behaves under an NVIDIA accelerator. The report also noted Samsung is still evaluating whether to build NVHBM base dies in-house if TSMC capacity runs short. The memory stack has a brain now, and NVIDIA is choosing who fabs it.

Tools · The Web Becomes a Tool Call

DeepSeek Hands Its Search Engine to Every Agent

A day after shipping a frontier coding model, DeepSeek opened standalone Search and Fetch APIs — the web returned as JSON or Markdown — at $0.75 per 1,000 calls, free through Claude Code and Codex until November.
$0.75 per 1,000 Search or Fetch calls

Alongside its V4.1-Flash-1017 model update on October 17, DeepSeek opened two standalone web APIs to every developer. The Search endpoint finds pages for a query and returns structured results from DeepSeek’s own in-house engine; the Fetch endpoint takes a URL and returns the page as Markdown. Both are plain JSON, sit at api.deepseek.com under /v1/tools/search and /v1/tools/fetch, and use the same bearer key as the company’s models. Each costs $0.75 per 1,000 successful requests, billed only when a call actually returns a result, and is capped at 5 requests per second per account.

The practical effect is that a developer already using DeepSeek’s models can bolt live web access onto an agent with no second vendor, no second key, and no separate billing relationship. Results carry a rerank score and an authority tier that an agent can use to filter sources before it reads them. And there is a free window: server-side tool calls made through DeepSeek Harness, Claude Code or Codex against the DeepSeek API are not billed until November 1, after which they start counting too — a detail that will quietly add a line to some developers’ bills.

The strategic read is a vertical play. DeepSeek is now selling the model, the tool and the index from one place at commodity prices, assembling the “agent stack” that the big labs sell in pieces. It is also a hedge against the web that is closing itself off: as more sites block outside agents, the value of owning your own crawler and search engine rises. A cheap model plus your own index is a way to keep the web reachable without asking any gatekeeper for permission — which makes the search engine the real product here, and the model the loss-leader.

04 · SCIENCE
Safety · Safeguards Need Builders

NVIDIA Puts $250 Million Behind Teaching Agents to Behave

As Jensen Huang said he would cut chip supply to any buyer building unsafe AI, NVIDIA pledged $250 million in compute over five years to UK research on controlling autonomous agents.
$250M NVIDIA’s five-year UK safety pledge

NVIDIA announced a five-year compute donation valued at $250 million — about $50 million a year — to strengthen the United Kingdom’s research into agentic safety and security. Most of the commitment goes to the wider UK research community, with a portion reserved for the British Open-Ended Learning and Discovery Lab (BOLD) at Oxford, the Science of Fundamental AI Research Lab (SOFAIR) at UCL, and Imperial College London, whose share includes safety engineering and robotics. Access is delivered through UK-based NVIDIA Cloud Partners, with the infrastructure hosted in the UK, and the work targets secure agents, open-model security, secure sandboxes and safety-assessment benchmarks.

Days later, in an interview with Bloomberg’s Emily Chang in London, chief executive Jensen Huang went further than the money. Asked whether he would stop supplying chips to a developer building unsafe AI, he answered: “It’s a hypothetical, but yes” — adding that “before you do so, you would have a conversation,” and that “there’s a limit to the judgment we apply to other companies’ use of our technology.” At the same time he pushed back on the scale of the alarm, calling part of it “self-imposed,” saying the industry does a “horrible job” of explaining AI’s benefits, and, on a day when chip stocks led US declines, insisting demand will outrun supply “…but not today.”

Put together, the two moves sketch a position: the dominant supplier of AI accelerators casting itself as a gatekeeper of who gets to build what, while arguing the safety panic is overstated — and funding the safety research that would give its judgment some grounding. It is a real lever, because for frontier training there is largely one supplier to go to, and the companies that would build risky systems are exactly NVIDIA’s largest customers. Whether that is stewardship or a private company quietly holding a public veto is the open question of the moment. Safeguards, it turns out, need builders too — and right now they are being built by the company that sells the fuel.

Consolidation · The Team Outlives the Company

A Bankrupt Chatbot’s Staff Becomes Disney’s AI Team

After Character.AI filed for bankruptcy, a court filing showed Disney agreed to hire at least 60 of its people — most of the workforce — for around $60 million a year, with $7.5 million of a $20 million deal up front.
60 Character.AI staff Disney is hiring

Character.AI filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on October 12, listing estimated liabilities between $50 million and $100 million, with Disney as the stalking-horse bidder: $20 million for a non-exclusive perpetual licence to certain intellectual property, plus transition services from employees it had hired. The fuller picture came from a Delaware bankruptcy filing, spotted by Bloomberg and reported by TechCrunch on October 18: Disney agreed to make job offers to at least 60 employees — the majority of the workforce — at roughly $60 million a year in compensation, the “vast majority” of whom accepted. Disney pays $7.5 million of the $20 million up front, and the hires keep running the platform for up to five months.

The shape is a familiar one wearing new clothes. Character.AI built a large, engaged user base but was hobbled by high costs and what the filing called potential litigation and regulatory liabilities — including a lawsuit from the mother of a 14-year-old who said her son became obsessed with a chatbot before his death, and government investigations into child safety. Its former chief executive, Karandeep Anand, is already Disney’s first chief technology officer. This is an acquihire, and not the company’s first: Google reportedly paid more than $2 billion in 2024 to bring in much of Character’s talent, including its founders.

The lesson is about what survives a shutdown. The enterprise that failed was the chatbot business — the compute bills, the legal exposure, the licence to operate. What held its value was the team that knew how to build and run a chatbot at consumer scale, and Disney bought that directly, without the liability attached. In a field where companies are dying faster than the people who built them, the talent is the durable asset and the entity is often just the wrapper it came in. Character.AI’s technology may be wound down; its engineers now work for Mickey Mouse.

Microsoft Walks Away From a UK Data Centre Over Power

Company Watch of the Day
ANTHROPIC
Anthropic is the day’s centre of gravity, and it was pulling in two directions at once. It committed 5% of its shares — rising as the company grows, and about 20% of the cap table once co-founder and employee pledges are counted — to a new Public Benefit Fund, a deliberate move of ownership toward the public just as an IPO reported as soon as November comes into view. In the same window it absorbed Opaque, the confidential-computing company, to make its models not just safe but verifiably so for the enterprises and governments it is courting. Read against the rest of the issue, the through-line is who owns and controls the stack: Disney buys the people instead of the company; NVIDIA tells its memory makers which fab to use and whether it would cut off an unsafe customer; DeepSeek gives every agent its own search engine; and NVIDIA funds the safety research that would judge the gate it now holds. The frontier is consolidating — and the question of the day is no longer who builds the best model, but who ends up owning the layer underneath everyone’s.
