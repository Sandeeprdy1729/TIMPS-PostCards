Access · The Web Puts Up Walls

Banks and Airlines Start Shutting the Door on AI Agents

JPMorgan Chase and Charles Schwab now block outside AI agents from customer accounts, and Delta will not let them book tickets — a quiet reversal of the assumption that the web is open to anything that can browse it.
80M JPMorgan customers behind the wall

The Verge reported that a widening set of sites is closing itself to AI agents: JPMorgan Chase and Charles Schwab are blocking outside agents from customer accounts, Delta Air Lines will not let them book tickets, and users report agents such as Muse, Grok and Dots bouncing off retailers and directories including Yelp, eBay and Zillow. On JPMorgan’s third-quarter call, chief executive Jamie Dimon put the bank’s position plainly: “we’re not going to just allow any outside agent” — citing the risk of rogue agents acting at volume. The bank serves roughly 80 million consumer customers, and its wall now stands between them and any assistant that is not its own.

The technical reason is easy to see and the commercial reason is not. The web was built for two kinds of traffic: people who click ads and crawlers that respect a robots file. An agent looks like the second and behaves like the first, spending a company’s compute, moving its data and transacting in its systems without ever seeing its advertising. That makes agents simultaneously a security question, a liability question and a revenue question — and the cheapest answer to all three is to block first and negotiate later. The result is a default that has quietly flipped: a year ago, being reachable by an agent was the point; now it is something a company opts into.

What is being built instead is a permissions layer. Some labs and platforms are drafting open standards that let a site declare what an agent may and may not do, so that access is granted rather than assumed. That is a reasonable answer to a real problem, but it also moves the internet’s centre of gravity: the open, crawlable web is being replaced by a web of gates, and whichever standard defines the gate becomes the place the value accrues. The agents are ready. The doors are not.

02 · GEOPOLITICS
Policy · Fencing the Frontier

China Moves to Fence In Its Own AI Frontier

Beijing proposed adding 23 AI technologies — including the weights and source code of its biggest models — to its export-control list, treating its own frontier as a strategic asset rather than a global public good.
23 AI technologies in the draft list

China’s Ministry of Commerce published a draft that would add 40 technologies to its export-control catalogue, 23 of them AI-related. The proposal covers the things that define a frontier: large models above roughly 2 trillion parameters or near the top of public benchmarks, along with their weights and source code; chip designs at or below 14 nanometres or built with advanced 2.5D and 3D stacking; large compute clusters; and agent technology. Two carve-outs soften it — open-source and already-published research are exempt, and applications or APIs hosted abroad are still allowed, while simply running a covered model abroad counts as an export.

The politics run opposite to the past several years. Export controls have been a Western tool aimed at keeping advanced chips and models away from China; now Beijing is drafting rules to keep its own best work at home. The justification breaks from pure security language — the draft cites the need to “ensure AI remains under human control” — and the effect is to treat a country’s frontier labs as national champions whose outputs are a matter of state, not of who downloads the weights fastest. The draft takes effect for pre-registration from October 16, with public comment open until November 15, so the final line is not yet fixed.

The timing is what makes it interesting. In the same week, Western labs kept releasing open weights that anyone, in any country, can run — a reminder that the frontier is not a border you can quietly lock. A model already mirrored across the world is hard to recall; a rule that governs what leaves can only govern what leaves next. China is discovering the same lesson the West has spent two years learning: in machine learning, the weights do not respect the map.

03 · MODELS
Release · Open Weights Keep Coming

DeepSeek Ships a Frontier-Class Model Under an MIT Licence

V4.1-Flash-1017 posts 77.0 on the DeepSWE software benchmark — above Claude Opus 5’s 74.0 — with a million-token context window and a licence that imposes almost no conditions.
77.0 DeepSWE v1.1, vs Opus 5’s 74.0

DeepSeek released V4.1-Flash-1017 on Hugging Face under the permissive MIT licence, and the numbers are the point: 77.0 on the DeepSWE v1.1 software-engineering benchmark, ahead of Claude Opus 5 at 74.0, and 60.5 on Automation-Bench against 50.3. The model carries a million-token context window and adds OpenSearch plus Search-and-Fetch APIs, and DeepSeek describes it as a Pareto improvement over its own September Flash release — more capable and no more expensive to run.

The contrast with the day’s other story is the story. Hours after Beijing published a draft to fence in large Chinese models by size and benchmark rank, a Chinese lab put a frontier-class coding model on the internet with a licence that allows almost anything, weights attached. That is not a contradiction so much as a race: a state trying to define what a frontier is while its own labs race to give that frontier away. Size and benchmark thresholds are, in effect, a list of what is worth protecting — and DeepSeek publishing under MIT is a way of staying ahead of the fence.

For everyone outside the argument, the practical result is a familiar one. A model that scores at Opus-5 level on coding, runs under an unrestricted licence and takes a million tokens of context is now something a developer can download on a Sunday. The gap between the frontier and what is freely available has again narrowed — and the interesting question is no longer who has the best model, but what happens to the economics of the best model when a near-peer of it is free.

04 · SCIENCE
Discovery · Science the Machines Can Check

In Under 30 Minutes, a Machine Traces Five NIST Curve Seeds to 1990s Code

An Anthropic researcher used lattice reduction to trace the seeds behind five NIST elliptic curves to a 1990s C random-number generator keyed on Unix timestamps — a long-open question, answered on a laptop.
5 NIST curve seeds traced to one PRNG

Anthropic published research tracing the provenance of the seeds behind five NIST elliptic curves — P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 — whose origin has been an open sore since the standards were set in the late 1990s. Using LLL lattice reduction, the team recovered the seed for each curve and identified the generator that produced them: the lrand48/drand48 linear-congruential random-number generator from the C standard library, seeded with Unix timestamps between May 1997 and April 1998. The seed for P-256 turned up at the 1,842nd candidate; only the seed behind the binary field curve B-163 remains unexplained.

The value here is not that anything is broken. It is the opposite: recovering the seed makes the curves more auditable, because it shows the constants were generated in an ordinary, reproducible way rather than chosen to hide a trapdoor — the fear that has hung over NIST curves since the Dual_EC affair. Anthropic put a $36,864 bounty on the result, which was claimed and donated in full to ISRG, the organisation behind Let’s Encrypt, and the finding was independently reproduced by the cryptographer Filippo Valsorda. A result that a handful of specialists can verify, on public inputs, is exactly the kind of claim the field can bank.

What makes it a sign of the times is the shape of the work: a search-and-verify problem, spanning a large space of plausible inputs, where a machine can run the search and a human can check the one answer it returns. That is the template for AI-assisted mathematics and science — not a machine asserting a truth, but a machine narrowing a haystack until the needle is something a person can confirm without trusting the machine. A 25-year-old question settled in under half an hour is the marketing; verifiability is the substance.

05 · BUSINESS
Platforms · Defaults Are the New Lock-In

Replit Drops Anthropic and Makes ChatGPT the Default

The coding platform’s chief executive said it is no longer working with Anthropic, citing cost and performance, and is pointing its agent at OpenAI by default — a small switch that speaks to how model vendors are chosen now.
OpenAI Replit Agent’s new default model

Replit chief executive Amjad Masad said the company is no longer working with Anthropic and has made OpenAI the default for its coding agent, in comments reported by TechCrunch. He cited a mix of cost and performance — telling the interviewer he thinks “ChatGPT is better now” — while noting that users can still select a Claude model if they want one. Insiders have pushed back on the framing, and the change is a re-pointing of a default rather than a hard removal, but the direction is clear: a coding platform has decided which lab its agent reaches for first.

That is where the leverage sits in 2026. Model quality across the top labs is close enough that a platform rarely switches because one is clearly the best; it switches on price, latency, reliability and the terms of the partnership. Because most users never change a default, the default is the product — which turns a procurement decision inside a single company into a meaningful shift in market share. The era of developers hand-picking a model per task is giving way to platforms deciding for everyone at once, and the vendors know it.

The wider lesson is about lock-in, and where it now lives. A year ago the fear was vendor lock-in through proprietary weights; today the more consequential lock-in is at the layer above — the default a platform sets, the integration it bakes in, the model it quietly trains its users to expect. Replit replacing one lab with another is not a crisis for anyone, but it is a reminder that in a market of near-equals, the winner is often whoever owns the unexamined choice.

Company Watch of the Day
DEEPSEEK
DeepSeek is the day’s sharpest contradiction. China’s commerce ministry published a draft to fence in large models by size and benchmark rank — and in the same window DeepSeek put a frontier-class coding model on Hugging Face under the MIT licence, weights attached, scoring above Claude Opus 5 on the DeepSWE benchmark with a million-token context. That is not defiance so much as a strategy: a state drawing a line around what a frontier model is, and a lab racing to give that frontier away before the line hardens. It is also the through-line of the whole issue. Banks fence their systems against agents; Beijing fences its models; DeepSeek and Black Forest Labs hand capable models to anyone with a GPU; Anthropic opens up a 25-year-old cryptographic secret to prove it was generated honestly. Read together, the day was about who controls access — and the answer kept splitting between those building walls and those pulling them down.
