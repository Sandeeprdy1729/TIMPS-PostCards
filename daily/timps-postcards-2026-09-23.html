Semiconductors & Materials · The 12-Inch Moment

A Singapore Startup Builds the First 12-Inch 2D Semiconductor Platform — and Moore's Law Gets a New Room

Nexstrom closed a $12M seed to commercialize single-crystal two-dimensional semiconductors at full-size wafer scale, on production-ready tools — positioning itself for the industry's first 12-inch 2D growth as silicon nears its physical end.
12" First 2D Wafer-Scale Platform Target

Every few decades the chip industry bets that the next transistor will not be silicon. This week it got a 12-inch answer in the making. Singapore's Nexstrom raised a $12 million seed round to commercialize single-crystal two-dimensional semiconductors at full wafer scale, the company announced Tuesday — building the first platform designed to grow atomic-thin materials on the 12-inch tools fabs already run, and driving toward the industry's first 12-inch 2D wafer.

Two-dimensional materials — sheets just one or a few atoms thick — are the leading candidate to extend miniaturization beyond silicon's physical limits, where electrons leak through ever-thinner channels. The field has spent a decade proving the physics in centimeters and millimeters. Nexstrom's pitch is that it can go straight to 300mm: wafer-scale growth on production-ready manufacturing equipment, validated with industry partners inside existing chip workflows. That matters as much for economics as for physics: a 2D material that needs bespoke machinery cannot compete on cost; one that drops into existing fabs can.

The round was led by Xora Innovation, with Foothill Ventures and SEEDS (an arm of SG Growth Capital) joining — $15 million total counting a $3 million non-dilutive grant. The headline number is the schedule: Nexstrom's bet is that roadmaps like Intel's and TSMC's move to 2D materials in the late decade, and that its platform will be waiting on the loading dock when they do. Nothing 12-inch has shipped yet — the milestone is the platform and the wafer-scale path, not a finished product. But for an industry staring at "a hard ceiling to how much silicon can be shrunk," the clock suddenly has a 300mm face on it.

Semiconductors & Geopolitics · The Sanand Ramp

India's Semiconductor Line Enters Its Optimization Phase — a Gujarat Reporter Goes from 300 Million Units to Billions

The Renesas–CG Semi packaging venture at Sanand began commercial production in July and is now working to lift yields and throughput toward full rated capacity, with a second, far larger facility under construction. "Now we continue to optimise, like any factory," says Renesas's India president.
4B Units/Yr Target Once G2 Comes Online

India's chip bets are leaving the ribbon-cutting phase. The Renesas–CG Semi semiconductor packaging joint venture in Sanand, Gujarat, has entered its optimization phase — past initial commissioning, now lifting yields and throughput toward full rated capacity, a senior Renesas executive told the Financial Express this week.

G1, which began production in July, has a peak capacity of 300 million units a year, and the plant's economics turn on a structure that spreads both risk and reward: Renesas holds a minority stake — about 6.8% — alongside Thailand's Stars Microelectronics, with majority owner CG Power and Industrial Solutions overseeing construction. Renesas's contribution is technology transfer plus its role as an anchor customer, drawing on CG Semi's capacity for select chip lines.

The bigger news is what comes next. A second, substantially larger facility — G2 — is already under construction at the same site, designed to scale the venture to roughly 4 billion units a year, an order of magnitude beyond G1, and expected operational late next year or early 2028. CG Semi is investing more than Rs 7,600 crore over five years across G1 and G2; the offtake agreement is non-exclusive, so CG Semi can also serve other customers. That optionality is precisely the kind of volume anchor India's assembly-and-test ambitions have needed, and it sets up Renesas's next lane: packaging India-designed chips locally as the localisation push matures.

Open Science & AI · Papers That Run

Stanford's Paper2Agent Turns Research Papers Into Working AI Agents — 74 of 100, Automatically

A new Nature-published pipeline reads a paper, finds its code, and rebuilds it as an executable MCP server — turning static manuscripts into "virtual corresponding authors" a model can call by name.
74 of 100 Papers Agentified, No Cleanup

The scientific paper is 361 years old and still mostly a document. Stanford researchers have published a system in Nature that turns papers into software: Paper2Agent reads a manuscript, locates its repository, builds a working environment, and reconstitutes the method as an MCP server an AI assistant can call — a "virtual corresponding author" whose analyses can be rerun and re-asked.

Tested adversarially, it performs: on 100 randomly sampled bioRxiv computational-biology papers — deliberately unfiltered, including repositories with messy documentation — the pipeline agentified 74 fully automatically, producing 599 candidate tools of which 593 passed validation. The 74 agents scored 91.2% on 300 tutorial-derived questions, beating Claude Code given direct repository access (80.3%) — at a third of the cost and a third of the latency per query ($0.20 and 1.6 minutes vs. $0.38 and 4.3).

The 26 failures are their own finding: 35% were papers with no executable code at all, 31% missing data or model files, 23% environment and dependency failures, 12% scripts hardwired to one dataset. In other words, the system quantifies what most scientists already suspect — about a quarter of published computational research cannot be rerun even by an autonomous tool built to rerun it. Paper2Agent's quiet revolution isn't only making papers executable; it's giving the reproducibility crisis a scorecard.

Quantum Computing · The Live Decoder

IonQ Decodes Quantum Errors in Real Time — on a Single Off-the-Shelf CPU

The company says it has demonstrated the industry's first end-to-end, real-time quantum error-correction decoder running on one standard CPU — a compute bottleneck that has quietly capped every logical qubit so far.
408 Logical Qubits Decoded Real-Time in Tests

Quantum computers are noisy by design, and the machines that correct the noise have become a bottleneck of their own. IonQ said Tuesday it has demonstrated the industry's first end-to-end real-time quantum error-correction decoder running on a single, standard, off-the-shelf CPU — a step toward stripping the error-decoding problem out of the quantum system entirely.

Quantum error correction works by spreading a fragile logical qubit across many physical ones and constantly re-reading the syndrome — a wall of classical computation that historically demanded specialized hardware and kept up only with delay. IonQ says its decoder keeps pace in real time: in the accompanying arXiv research, the dual-decoder architecture ran benchmark circuits simulating up to 408 logical qubits across 88 memory blocks and magic factories — more than 31.5 million quantum operations at the "MegaQuOp" scale — while the decoding itself ran on a single commodity CPU.

The substance behind the milestone is that it moves classical work out of the cryostat. Offloading decoding to ordinary CPUs keeps the quantum stack from growing ever more bespoke — and, said IonQ quantum research lead and paper co-author Nicolas Delfosse, "successfully validating real-time decoding across hundreds of logical qubits and over millions of logical operations is an important milestone" that "provides a practical path to commercial-scale fault-tolerant quantum computing." Every serious path to fault-tolerant quantum computing — surface codes, LDPC, real-time noise tracking — terminates in faster decoding. Making it a software problem instead of a hardware one is how error correction becomes cheap enough to schedule.

05 · GEOPOLITICS
Planetary Science · The Margin Unit

Perseverance Finds Mars Wasn't One Lake — It Was Three Eras of Water, Recorded in 185 Rocks

A Purdue-led analysis of the Margin Unit's 185+ bedrock targets shows ancient groundwater, a paleolake, and late hot-water veins each reshaped the same rocks — and that the samples Perseverance sealed may hold all three habitats.
3 Distinct Water Episodes on Early Mars

The oldest question about Mars is not whether its water existed, but how many different waters it was. New research on Perseverance's Jezero crater findings shows the rocks around the crater rim were transformed by at least three separate watery environments — groundwater, a lake, then hydrothermal fluids — a sequence that reads like a geological diary.

Perseverance drove through the Margin Unit, an enigmatic band ringing Jezero's inner rim, and SuperCam's laser scanned more than 185 bedrock targets across ~870 feet (265 m) of elevation. Higher up, the rock was igneous olivine — barely touched by water. Lower down, it bore three generations of alteration: carbonate ridges left by CO2-rich groundwater, silicon-rich cement laid down when lake or groundwaters remobilized it, and late veins of calcium sulfate and fluorite, which on Earth form from hot (hydrothermal) fluids.

The sequence matters far beyond geology: carbonates are exactly the minerals orbiters made Jezero famous for, and on Earth they form in shallow, life-capable waters — so mission scientists knew these were among the rocks Perseverance was sent to drill and seal for the planned Mars Sample Return. Lead author Candice Bedford (Purdue) puts the twist plainly — the Margin Unit became "a crossroads for aqueous systems," not one calm shoreline. Instead of one habitat, the rocks record a groundwater-fed lake and a hydrothermal system layered on top. For astrobiology, early Mars is not one environment but a stack of them — and the samples en route to Earth matter a little more now.
