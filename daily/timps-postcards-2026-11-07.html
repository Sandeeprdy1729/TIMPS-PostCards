Security · The Huang File

Congress Wants Jensen Huang to Explain a Relationship U.S. Intelligence Calls a Spy Operation

Three House committee chairs have asked Nvidia’s CEO to brief them on Nov. 18 about Alice Huang, a Megaspeed executive U.S. intelligence concluded was recruited by China’s MSS. The company says she received ordinary business information. The timing — one day after earnings — is the tell.
MSS China’s Ministry of State Security, per U.S. intelligence

Three House Republican committee chairs — Brian Mast of Foreign Affairs, John Moolenaar of the Select Committee on China and Rick Crawford of Intelligence — have asked Jensen Huang to appear for a closed-door joint briefing on Nov. 18, the day after Nvidia reports third-quarter earnings. The letter, dated Wednesday and made public Thursday, says they want to discuss Huang’s interactions with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang and Nvidia’s “security protocols against foreign adversary espionage.”

The underlying reporting is stark. The New York Times reported that U.S. intelligence concluded Alice Huang, a Chinese citizen, was recruited by China’s Ministry of State Security and had “developed a close relationship” with Jensen Huang. The Financial Times reported that U.S. officials believe the relationship was romantic. The Times also reported that Donald Trump was briefed on the intelligence about a year ago and, according to people briefed on the surveillance, “chose not to do anything with the information.”

Nvidia’s response has been consistent and narrow. A spokesperson said regional cloud customers receive “ordinary business information — not sensitive NVIDIA trade secrets” and “zero information relevant to national security.” The company added that “meetings with customers are part of a CEO’s job; Jensen talks with thousands of customer executives every year,” and that if the U.S. government believed any person was a threat to American industry, it would let the company know. Megaspeed did not respond to a request for comment.

The national-security question and the corporate-governance question are not the same, and the letter blurs them. Congress is asking whether a foreign intelligence service got access to the most important semiconductor company on earth through a customer relationship. Nvidia is answering that customer meetings are routine. Both can be true. What makes the briefing consequential is the setting: a closed room, three committees, and a CEO who is also the industry’s most effective diplomat, arriving the day after he has to explain earnings to investors.

There is a wider pattern here. On the same day, Bloomberg reported that Nvidia is requiring Southeast Asian customers seeking Nvidia Cloud Partner status to enroll in a program that tracks the physical locations of their AI chips, part of the fight against smuggling. Export controls are turning into telemetry. The CEO’s customer relationships are turning into a congressional oversight matter. The personal and the systemic are collapsing into the same file, and the company now has to manage both with the same small group of people.

02 · CAPITAL
Markets · The $1.25 Trillion Queue

Anthropic Wants to List at $1.75 Trillion. OpenAI Just Closed at $1.25 Trillion.

OpenAI has closed roughly $11 billion of new funding at a $1.25 trillion valuation, with the round possibly reaching $20 billion. Anthropic still plans to list before Thanksgiving, targeting $80–100 billion at $1.25–1.75 trillion. The private market has picked a number. The public market gets to vote.
$11B closed by OpenAI in its latest funding round

Two numbers landed within hours of each other. Bloomberg reported that OpenAI has closed on roughly $11 billion of new funding at a $1.25 trillion valuation, and that the round could grow to as much as $20 billion in total. The Financial Times reported that Anthropic is continuing with plans to list before the Thanksgiving holiday this month, expecting to raise $80–100 billion at a valuation of $1.25–1.75 trillion.

Set those against Thursday. The Nasdaq 100 fell 6% and roughly $2.5 trillion came out of technology stocks, with the Philadelphia semiconductor index down 12%. Major investors had already told the Wall Street Journal they were skeptical that Anthropic could hold a $1.5 trillion valuation in an IPO, with one suggesting the number could land closer to $1 trillion. The new range does not lower the ambition. It widens it, and it puts the top end $250 billion above the mark those investors were already doubting.

The mechanics matter more than the headline. An $80–100 billion raise would be one of the largest listings in market history, and it has to be sold into a market that just decided AI capex carries a new political risk premium. Underwriters will need to build a book from sovereign funds, index funds and generalist institutions that are all now asking the same question: what does this company earn after it pays for compute? The answer is improving, but it is not yet a number anyone can underwrite with confidence.

The useful signal is the spread between the private mark and the public price. A successful listing at $1.25 trillion would validate the entire private AI complex and restart the growth-round treadmill at last year’s marks. A listing at the bottom of the range, or a delay, would do the opposite: every fund holding an AI company would have to mark it against a public comp that just came in below the private round. That is how a valuation story becomes a liquidity story.

For OpenAI, closing $11 billion at $1.25 trillion is a defensive win: it locks in capital before the public market gets a say. For Anthropic, the IPO is the say. Both companies are now racing to convert private confidence into public currency before the window that opened in 2024 closes. The next three weeks will tell us whether the market is repricing AI or merely repricing the cost of building it.

03 · INFRASTRUCTURE
Buildout · The Subtraction

Microsoft Is Quietly Unplugging the AI Capacity It Rented From Oracle

Microsoft has been turning off rented computing capacity from Oracle after declining to renew a 2023 deal, because the hardware ran older Nvidia chips. It is also offering buyouts to 60,000 employees. The buildout’s second act is subtraction.
60,000 Microsoft employees offered buyouts

Microsoft has been turning off rented computing capacity from Oracle after declining to renew a 2023 deal, according to Bloomberg. The reason is technical: the hardware in that arrangement used older Nvidia chips. On the same day, the New York Times reported that Microsoft is offering buyouts to 60,000 employees as its spending priorities shift toward AI.

Read together, the two stories describe the same management decision from different angles. The first generation of rented AI capacity is already obsolete on performance per watt, and power and cooling costs now dominate the economics of a data center. A chip that was scarce in 2023 is a liability in 2026 if it occupies a rack that could hold something denser. Microsoft is not exiting AI infrastructure. It is pruning the least efficient layer of it and the headcount built around the previous operating model.

This is what the top of a capex cycle looks like. Yesterday the market repriced the buildout by $2.5 trillion. Today the largest buyer is trimming the parts of it that no longer earn their cost of capital. Neither move is a retreat. Both are a reallocation toward newer accelerators, higher-density sites and fewer people per unit of revenue. The companies that sold the old capacity — Oracle in this case, and the neoclouds behind it — now face a smaller market for the previous generation.

The second-order effect is the one to watch. If Microsoft walks away from older GPU capacity, the marginal buyer for that capacity disappears, and the depreciation assumptions of every landlord who built around it get harder to defend. Oracle’s backlog and the neocloud contracts built on Microsoft-adjacent demand become the leading indicator. The AI buildout is not slowing because demand fell. It is slowing at the bottom because the good stuff got better, and the bad stuff got expensive to keep plugged in.

There is a human cost that the capex framing hides. Sixty thousand buyouts at a company long known for stability is not a rounding error; it is a signal to the labor market that the AI transition is now being funded partly out of existing payroll. The people who built the cloud are being asked to leave as the company builds the thing that replaces the cloud’s economics. That is the part of the cycle that does not show up in a chip index.

04 · SUPPLY CHAIN
Semiconductors · The Memory Squeeze

Nvidia Asked TSMC to Make Memory. TSMC Is Considering It.

With AI demand straining DRAM supply, TSMC is weighing 3D DRAM manufacturing at its biggest customer’s request. The bottleneck has moved from logic to memory — and the foundry that owns logic may end up owning the stack.
3D DRAM, the memory layer Nvidia wants TSMC to own

TSMC is considering expanding into manufacturing 3D DRAM at the request of Nvidia, its largest customer, according to The Information. The trigger is the memory shortage created by the AI boom. Nvidia can get all the logic wafers it can pay for; what it cannot reliably get is enough high-bandwidth memory to attach to them at a price that keeps system margins intact.

3D DRAM is a different way of building memory: instead of shrinking cells laterally, the layers are stacked vertically to raise density and bandwidth inside a smaller footprint. It is the memory industry’s answer to the same wall that pushed logic into 3D packaging. Today the technology is owned by Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron, with SK Hynix dominating the high-bandwidth memory that Nvidia’s accelerators depend on. Adding TSMC would create a fourth supplier with the most advanced process technology and the closest relationship to the customer setting the spec.

For Nvidia, the logic is obvious. TSMC already packages HBM into its CoWoS process, so bringing DRAM manufacturing inside the same foundry relationship shortens the supply chain and reduces dependence on three Korean and American memory makers who are simultaneously the bottleneck and the price-setter. For TSMC, it is a much bigger bet: 3D DRAM is a different process with different yields, different equipment and different competitors, and the capex required would be enormous relative to a mature logic node.

The pattern across this week is that the AI bottleneck keeps moving, and every move transfers leverage. First it was GPUs. Then advanced packaging. Then power and permits. Now it is memory. Each time the constraint moves, the company that owns the new bottleneck gains pricing power and the previous owner has to share it. If TSMC becomes a memory manufacturer, the most important supplier in AI becomes more important still, and the memory oligopoly gets a fourth player with the best process technology on earth.

Keep the caveat attached. “Considering” is not building. 3D DRAM yields are hard, qualification cycles are long, and Nvidia’s request is as much a signal of desperation about supply as a strategic blueprint. But requests like this are how supply chains re-form. The fact that Nvidia made it, and that TSMC is publicly weighing it, tells you where the next shortage will be fought.

05 · FRONTIER
Science · Proof by Machine

An AI Model Settled a Quantum Question That Had Been Open Since 2008

PSI says a system running a non-public Anthropic model proved the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture, and Lean verified every step. It is the clearest case yet of a machine producing new mathematics rather than retrieving old mathematics.
2008 the year the conjecture was posed

Physical Superintelligence (PSI) says a research system it built internally, running instances of a non-public Anthropic model on its own infrastructure, produced a proof of the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture — an open problem in quantum complexity theory since 2008. The system had a complete proof on the third day of the run, and Lean, the proof assistant that checks every step mechanically, accepted it. PSI then set the system on related problems; it improved the result twice and Lean accepted the final version.

The conjecture is about when quantum computers can be exponentially faster than ordinary ones. Scott Aaronson and Andris Ambainis asked whether a quantum computer can answer a yes-or-no question about a random string with exponentially fewer reads than a classical machine. They reduced that to a statement about bounded polynomials and their influence, and showed that if the statement held, exponential quantum speedup requires structure in the input. Proving it settles a central question about the limits of quantum advantage.

The result arrives with unusually strong corroboration. In August 2026, Guy Blanc, Jordan Docter, Carmen Strassle and Li-Yang Tan of Stanford had proved the conjecture’s prediction in a special case. They have since read PSI’s proof, sent comments and are writing a shorter exposition for experts. Their assessment: “This is a huge result. It resolves a decades-old conjecture with important implications for the nature of problems that admit quantum speedups.”

The important detail is the workflow, not the model. PSI used a non-public model, a scaffolded research system, a mechanical proof checker and named humans who are accountable for correctness: David Turturean led the work and reviewed the paper, Jim Halverson audited the proof, and Phiala Shanahan commented on the formalization. That is a template institutions can audit. The claim does not rest on a benchmark score or a demo; it rests on a proof that Lean will check and mathematicians can read.

The caveat is real but narrow. Because the model is non-public and the run was internal, no outside lab can replicate the generation process today. That matters for claims about capability. It matters much less for the theorem, which stands or falls on its own mathematics. The right conclusion is not that AI has replaced mathematicians. It is that a machine has now participated in producing a new, machine-checkable result at the frontier — and the institutions around mathematics have a working example of how to absorb that.

Company Watch of the Day
QUALCOMM
On a day when the biggest AI story was a spy investigation and the second was a $1.25 trillion valuation, the most quietly telling number was $548 million. That is what Google spent to buy around 3 million Qualcomm shares, according to The Information, as Qualcomm pushes into the AI data center market. The check is small against Google’s capex, but strategic stakes usually start small. Qualcomm’s mobile base gives it something Nvidia does not have: distribution into phones, PCs and edge devices where inference can run locally. If the next phase of AI splits between giant training clusters and on-device models, Qualcomm is one of the few companies with a real position in both. Google already designs its own Tensor chips and rents Nvidia capacity by the gigawatt; buying a stake in Qualcomm is a hedge against the possibility that the most valuable inference layer ends up in the user’s hand rather than the data center. Watch whether the stake grows, and whether Qualcomm converts it into design wins. A strategic investment is a conversation, not a contract.
