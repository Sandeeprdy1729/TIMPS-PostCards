01 · LEAD
Security · The Empty Office

U.S. Cleared Nvidia’s Best AI Chips for Chinese Firms With Empty Offices

A New York Times investigation found that some Chinese companies licensed by the Commerce Department to handle Nvidia’s H200 chips have vacant offices, no public records and owners tied to firms under U.S. sanctions. The paperwork was approved. In some cases, the company was not there.
600K H200s reportedly stranded in Hong Kong awaiting Chinese import approval

The New York Times reported Saturday that the Commerce Department has approved dozens of Chinese companies to handle Nvidia’s H200 AI chips, and that some of those companies exist mainly on paper. Reporters visited the listed Beijing office of New Matrix, which is cleared to assemble H200s, and found what the paper described as “a hollow gray shell with wires dangling from the ceiling where lights should be.” The company was created 17 months ago, does not appear in the building’s directory, and the property manager said there was no record of it. Its owners are tied to Wise Road Capital, a Chinese investment firm blacklisted by the U.S. since 2024.

Maginfra, another licensee, also appeared deserted, according to the Times. The server maker shares staff and patents with Inspur, the Chinese server giant placed on the U.S. Entity List for working with the Chinese military, and analysts told the paper they believed Maginfra was an Inspur offshoot. Two more licensees, Zhejiang Yindun Cloud Technology and Shanghai Jiatang, are tied through their owners to companies that serve the Chinese military. New Matrix, Maginfra, Yindun and Jiatang did not respond to the paper’s requests for comment. Neither Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security nor Nvidia commented publicly.

The chain of decisions that produced this is short. The Biden administration restricted Nvidia’s chip sales to China. The Trump administration agreed last December to let Nvidia sell the H200 to Chinese buyers after Jensen Huang lobbied hard against the limits, and Commerce attached conditions to the approvals. Reuters reported that ZTE Kangxun Telecom and Maginfra had been permitted to buy H200s, and in May that around ten Chinese firms, including Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and JD.com, had been cleared, though no chips had been delivered at the time.

An export license is a verification business, and the Times story is a verification failure. Approving an entity on paper is not the same as knowing who operates it, who owns it or whether it has employees. The empty office is the tell: the control assumes the licensee is a real counterparty with something to lose. If the U.S. cannot establish that a company has a desk and a payroll, the license is a signature rather than a barrier — and it leaves Nvidia in the position of vouching for customers it says it does not control.

The bottleneck may now be Beijing rather than Washington. DatacenterDynamics reported that almost 600,000 H200s are stranded in Hong Kong while the Chinese government stalls on import approvals, as Beijing pushes buyers toward domestic chips. That is the strange equilibrium of the current policy: Washington licenses sales that Beijing may not permit, to companies that in some cases cannot be found. The control is being fought over at both ends of the transaction, and neither end appears to be checking the middle.

02 · CAPITAL
Politics · The $110 Million Fight

A $63 Million Super PAC Beat a $100 Million Machine. Then Washington Changed Hands.

Public First, funded by Dario Amodei and other AI-safety advocates, put more than $63 million into the midterms. Ten of the 17 congressional candidates it backed won. Now Democrats are planning a flood of AI bills, and Elizabeth Warren is a top contender for the Senate Banking gavel.
10/17 candidates backed by Public First who won, per Bloomberg

Public First, a political group funded by Anthropic co-founder Dario Amodei and other AI-safety advocates, poured more than $63 million into the 2026 midterms to promote candidates aligned with stricter regulation, according to the group’s own numbers. Bloomberg reports that 10 of the 17 congressional candidates it backed won. Politico frames the result as a coalition that organized in Signal chats growing into a network that beat Leading the Future, a $100 million super PAC on the other side of the fight.

The asymmetry is the story. The smaller, newer, more concentrated side won the races it chose, in part because it was selling a message voters already wanted: that the technology is moving faster than the rules around it. The larger side was defending a position — less regulation — that polled worse than the checks it was writing. Money buys reach. It does not buy a popular argument.

What the win converts into is now being priced. Reuters reports that Democrats are planning a flood of AI bills once they take control of Congress. Bloomberg reports that Elizabeth Warren is a top contender to chair the Senate Banking Committee and is poised to step up oversight of Nvidia over chip exports and circular financing. The Washington Post reports that Democrats are eyeing Apple, Meta and Elon Musk-led companies as early investigation targets. Deutsche Bank told clients the new majority could slow AI investment by pacing it out, with “shaky” data center projects killed first.

The AI-safety movement has just discovered that electoral politics works, and the industry’s lobbying math has to change with it. For two years the argument was that safety advocacy was a research culture, not a donor network. That is no longer true: there is a proof of concept, a donor list and a set of committee chairs who now owe the coalition something. The more important shift is on the other side of the ledger. If a Democratic Congress can slow capex through hearings and permitting, then political risk becomes a line item in every AI business model — not a headline risk, a discount rate.

Keep the result in proportion. Ten of 17 is a good night, not a wave, and $63 million is small next to what the industry spends on lobbying and litigation. “AI safety” also covers a wide range of views, from evals and licensing to outright pauses, and the candidates who won did not all run on the same policy. But the coalition now has what it did not have on Tuesday morning: a demonstrated ability to move a narrow race, and a Congress that will be writing the rules it wants.

03 · INFRASTRUCTURE
Buildout · The Middleman Removal

Google Bought the Wyoming Data Center Crusoe Pitched. Then It Pledged $17 Billion.

Crusoe spent years selecting the site, winning permits and arranging power in southeast Wyoming. Google has now bought the site out from under it and will build the project itself. The neocloud middle layer just lost its most visible deal.
$17B Google’s planned investment in the Wyoming site

Google has bought out Crusoe’s data center site in southeast Wyoming, opting to take the project on itself after the AI computing upstart failed to lock in customers, according to Bloomberg. Crusoe said the deal ends its “engagement in pre-development work on the Wyoming project — which included data center site selection, governmental approvals, and power strategy.” Terms were not disclosed. Google said Thursday it would invest $17 billion on the site.

The backstory matters. Bloomberg reported in June that Crusoe had been pushed aside at the Wyoming project after Google raised concerns — a customer becoming the developer. What Crusoe had assembled was not concrete or servers. It was optionality: land, permits, a position in the interconnection queue and a power strategy in a state that wanted the project. In 2026 that is the scarce asset, and the party that can sign the biggest check can buy it.

The neocloud thesis was that specialists could build faster than hyperscalers and rent the capacity back. Wyoming shows the endgame of that model. When the customer is large enough, it takes the site and the specialist becomes a consultant. The value accrues to whoever holds the permits and the power, not the brand on the lease. That is a harder business than the one the neoclouds pitched to investors in 2024.

The political layer makes the real estate layer more valuable, not less. Cleanview estimates that roughly 40% of pending U.S. data center capacity is planned in states where newly elected governors have pledged to stop or pause construction. In Texas, public records appear to show SpaceX building a “Starbase supercomputer,” while governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has signaled she may slow some data center plans. Every project that clears a permitting hurdle becomes harder to replicate, and every operator without a site becomes a buyer of one.

Watch what Google does with the $17 billion rather than what Crusoe says about the exit. If the site is built as owned capacity, it improves Google’s cost per token and reduces its dependence on rented neocloud capacity. If it is built slowly, it becomes another example of a headline number that outran the grid. Either way, the lesson for the sector is the same: in the AI buildout, the intermediary is a temporary role.

04 · SUPPLY CHAIN
Semiconductors · The Domestic Stack

Huawei Built a Desktop PC With No Intel, No Qualcomm and Nothing Imported

Huawei’s Qinyun HM650 desktops run HarmonyOS on a Kirin X90 and, the company says, use 100% domestically made components. They are aimed at government and enterprise buyers — precisely the customers export controls were meant to constrain.
100% domestically made components, per Huawei’s claim

Huawei unveiled the Qinyun HM650 series of desktop PCs for enterprise and government users, running its HarmonyOS operating system on a Kirin X90 processor. The South China Morning Post reports that the company describes the machines as built from “100% domestically made” components — no Intel, no Qualcomm, no imported silicon at the center of the system.

This is the substitution stack arriving at the desktop, which is the last great stronghold of the Wintel duopoly. The SoC, the operating system, the memory and the board are being pulled inside China’s own supply chain, and the first customers are the ones with procurement mandates rather than performance benchmarks: ministries, state-owned enterprises and government offices that can be told what to buy.

The timing is not accidental. Washington’s H200 licensing process is stalled at the Chinese end, with hundreds of thousands of chips reportedly sitting in Hong Kong while Beijing slows import approvals and pushes buyers toward domestic parts. Shenzhen, the capital of the smuggled-chip trade, is clearing out its illicit markets ahead of the APEC summit. A domestic desktop is the visible end of a policy that has been building for four years.

The strategic logic of export controls is to keep a performance gap wide enough that the constrained side has to keep buying. Every fully domestic product narrows that gap in the one place that matters most for institutions: the general-purpose computer that governments and enterprises actually deploy. The Kirin X90 is unlikely to match the best Intel or AMD parts. It does not have to. Procurement mandates do not reward the fastest chip. They reward a supply chain that cannot be cut off.

The caveat is the software ecosystem. HarmonyOS on the desktop has to support the applications enterprises run, the developer tools their engineers use and the compatibility layers their vendors assume. That is a multi-year problem, and it is where most domestic PC efforts have stalled before. But the hardware milestone is real, and it arrives in the same week that Washington discovered its own licensing process was approving companies that may not exist. Both stories point at the same conclusion: the control regime is being stress-tested from both sides at once.

05 · FRONTIER
Science · The Complexity Break

OpenAI Says It Disproved the Exponential Time Hypothesis. Its Co-Author Calls the News “Shocking.”

A longstanding assumption held that some problems would always be hard for computers to solve quickly. OpenAI says it has a disproof, and Russell Impagliazzo, who helped formulate the hypothesis, told the Times the result is “shocking news” — with implications that reach chip design and encryption.
ETH the assumption that some problems are inherently exponential

The New York Times reported that OpenAI has produced a disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, a foundational assumption in computer science. Russell Impagliazzo, one of the researchers who formulated the hypothesis, called the result “shocking news,” according to the Times, and the paper notes that it could one day speed up chip design and break encryption. The Wall Street Journal put the stakes more bluntly: “Quantum Computers Are Poised to Crack Encryption. AI Could Beat Them to It.”

The hypothesis is one of the load-bearing walls of complexity theory. In its common form it says that 3-SAT — deciding whether a Boolean formula can be satisfied — cannot be solved in sub-exponential time. A large architecture of hardness results, lower bounds and cryptographic security arguments is built on that belief. A disproof means an algorithm exists that does better than the hypothesis permits, which is why the reaction from the hypothesis’s own co-author is the most important line in the coverage.

The implications are not abstract. Faster satisfiability changes verification, planning and hardware design, where SAT solvers are already used to check circuits and optimize layouts. It also changes the security analysis of anything that assumes a problem is too hard to solve. As Christina Ayiotis put it on Bluesky, the proof “could point to a future in which computers rapidly crack the encryption that guards the world’s secrets or design new medicines by optimizing the behavior of proteins in the body.”

The headline is not that AI solved mathematics. It is that a machine-assisted result has landed on one of computer science’s foundational assumptions, and the field now has to check it. Complexity-theory claims are graded by the community, not by a benchmark: the proof has to survive expert reading, and the algorithm has to be shown to do what the theorem says. If it holds, it matters more than any model release this year. If it does not, the correction will be just as instructive about how fast these claims now travel.

The caveats are real. “Disproof” can mean a proof that the hypothesis is false, a construction that evades it, or a result that holds only under assumptions the paper has not yet fully specified, and the practical consequences could take years to arrive. None of that changes the signal. The person who helped define the problem has looked at the answer and called it shocking. That is not a press release. It is a field being told to re-read its foundations.

Axon Puts $1.2 Billion Into Skydio at a $5.2 Billion Valuation
Axon is investing $1.2 billion in drone maker Skydio at a $5.2 billion pre-money valuation, taking a minority, non-voting stake and becoming its exclusive public safety distributor in the U.S. and Canada. The two companies seriously considered a full acquisition before deciding to stay independent, Skydio CEO Adam Bry said. The Treasury Cuts MEXC Off From the U.S. Financial System
The Treasury Department moved to sever crypto exchange MEXC from the U.S. financial system, alleging more than $16 billion in illicit flows, including Russian and Iranian sanctions evasion. The action, part of “Operation Economic Outcast,” was followed by roughly $126 million in outflows from the exchange. Tesla Will Wind Down the Cybertruck to Build More Cybercabs

The pickup was the most conspicuous product bet of Tesla’s past five years. Ending it is an admission that the robotaxi line now gets the factory, the capital and the attention.

Hundreds of Exposed Copies of a Chinese AI Hacking Tool Are Running in the Open

Security firms have linked Artex to attacks on South Korean banks and to the Microsoft cloud intrusion that let Chinese-linked hackers spy on thousands of organizations. Company Watch of the Day
SKYDIO
On a day when Washington was discovering that its chip licenses pointed at empty offices, the most concrete number in the news was $1.2 billion. That is what Axon is putting into Skydio at a $5.2 billion pre-money valuation, in a deal that makes Axon the exclusive public safety distributor for Skydio drones in the U.S. and Canada and leaves Skydio independent to keep selling to the military. Skydio’s numbers explain the price: more than 1,500 public safety customers, 28 million Americans living within two miles of one of its Drone as First Responder docks, every branch of the U.S. military and 32 allied nations as customers, and roughly $1 billion in expected bookings this year. Axon is buying distribution into a category where the buyer is a police department or a defense ministry, not a consumer. That is the part of the AI buildout that produces revenue today rather than a capacity contract for 2028. Watch whether the minority stake becomes a majority one, and whether the defense business stays outside the Axon exclusivity. A non-voting stake is a courtship, not a merger.
