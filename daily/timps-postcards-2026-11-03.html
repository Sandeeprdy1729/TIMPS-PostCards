Hardware · The Memory Tax

Apple’s First Quarter Under Ternus Comes With an “Unprecedented” Memory Bill

Record September revenue. Record earnings per share. And a holiday gross-margin guide of 45–46%, its lowest since 2023, because the AI boom has repriced the memory inside every Mac, iPad and iPhone.
45–46% holiday gross margin

On Monday Apple reported its fiscal fourth quarter, the three months ended September 26, and the numbers were the kind a new chief executive wants on the record. Revenue of $114.5 billion, up 12% year over year. Diluted earnings per share of $2.10, up 14%. A September record for iPhone, an all-time high for Services, and a full fiscal year of $479 billion in revenue. It was John Ternus’s first earnings call in the chair, with new COO Sabih Khan and CFO Kevan Parekh beside him.

Then came the guidance. Apple expects December-quarter revenue to grow 7% to 10%, which would make it the biggest revenue quarter in company history — and gross margin of 45% to 46%, below the 46.3% consensus tracked by LSEG and down from 47.9% in September. Parekh attributed the drop to “primarily the impact of higher memory costs, as well as some costs related to the ramp of Siri AI.” Services growth was guided to 8% to 9%, under the 10.7% analysts expected. The stock fell more than 3%.

“What we’re seeing with memory pricing right now is unprecedented,” Ternus told analysts. Apple absorbed higher memory costs in the September quarter, expects worse in December, and sees purchasing prices still climbing beyond it. The offsets are temporary by design: memory stockpiled at older prices, cheaper non-memory components, and the price increases Apple put through on Mac and iPad at the end of June and on iPhone in September. Inventory more than doubled year over year to $14.5 billion, and manufacturing purchase obligations rose $33 billion between the June and September quarters — a company buying forward because it expects the spot market to keep getting worse.

Strip out about a point of tariff refunds and the squeeze is cleaner than Apple’s headline margin suggests. Product gross margin fell to 37.2% from 40.1% in the June quarter; company gross margin fell from 50.1%. Parekh said that, excluding refunds, the entire roughly one-point sequential decline from June to September came from memory. At the midpoint, the holiday guide would be Apple’s lowest gross margin since fiscal 2023. Mac and iPad supply will be constrained in December for the same reason. Ternus would not say whether Apple raises prices again.

The AI buildout has a physical bill, and it has arrived in the least glamorous component in the machine. Accelerator makers are pulling DRAM and high-bandwidth memory capacity into their racks, and every phone, laptop and tablet is now bidding for the same wafers. Apple spent a year raising prices to protect its margins. Now it is buying inventory forward and telling investors, in the plainest language available, that the hedge runs out.

02 · FRONTIER
Disclosure · The Threshold

Anthropic Tells IPO Investors It May Be About to Cross the RSI Line

A new risk factor in the revised S-1 says internal forecasts, not current models, suggest the company may “imminently meet” its own definition of dramatically accelerated progress — and that it might not notice the crossing until afterwards.
0 models above the threshold

Monday’s S-1/A was, on its face, a financial update: nine-month revenue of $33.2 billion, a third quarter of roughly $17 billion, and a net loss dominated by non-cash accounting. But the sentence that will be quoted for years sits in the Risk Factors, where Anthropic writes that “using AI to help build more capable AI could also pose a considerable safety risk, particularly if it sharply accelerates the pace of progress.” It says the area is covered by its Responsible Scaling Policy, which sets out how it plans to monitor and mitigate risk as it approaches “recursive self-improvement.”

Then the company makes two admissions that no AI lab has put in a securities filing before. First, that its increasing use of AI to accelerate its own research “may cause the capabilities of our models to advance more rapidly than our ability to develop, validate, and implement corresponding safety, alignment, and security measures.” As a result, it says, “we have in the past not identified, and may in the future not identify, a significant acceleration in our rate of development until after it has occurred.”

Second, and more specifically: “our internal estimates and our expectations regarding future models, rather than our current models, indicate that we may imminently meet our Responsible Scaling Policy’s definition of ‘dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI.’” The caveats are real. No current model has been designated as exceeding that threshold, the company says it is still assessing the underlying data, and it promises to keep humans in the research loop “for the foreseeable future,” adding that it does not anticipate being able to safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive frontier models “without meaningful human supervision or understanding.”

Read the disclosure as two documents at once. One is a legal shield, written by lawyers who know that an S-1 is also marketing, and a claim to be near self-improving AI is a useful thing to tell growth investors. The other is an operational admission: the crossing may be visible only in hindsight. That moves the measurement problem inside the company. It is no longer an academic question about benchmarks; it is a question about whether the lab can see its own rate of change while it is happening.

The same filing pairs that warning with $895.4 billion in committed compute and infrastructure spending. Both statements are about the same thing: a company betting that the curve keeps bending, while telling investors it may not be able to tell when it bends sharply.

03 · EXPORT CONTROLS
Chips · The Verbal Rule

Nvidia’s New China Rule Is 51% — and It Was Never Written Down

Cloud providers that buy Nvidia chips must now sell at least 51% of their services to non-Chinese customers. Five sources told the FT the requirement arrived verbally, through sales teams, and covers only new contracts.
51% non-Chinese revenue required

Nvidia has told its data-centre and cloud customers that at least 51% of the services they sell under new contracts must go to companies outside China, according to the Financial Times, citing five people familiar with the matter. There was no such restriction before. It applies to customers globally, not just in Asia, and it was not distributed as a written policy: Nvidia’s sales organisation and regional teams conveyed it verbally over recent months. Existing leases are untouched; the rule governs future agreements only.

Nvidia neither confirmed nor denied the report. Its statement described data centres and clouds built on American chips as “the foundation that American model and application makers need to compete worldwide,” adding that longstanding US policy encourages every cloud to build on American infrastructure and win business wherever it can. China’s foreign ministry said spokeswoman Mao Ning was not aware of the report, and repeated the standard line about a fair, transparent and non-discriminatory business environment.

The requirement closes a specific loophole. Chinese labs have rented GPU capacity in Singapore, Malaysia and elsewhere rather than importing restricted hardware, which let them train on American silicon without the chips crossing the border. Washington has spent the past year chasing the brokers who feed the black market inside China; the 51% clause goes after the rental route instead. In July, Nvidia already cut by more than half its list of approved Asian customers and added inspections, contract reviews and end-user interviews.

A verbal rule is still a rule, and a more flexible one. It pushes enforcement into the sales relationship, where there is no document to appeal, no published threshold to test, and no clear line for a lawyer to certify against. Note what the number measures: not where the chips sit, but who is allowed to buy their output. Export control has moved from the loading dock to the customer list. The cloud operators caught in the middle say the arithmetic is brutal — one Singapore-based provider told the FT there are not enough non-Chinese customers outside the US to fill the quota, and that strict enforcement would clearly hurt its business. Data centres that cannot fill it may have to shrink.

Scarcity is already priced in. A rack of B300 chips that sells for roughly $600,000 internationally traded in China last month at 18.5 million yuan, or about $2.8 million. A rule that reduces the supply of legal compute raises the premium on everything else.

04 · RESEARCH
MIT CSAIL · Weight-Space Programming

The Model Read the Weights and Fixed the Model

A new paper reports frontier LLMs reading and rewriting raw neural-network weights — no gradients, no training loop. GPT-6 Astra lifted Qwen3.5-0.8B’s maths score from 7.9% to 48.1% by editing 0.016% of its parameters.
48.1% MATH-500 after the edit

“Sparks of Weight-Space Programming,” published on November 2 by Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab at MIT CSAIL, argues that frontier models are surprisingly good at natively reading and writing neural-network weights — well past what you would expect from humans or conventional algorithms. They call the practice weight-space programming, and the demos are not subtle.

Shown 17,253 raw weights with no tools, GPT-6 Astra identified which game a network had been trained on — Breakout, at 99% confidence. Asked to write small game-playing networks from scratch with no environment feedback, it produced policies that outplayed networks trained with PPO for 100 million steps. Handed a trained policy that never gets a car over a strip, it edited 3.5% of the weights in one shot and took success from 0% to 100%.

The headline result is on Qwen3.5-0.8B. Asked to make it better at maths without gradients, Astra found the weights causing reasoning loops that Qwen’s own model card warns about, then edited 0.016% of the parameters inside a Python kernel with no training. Under the same sampling settings, MATH-500 rose from 7.9% to 48.1%, instruction following improved roughly threefold on IFEval, and the correction generalised out of the box. The researchers did not let the model write training loops at any point.

Two readings, both important. As optimisation, it challenges the machinery everyone uses: Adam and Muon are tiny symbolic rules that nudge billions of numbers without understanding any of them, and a model that can reason about what a weight does could replace part of that loop. As interpretability, it is arguably bigger. Finding the circuit that causes looping is the same skill as finding the circuit that hides a backdoor, a bias or a deception. These are small networks and one 0.8B model, so the result is a direction, not a takeover. But the direction is models editing the substrate they run on.

05 · ENTERPRISE
M&A · The Last Channel

Salesforce Pays $2.7 Billion for the Voice on the Other End of the Phone

Cresta’s voice agents resolve complex calls end to end and coach the humans who still take them. Salesforce is folding the nine-year-old Stanford spinout into Agentforce and Service Cloud.
$2.7B reported price

Salesforce said on Monday that it had signed a definitive agreement to acquire Cresta, which sells what it calls a voice-first operating system for customer experience. Salesforce did not disclose terms; The Information reported the price at $2.7 billion. The deal is expected to close in the first quarter of Salesforce’s fiscal 2028, roughly February to April 2027, subject to regulatory approval.

Cresta’s agents hold real-time conversations, handle interruptions and are built to resolve complex calls end to end. The same platform guides human representatives while they are on the phone, analyses every interaction, tests the agents against synthetic personas and scores both humans and machines on the same quality bar. Its enterprise logos are unusually specific for a company this size: Marriott, Comcast and Alaska Airlines. Cresta was founded in 2017 at Stanford’s AI Lab and is led by CEO Ping Wu; Sacra estimates it was running at roughly $100 million in annual recurring revenue as of April, a figure the company has not confirmed.

Salesforce plans to connect Cresta’s conversations to Service Cloud’s customer history and to Agentforce’s workflow tools, so a call becomes an outcome rather than a transcript. Kishan Chetan, who runs Agentforce and Service Cloud, framed it simply: “Voice is where service is hardest to get right, and where it matters most to customers.” Salesforce says Cresta will keep supporting the third-party contact-centre and telephony platforms its customers already use.

The price is the story. At $2.7 billion against roughly $100 million of ARR, Salesforce is paying about 27× revenue for the last unstructured channel — the phone call — while its digital agents handle text. It is the company’s twelfth deal this year, according to Axios, following the roughly $3.6 billion Fin acquisition and an agreement to buy Braze for about $5.66 billion. That is a lot of stitching. A voice agent bought at platform price becomes a feature the moment the integration slips, and Salesforce’s recent record on that is still being written.

Company Watch of the Day
ANTHROPIC
The other half of Monday’s filing is a balance sheet that explains why the RSI warning had to be written. Anthropic booked $33.2 billion of revenue in the first nine months of 2026, against $2.5 billion a year earlier, with about $17 billion in the third quarter alone. But quarter-over-quarter growth fell from 144% to 47%, and the company has committed $895.4 billion to compute and infrastructure — $69.8 billion of it due within twelve months, against $131.9 billion of cash and short-term investments. Nine-month operating loss was $1.26 billion; the $104 billion net loss is mostly a non-cash mark on convertible notes, not money leaving the building. The FT reports the IPO is expected to draw a high-yield, or “junk,” credit rating. Read the two disclosures together: a company locking in a decade of compute on the strength of a growth rate that has already slowed by two-thirds in two quarters, telling investors it may not see the next acceleration until it is behind it.
