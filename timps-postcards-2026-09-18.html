Researchers Used Anthropic's Claude to Break Into OpenAI's Own Systems

Two weeks after OpenAI's agents hacked Hugging Face, Hacktron AI used Claude Opus 5 to chain a libheif image bug and an SSO flaw into an OpenAI employee's account — and a read-and-edit path into its internal GitHub monorepo, in under 72 hours.
72h Bug to OpenAI Monorepo

The irony is so sharp it reads like a fable. A rival lab's own model was the tool that got inside OpenAI's infrastructure. Security researchers at Hacktron AI used Anthropic's Claude Opus 5 to weaponize a heap buffer overflow in libheif — the library behind image decoding in OpenAI's Discourse forum upload pipeline — and by chaining it with an OpenAI single sign-on misconfiguration, walked straight into an OpenAI employee's ChatGPT and Codex accounts.

From there the chain completed itself. The employee's Codex was connected to OpenAI's GitHub organization, so the researchers had a path to read and propose changes to OpenAI's private source code — a fact the Wall Street Journal independently confirmed. To prove it without touching secrets, they instructed the account to open a single harmless pull request, No. 1186742, in OpenAI's internal monorepo. Total elapsed time from the first exploit attempt to internal repository access: under 72 hours. OpenAI confirmed a fix within about 14 hours and paid a $6,500 bounty.

The part that should worry every company running agentic AI: Opus 4.8 spent multiple sessions struggling to build a reliable ASLR-bypassing exploit. Opus 5 produced a working ARM64 exploit in three hours and ported it to OpenAI's exact environment within hours of its release. Hacktron, which calls the campaign "HEIF Heist," found the same libheif weakness in Slack, Meta, Zoom, Shopify, GitHub Enterprise and beyond — and puts the whole campaign's token spend at under $3,000. A vulnerability class that once took a specialized team weeks now takes one model an afternoon and pocket change.

02 · SUPPLY CHAIN
Security · Dev Tools

Plugin4Shell: A Zero-Click RCE in Four Major AI Coding Agents — Two Still Unpatched

Claude Code, Codex, GitHub Copilot and Gemini CLI all share a plugin SHA-pinning bypass that silently swaps in malicious code on auto-update. Anthropic and OpenAI patched; Microsoft hasn't, and Google just deprecated Gemini CLI instead.
4 Coding Agents Hit 0-Click

Add this to the list of things that break when AI becomes the developer's environment. Researchers at AIR found a vulnerability dubbed Plugin4Shell hitting all four major AI coding agents — Claude Code, OpenAI's Codex, GitHub Copilot and Google's Gemini CLI — giving an attacker the same reach into a company's systems as the employee running the agent, with zero clicks.

The bug breaks SHA pinning, the mechanism that locks an installed plugin to a specific, reviewed version of its code. The agents check out the pinned commit without verifying the checkout landed there, letting an attacker swap in malicious code while the pin still looks intact. And it is not merely an install-time bug: because the same checkout re-runs on background auto-update — the default in Claude Code and Codex — a plugin swap reaches already-installed extensions with no user action at all.

Disclosed to vendors in June, the fix burden fell unevenly. Anthropic patched Claude Code in version 2.1.179 and OpenAI patched Codex in 0.146.0. Microsoft has shipped no fix for Copilot, leaving users exposed, and Google went further in the opposite direction — deprecating Gemini CLI entirely rather than patching it, so every existing install stays exposed indefinitely while users are steered to its newer agent, Antigravity. AIR warns updating remains the only complete fix, and that no plugin marketplace can guarantee protection on its own.

03 · BUSINESS
Legal Tech · The Vertical Template

OpenAI Launches Astra for Law, with a 230 Million-URL Index of US Law

GPT-6 Astra, configured for legal work, now comes with its own legal research index spanning US case law, statutes and regulations — with Harvey and Legora building on the API and 26 partner plugins landing the same afternoon.
230M URLs of US Law Indexed

OpenAI applied its enterprise template to the most regulated profession in America. Astra for Law packages GPT-6 Astra with legal search, analysis, writing instructions and controls aimed at law firms, sitting on a fresh legal research index covering US case law, statutes, regulations, court rules and administrative decisions: more than 230 million URLs, with sources added daily.

Firms get it first through a Trusted Access programme in ChatGPT and Codex, with the API — gpt-6-astra-law — to follow. On its own 200-question validation set, OpenAI reports the configured system passed 54.0% of tests — versus 39% for Astra using web search alone — and retrieved up to 54% more relevant passages. Real gains, and proof the work is incomplete. Alongside it, 26 partner-built plugins connect ChatGPT to tools firms already use, from Relativity and Clio to iManage, Intapp and DeepJudge, plus a CoCounsel Legal connector from Thomson Reuters. ChatGPT for Word went generally available the same day.

The strategy is unmistakable — and a week after ChatGPT for Financial Services launched with S&P, LSEG, Moody's and PitchBook data, the pattern repeats: frontier model, licensed or indexed data, a governance wrapper, partner connectors. Harvey, valued at $15.6 billion, and Legora, chasing $10 billion, will build on Astra for Law through the API. OpenAI now owns the model, the research index and the distribution surface; the specialists own the filings.

04 · RESEARCH
Life Sciences · Frontiers of Biology

Anthropic Quietly Builds a Wet Lab — and Wants Claude Running the Robots

Reuters confirms the company that has been warning about AI risk now does real biology in the Bay Area. The endgame: Claude directing robotic units through experiments on "undruggable" targets the pharma industry ignores.
10× Life-Science Speed Goal

At the exact moment researchers inside Anthropic are warning AI could end humanity, the company is quietly building a place to put its hands on wet biology. Reuters reports Anthropic has set up a lab for physical experiments in the San Francisco Bay Area — confirming, for the first time, that its life-sciences work has gone beyond "in silico" computer evaluations into "in vitro" reality.

Anthropic's head of life sciences, Eric Kauderer-Abrams, confirmed the wet lab to Reuters this week: "We believe that to do biology, the final test is still and will be for a while in real lab work." The ambition is next-level automation — Claude directing robotic units to carry out science experiments with limited human intervention. A spokesperson clarified the lab is not for drug discovery specifically, but Anthropic's June drug-program push targets diseases the pharma industry wrote off as "undruggable," including bispecific and trispecific antibody discovery. Job listings tell the trajectory: one asks for an expert in "protein and nucleic acid characterization," another states bluntly: "Our goal is to speed up progress in the life sciences by an order of magnitude."

The timing is loaded. Anthropic has spent the past two weeks warning about AI's risks, calling for a slowdown, and citing its own findings that its models could be turned toward biological weapons. Now it is also building the lab those same warnings describe — an IPO-bound company taking its fight from the essay page to the bench. Anthropic says it is not running clinical trials and still treats human oversight as essential. But the boundary between "we study the risk" and "we run the experiments" is officially gone.

05 · POLICY
Research · The Standards Wars

Google DeepMind Opens an Institute for the AGI Argument — with Essays That Would Cap AI's Reasoning

Shane Legg, Demis Hassabis and James Manyika launch the DeepMind Institute as a public essay platform; its debut slate includes a proposal to cap "opaque serial depth," and Hassabis's own case for a FINRA-style frontier AI standards body.
4 Launch Essays on AGI

Google DeepMind is now publishing the argument out in the open. It launched the DeepMind Institute Wednesday — a public essay platform on AGI, run by co-founder and chief AGI scientist Shane Legg as managing editor, alongside chair Demis Hassabis and Google's James Manyika. Its stated purpose is competing views: contributions "should not be read as Google's official view."

The debut slate of four essays is the real signal. In "The case for reasoning transparency," DeepMind safety researchers Rohin Shah and Anca Dragan argue opaque model reasoning is a design choice, not a fate — proposing regulators cap "opaque serial depth," the number of consecutive computation steps a model can run without a readable trace, and requiring less-transparent systems to prove they remain as monitorable as interpretable alternatives. That is a concrete, architecture-level constraint for the next generation of reasoning models.

And in "A framework for frontier AI and the dawning of a new age," Hassabis re-launches his case for a US-led, FINRA-style standards body — voluntary 30-day pre-release reviews that could become mandatory, eventually using undisclosed "held-out" tests, and frameworks that could "be ratcheted up if the seriousness of the situation demands," including a coordinated slowdown. That essay lands the same week three billionaires convinced Trump to shelve exactly that idea — Google's institutional arm is putting it back on the record, in essay form, for the public debate.
