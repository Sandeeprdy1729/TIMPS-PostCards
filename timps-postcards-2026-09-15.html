Trump Calls the World's Top Chip CEO Onstage to Declare AI Fears a 'Hoax'

Mid-interview at the All-In Summit, Jensen Huang answered the president on speakerphone in front of thousands — and Trump turned the call into a live defense of the AI buildout: data centers are "the oil of the next 20-25 years," the safety panic is a hoax, and the only ones smiling are in China.
5 min Live Call, Onstage

"The robots will not be taking over. That's not going to happen." With a cheering Los Angeles crowd behind him, President Trump called Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang mid-interview Monday afternoon — and Huang, dressed in his now-signature alligator leather jacket, put the commander-in-chief on speakerphone in front of the All-In Summit audience.

Trump had spent the morning on Truth Social calling Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's weekend essay a "hoax" and a "scam," warning that concern about AI is "a SICK conspiracy" that only benefits China. On the call he kept the same frame: data centers are "great," they make people wealthy, and they're "the oil of the next 20, 25 years." He told Huang "I'm with you all the way" — after noting he "can't figure out how to put you on speaker."

Huang mostly declined to disagree, replying "You're right. We're not going to let that happen, sir." His fuller views that day were more careful than the president's: he called apocalyptic predictions "not grounded in science, obviously," credited Anthropic whistleblower Jacob Coxon with "great courage," and said companies should "pause or pace their work if they feel that their company is out of control." Interpreters noticed the gap — and that the CEO of the company selling the chips any slowdown would leave unsold has the clearest commercial interest in the room.

Trump also claimed the AI story is "bigger than the internet" and that opposing communities have been "playing right into the hands" of unnamed enemies, "could be political people, could also be China." Nvidia shares slipped a few percent anyway — after an AI-led run that has left the stock up about a third over the past year.

Siri AI Finally Ships — as a Beta, English Only, and Not in the EU

Apple's long-delayed assistant overhaul arrives inside iOS 27, macOS 27 "Golden Gate" and three other platforms: personal-context search, onscreen awareness and cross-app actions — wearing a beta label, daily usage caps and a two-continent blackout.
15 Eligible iPhone Models

Two years after it was teased at WWDC 2024 and delayed through a full product cycle, Siri AI began rolling out to the public Monday as part of iOS 27, iPadOS 27, macOS 27 "Golden Gate," watchOS 27 and visionOS 27. Powered by the next-generation Apple Foundation Model — trained with Google Gemini — it searches personal context across Messages, Mail and Photos, knows what's on screen, takes systemwide app actions, and holds genuinely conversational back-and-forth, with a new Siri app syncing history through iCloud.

But the launch is carefully fenced. Siri AI ships as a beta in English only, with French, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese and Spanish due in October — no specific day attached. It requires an Apple Intelligence device, and its most advanced on-device model runs on just six models (iPhone 18 Pro family, Duo, 17 Pro family and Air). Users under 13 are out, server-backed features carry daily usage limits, and Apple says expanded access will eventually be "available for a fee."

The biggest gaps are geographic. Apple is withholding Siri AI from iPhone, iPad and Apple Watch in the European Union, citing interoperability requirements it says would force it to give rival assistants equally deep system access; Mac and Vision Pro owners in the EU do get it. Mainland China gets nothing yet. Asked why the most consequential assistant update in a decade ships half-open, Apple's answer is deference to regulators — and regulators, notably, are exactly who President Trump spent Monday afternoon telling to stand down.

China · The Essay Wars

Beijing Bristles at Amodei's Essay — 'Fearmongering,' Says Its Foreign Ministry

China's answer to "We Must Pace the Frontier" arrived on three fronts in two days: the Foreign Ministry called it fearmongering, the Commerce Ministry dismissed the distillation accusations as groundless, and the intelligence chief warned AI now threatens China's own political security.
3 Chinese Bodies Push Back

"Fearmongering, confrontation and vicious competition will only disrupt the process of global AI governance," Foreign Ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun said Sunday, responding to Amodei's essay and its warning that "a Chinese lead in AI would pose grave danger for the United States and the world." Beijing's line: his alarms about China are counterproductive, and everyone should cooperate instead.

The Commerce Ministry went further, dismissing the FBI-NSA-CISA claim that Chinese firms ran "aggressive, malicious" distillation of US frontier models as groundless — retorting that distillation is common practice and that Washington is pursuing "a monopoly of the AI industry." It was the same week Beijing had no trouble lobbing the accusation itself at others.

In a Sept. 13 essay posted on social media, Chen Yixin, head of China's Ministry of State Security, warned that AI in the hands of "hostile forces" poses a direct threat to China's "political, institutional and ideological security" — and that foreign agencies are using AI to "aggressively harvest sensitive data, including critical national data, trade secrets, and citizens' personal privacy information." The symmetry matters: both capitals now frame the other's frontier AI as an offensive weapon, which is exactly the dynamic Amodei's essay asked democratic governments to engineer around, and exactly the backdrop Trump used to call the whole debate a hoax.

04 · MARKETS
AI Silicon · The Challenger Wave

Capital Swarms Nvidia's Moat: €200M for EUCLYD, $205M for Cornelis, $875M for Positron

In roughly 48 hours, three companies with three different routes around the Nvidia stack — a Dutch inference-silicon startup, an Intel-spun-out networking fabric and a no-HBM chipmaker — pulled in more than $1.3 billion of fresh money.
$1.3B+ Raised by Challengers in 48h

Eindhoven's EUCLYD signed a €200 million ($230M) Series A Monday co-led by Samsung, Somerset Capital Partners, the EQT-managed Scaleup Europe Fund and Innovation Industries — and, more striking than the cheque, former ASML CEO Peter Wennink joining as board chairman. Its pitch: "craftwerk," which it calls the world's first agentic AI silicon, plus a "lowest-power exascale" rack, aimed squarely at inference cost and energy rather than raw training scale.

Cornelis Networks, spun out of Intel in 2020, raised $205M led by IAG Capital Partners for "Active Compute Fabric," an architecture that puts programmable compute inside the network instead of using it merely to connect — targeting the enormous GPU time wasted waiting for data, and built on open standards (UALink, ESUN) rather than Nvidia's proprietary NVLink.

Positron AI, meanwhile, closed into an $875M Series C valuing it near $5 billion — up roughly fivefold since February — with NAVER Ventures among the investors, per reports Monday. Its bet: frontier inference without HBM, using widely available LPDDR5X memory (the "Atlas" system today, "Asimov" next). Together the trio says something about this market: as inference costs outpace training costs and America's top chipmaker stands onstage agreeing with a president, capital is deciding the moat isn't silicon — it's energy efficiency and open interconnects.

05 · INFRASTRUCTURE
Energy · The Grid Buildout

The Grid's $110 Billion Question: Moody's Says US AI Needs 45 GW of New Power

America's data-center boom needs $110 billion of new generation through 2030 — more than 30 gigawatts of it natural gas — according to a new Moody's Ratings analysis, landing the same day Trump compared data centers to oil.
45 GW Moody's Price Tag: $110B

The analysis, published Monday, puts a concrete price on the loop the president was standing inside: to power the coming data centers, the US needs to build 45 gigawatts of new generation through 2030. More than 30 GW of it is expected to come from gas-fired units — roughly 4 billion cubic feet of incremental supply a day — with solar and storage making up most of the rest and nuclear restarts below 5%.

BloombergNEF's longer outlook is starker still: US data centers on track to add 15 billion cubic feet per day of gas demand by 2035, more gas than every country on Earth except China, Russia, Iran and the US itself, with natural gas providing an estimated 69% of new grid-connected power. Grid operators are already feeling it — PJM is writing "ride-through" rules for large data centers after a ~4,000 MW Northern Virginia cluster tripped off in July, FERC is forcing its operators into a two-tier market of fast-but-interruptible versus slow-but-firm connections, Texas has paused new hookups, and 71% of Americans now say they oppose data centers near them.

The number worth holding onto is simpler than any of that. When Trump told Jensen Huang that data centers are "the oil of the next 20, 25 years," he was describing, with unusual accuracy, the physical commodity — the molecules, the megawatts and the grid contracts — that now decides whether the AI buildout Trump is campaigning on can actually happen.
