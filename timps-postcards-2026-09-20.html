Business & Law · The Slowdown Lawsuit

Subscribers Sue Four AI Labs for Agreeing to Slow Development — Calling the Pact a "Self-Serving Shortcut"

A proposed class action names Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google, arguing their coordination to pace AI development — starting in July — reduces the value of paid subscriptions. The lead counsel warns AI "could kill us all if" safety is "controlled by private self-serving agreements."
4 Frontier Labs in the Class Action

Four subscribers — one each to ChatGPT, Claude, Grok and Gemini — have filed a proposed class-action lawsuit alleging the developers of those models violated antitrust law when they agreed to slow AI development, per the AP. The plaintiffs argue the agreement would "reduce the value consumers get for paid AI subscriptions," and that the coordination began in July 2026, after leading labs signed a statement admitting there is "intense competitive pressure not to unilaterally slow" development.

The plaintiffs aren't objecting to slowing down — they object to how it's being done. They say Anthropic founder Dario Amodei's cooperation proposal is a "shortcut" that "substitutes collective restraint for individual accountability." Lead counsel Nick Rowley is blunt: "AI will quickly spin out of human control and could kill us all if we allow AI safety and protocol … to be controlled by private self-serving agreements between the world's most powerful 'for profit' technology companies."

The irony the courtroom will have to sit with: Amodei's own essay flagged the antitrust risk and hoped the government would make an exception. Altman answered on X that he welcomes "a federal framework that sets consistent safety requirements for frontier AI" but doesn't believe labs should "wait for an antitrust exemption." The Trump administration shot the idea down — the president called existential-risk claims "a HOAX." And China Daily dismissed the pact as "a club whose membership rules have been drafted before the guest list is announced."

02 · WORLD
Security & Geopolitics · The Chatbot at the Helm

A Hallucinated Intel Report "Almost Started a War" — Boarding Teams Were Ready for the Wrong Ship

CNN reports the US military prepared to intercept a Chinese vessel this spring after an AI-assisted report falsely claimed it carried nuclear program components. Armed personnel stood by boarding; planes were in the air.
2 AI Passes That Sealed One False Report

This spring, amid the war with Iran, an intelligence report "immediately set off alarm bells," CNN writes: a Chinese ship in the Middle East was transporting components of a nuclear weapons program. The US military swung into action, and per two sources armed members of the military were preparing to board the vessel — with military aircraft already in the air.

Then someone checked the source. The report had been assembled with the help of AI: an analyst at US Special Operations Command Pacific in Hawaii queried a chatbot about the ship's manifest, and the tool fused open-source intelligence with classified signals intelligence — then misidentified the cargo. The analyst used AI a second time to package the finding into a standard intelligence report, which circulated through the military in a format trusted by officials. The misidentification came to light only just before the planned operation.

One source described it as "entirely false"; another said it "almost started a war." SOCOM Pacific and the Pentagon did not respond to CNN's questions. The broader lesson lands hard amid an "AI-first" Pentagon: a hallucination can walk through the door wearing a uniform report's clothes, and nobody checks the name tag until the boarding ladder goes down.

03 · MEDIA
Law & Publishing · The Copyright Belly-Up

"Largest Theft of Labor in Human History": Unsealed Filings Expose How AI Execs Privately Talked About News

In the unsealed discovery of The New York Times' copyright fight, a Microsoft executive calls AI scraping "the largest theft of labor in human history," and OpenAI leadership privately brands ChatGPT an "existential threat" to publishers.
1 Microsoft Exec: "The Largest Theft of Labor in Human History"

The courtroom mask slipped this week. In legal filings newly made public in the New York Times' copyright suit, a Microsoft director called AI scraping "the largest theft of labor in human history," while OpenAI leadership reportedly branded ChatGPT an "existential threat" to publishers — words the newspaper's lawyers will surely read back to a jury.

The filings cap a week already hostile to the fair-use defense. Reuters and TechCrunch report that newly unredacted documents show executives at both companies privately described their AI products as substitutes for journalism — language that punches a hole in the argument that training on news is "transformative" or market-compatible. NYT attorneys wrote that the executives' own words amount to a concession that "the products are substitutes for the journalism they consume."

Why this matters now: the quotes arrive just as publishers watch every media business model bend toward AI distribution — and three days before the Grand Theft Auto-style stakes get a price check in the form of courtroom discovery becoming public record. The Times called the internal communications a roadmap of "knowing, systematic theft of decades of reporting."

AI · The Post-Chat Model

Meet Jev: A Card-Carrying ChatGPT Creator's New Model That Refuses to Chat

Diogo Almeida, who helped build ChatGPT and co-invented RLHF, has shipped Jev after two years of stealth — a "System One" model that answers typed questions with probabilities, in 70–500 ms, for fractions of a cent per decision.
$0.042 Per Million Input Tokens · Output Free

ChatGPT broke Diogo Almeida's heart, he told TechCrunch — so the former OpenAI researcher walked away and spent two years, and a $40 million DCVC-led seed round, building the opposite of a chatbot. His startup TypeSafe AI released Jev this week: a transformer-based model that outputs no text at all, only "calibrated decisions" — a yes/no, a choice from up to 255 options, or a score on a scale, each with a probability, all evaluated in one parallel pass.

The superpowers come from giving up language. End-to-end response time is 70–500 ms versus seconds-to-minutes for frontier LLMs; input tokens are metered by the billion, and outputs are free because there's nothing to meter. Because answers are constrained to predefined types, Jev cannot hallucinate — a feature LLMs structurally can't offer. TypeSafe's own workflow tests claim up to 193.6× faster and 444.6× cheaper on narrow decision tasks. Early hands-on results are modest but real: one test ran 777 judgments in under 0.7 seconds for about a quarter-cent; a smaller trial caught 6 of 7 planted writing flaws versus 7 of 7 for Fable 5.1, at ~25× the speed.

Developers are buying the thesis fast — Vercel called Jev the fastest-adopted model in AI Gateway history, with nearly 13% of paid teams on it within 24 hours, and Cloudflare and LangChain have integrated it. Almeida's argument: the industry got "very good at human language," but "computers speak a different language," and most intelligence should run quietly inside software — not in a chat window. After 2017's Transformer dropped sequential processing, Jev drops writing itself. The name is Jevons: every order-of-magnitude drop in the cost of intelligence unlocks orders of magnitude more use.

05 · GEOPOLITICS
Security & Trade · The Distillation Files

Anthropic Says Chinese Labs Distilled Claude at Industrial Scale — 151M Queries on Alibaba-Linked Accounts Alone

A September 10 threat report blames seven China-based outfits for campaigns targeting Claude — while NSA, CISA and FBI call "industrial-scale distillation" a national-security problem dating to at least late 2024.
151M Claude Exchanges via 3,500+ Alibaba-Linked Accounts (May–July)

The capabilities war has a new front line, and it isn't chips. Anthropic's threat report attributes more than 151 million Claude exchanges between May and July 2026 to Alibaba-linked operators using more than 3,500 fraudulent accounts — allegedly to support building Qwen. It alleges Moonshot AI generated 23+ million exchanges and routed nearly 300,000 real customer requests through Claude over ten days, and DeepSeek logged 12.1+ million exchanges in a 14-day window in July.

This is no longer just a corporate complaint. A September 8 advisory from NSA, CISA and the FBI describes industrial-scale distillation against US AI companies, names six Chinese firms, and dates the activity to at least late 2024 — adding the judgment that it was "likely conducted with Chinese government awareness," without asserting Beijing directed every operation.

The stakes go beyond IP. The alleged campaigns used fraudulent accounts, proxy networks and geographic-restriction evasion — and, in Moonshot's case, routed customer traffic carrying names, emails and corporate data through a foreign competitor's model unseen. Beijing called the report "unfounded in fact and law" and warned of countermeasures. It is the distillery behind the labels on the table at the Trump–Xi state dinner on September 24 — the exact tension Amodei's "pace the frontier" essay told Washington to confront.
